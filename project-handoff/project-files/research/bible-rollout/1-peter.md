# 1 Peter — book summary

## Short

1 Peter is a letter of hope for believers who are suffering for doing right. Peter writes to "the chosen ones who are living as foreigners in the Dispersion" — scattered across five regions — and tells them who they are: born again "to a living hope through the resurrection of Jesus Christ from the dead," redeemed by Christ’s precious blood, living stones built on the chosen cornerstone, "a chosen race, a royal priesthood, a holy nation." Because of that identity, they can be holy in a hostile world, live so honorably that slander falls silent, endure unjust suffering the way Christ endured his, and entrust their souls "to a faithful Creator" — humble under God’s mighty hand, casting every worry on him, standing firm against the devil until the chief Shepherd appears.

## Full

Peter addresses his readers with two words that frame the whole letter: chosen, and foreigners. They are "the chosen ones who are living as foreigners in the Dispersion in Pontus, Galatia, Cappadocia, Asia, and Bithynia" — precious to God, out of place in the world. The letter’s work is to hold those two truths together for people being insulted and slandered for their faith.

It opens with a doxology — a burst of praise: God "caused us to be born again to a living hope through the resurrection of Jesus Christ from the dead," to an inheritance "that doesn’t fade away, reserved in Heaven." Present trials are real — grief "in various trials" — but they refine faith the way fire refines gold, "more precious than gold that perishes." The prophets searched out this salvation in advance; angels long to look into it. So hope is to be set fully on coming grace, and life is to change now: "just as he who called you is holy, you yourselves also be holy in all of your behavior," for they were redeemed "not with corruptible things like silver or gold ... but with precious blood, as of a lamb without blemish or spot, the blood of Christ."

Chapter 2 gives the church its architecture and its charter. Coming to Christ, "a living stone, rejected indeed by men, but chosen by God, precious," believers themselves become "living stones ... built up as a spiritual house" — and Peter stacks up Israel’s old titles for them: "a chosen race, a royal priesthood, a holy nation, a people for God’s own possession," "called you out of darkness into his marvelous light." From that identity flows conduct among outsiders: as "foreigners and pilgrims," they abstain from warring lusts and live so well that accusers end up glorifying God. Peter then addresses the stations his readers actually occupied in Roman society — subjects under emperor and governors, household servants under masters good and wicked, wives (even wives of husbands who "don’t obey the Word"), and husbands. The letter’s instructions are submission "for the Lord’s sake," respect, and fearless good conduct; its summary is four clipped commands: "Honor all men. Love the brotherhood. Fear God. Honor the king." And beneath all of it stands the pattern: Christ, who "didn’t sin," who when cursed "didn’t curse back," who "bore our sins in his body on the tree" — "You were healed by his wounds."

The letter’s second half faces suffering directly. To suffer "for righteousness’ sake" is blessing, not shame; the answer to hostile questions is a ready, humble "reason concerning the hope that is in you"; and Christ himself "suffered for sins once, the righteous for the unrighteous, that he might bring you to God." One dense passage (3:19–21) says he "preached to the spirits in prison" from the days of Noah, when "eight souls, were saved through water" in the ship — our WEB translation’s word for the familiar "ark" — and that this water "is a symbol of baptism, which now saves you—not the putting away of the filth of the flesh, but the answer of a good conscience toward God—through the resurrection of Jesus Christ." Readers should not be astonished at "the fiery trial," but rejoice as "partakers of Christ’s sufferings": to suffer "for being a Christian" is no shame but a way to glorify God, entrusting one’s soul "to a faithful Creator."

The close turns to the community’s inner life: elders are to "shepherd the flock of God" willingly and by example — "When the chief Shepherd is revealed," they "will receive the crown of glory that doesn’t fade away"; all are to wear humility, "casting all your worries on him, because he cares for you"; and all must stay sober and watchful, for "Your adversary, the devil, walks around like a roaring lion" — to be withstood "steadfast in your faith." The last word is a prayer: "may the God of all grace, who called you to his eternal glory by Christ Jesus, after you have suffered a little while, perfect, establish, strengthen, and settle you."

# The five chapters

## 1 Peter 1
**Summary:** Peter greets "the chosen ones who are living as foreigners in the Dispersion" across five regions of Asia Minor, chosen by the Father, sanctified by the Spirit, sprinkled with Christ’s blood. He blesses God for new birth "to a living hope through the resurrection of Jesus Christ from the dead" and an unfading inheritance "reserved in Heaven" — a joy that holds even while "various trials" grieve them, because tested faith, like fire-tried gold, ends in "praise, glory, and honor at the revelation of Jesus Christ." This salvation is what the prophets searched for and angels desire to look into; the response it demands is sober hope and a changed life: "just as he who called you is holy, you yourselves also be holy," living in reverent fear as people redeemed "with precious blood, as of a lamb without blemish or spot." Born again "through the word of God, which lives and remains forever," they are to love one another fervently — for "All flesh is like grass," but "the Lord’s word endures forever."

## 1 Peter 2
**Summary:** Having tasted that the Lord is gracious, believers are to shed wickedness and deceit and, "as newborn babies, long for the pure spiritual milk" and grow. Coming to Christ — "a living stone, rejected indeed by men, but chosen by God, precious" — they are themselves built up "as a spiritual house, to be a holy priesthood," resting on the Scripture’s cornerstone: "For you who believe therefore is the honor," but to those who are disobedient he is "a stumbling stone." Peter then hands them Israel’s titles — "a chosen race, a royal priesthood, a holy nation, a people for God’s own possession" — once "not a people," now God’s people, called to proclaim him and to live among the nations so honorably that slander turns to glorifying God. That means submission "to every ordinance of man for the Lord’s sake" — king and governors — living as free people who are God’s bondservants; and servants are to be subject to their masters "not only to the good and gentle, but also to the wicked," because patiently enduring unjust suffering "is commendable with God," following in the steps of Christ, who when cursed "didn’t curse back," and who "bore our sins in his body on the tree" — "You were healed by his wounds."

## 1 Peter 3
**Summary:** Wives, Peter continues, are to "be in subjection to your own husbands," so that husbands who "don’t obey the Word" may be "won by the behavior of their wives without a word" — a beauty located not in braided hair or gold but in "the hidden person of the heart," with Sarah as the model; husbands in turn must live considerately with their wives, "giving honor to the woman as to the weaker vessel, as also being joint heirs of the grace of life," so that their prayers are not hindered. All believers are called to like-minded, tenderhearted blessing-for-insult conduct, backed by a quoted psalm’s promise that the Lord’s eyes are on the righteous; if suffering comes anyway "for righteousness’ sake," it is blessing, so "sanctify the Lord God in your hearts," and "Always be ready to give an answer to everyone who asks you a reason concerning the hope that is in you, with humility and fear." For "Christ also suffered for sins once, the righteous for the unrighteous, that he might bring you to God." In a dense closing passage he also "preached to the spirits in prison," those disobedient "in the days of Noah," when eight souls "were saved through water" in the ship. That water, Peter says, "is a symbol of baptism, which now saves you—not the putting away of the filth of the flesh, but the answer of a good conscience toward God—through the resurrection of Jesus Christ," who has gone into heaven with all powers subject to him.

## 1 Peter 4
**Summary:** Since Christ suffered in the flesh, believers arm themselves with the same mind, done with the old life of lusts, drunken binges, and idolatries — even when former companions "think it is strange" and speak evil of them, for "They will give account to him who is ready to judge the living and the dead." To that judgment Peter attaches one of the letter’s dense sayings: "For to this end the Good News was preached even to the dead, that they might be judged indeed as men in the flesh, but live as to God in the spirit" — like the spirits-in-prison passage of chapter 3, a disputed text (readings differ on who these dead are), reported here in the text’s own words. With "the end of all things" near, the community’s orders are sober prayer, earnest love — "love covers a multitude of sins" — hospitality without grumbling, and every gift employed "in serving one another, as good managers of the grace of God," so that "in all things God may be glorified through Jesus Christ." Then Peter turns to the "fiery trial": don’t be astonished by it, but rejoice as "partakers of Christ’s sufferings"; to be insulted "for the name of Christ" is blessing, and to suffer "for being a Christian" is no shame but a way to glorify God. Judgment begins "with the household of God," so let those who suffer according to God’s will, doing good, "entrust their souls to him, as to a faithful Creator."

**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings
**Sections:**
- **4:1–11 — Living for God’s Glory** (title: BSB) — Armed with Christ’s mind, believers are finished with the old Gentile excesses whatever former friends say — the mockers will give account, and "the Good News was preached even to the dead" (a disputed line, reported in the text’s own words). With the end near, they pray soberly, love earnestly, host gladly, and steward every gift so God is glorified through Jesus Christ.
- **4:12–19 — Suffering as Christians** (title: BSB) — The fiery trial is no strange thing: sharing Christ’s sufferings now means exceeding joy at his revelation; suffering as a Christian glorifies God, and as judgment begins at God’s own house, sufferers entrust their souls to a faithful Creator.

## 1 Peter 5
**Summary:** Peter exhorts the elders as "a fellow elder and a witness of the sufferings of Christ": "shepherd the flock of God which is among you" — willingly, not for dishonest gain, not "lording it over" the flock but as examples — and "When the chief Shepherd is revealed," they "will receive the crown of glory that doesn’t fade away." Younger believers must "be subject to the elder," and everyone is to be clothed with humility, for "God resists the proud, but gives grace to the humble" — so, "Humble yourselves therefore under the mighty hand of God, that he may exalt you in due time, casting all your worries on him, because he cares for you." One warning stands over the whole flock: be sober and watchful, for "Your adversary, the devil, walks around like a roaring lion, seeking whom he may devour" — withstand him "steadfast in your faith," knowing believers worldwide share the same sufferings. The letter closes in benediction — "may the God of all grace," after brief suffering, "perfect, establish, strengthen, and settle you" — with greetings from "She who is in Babylon" and from Mark, and peace "to all of you who are in Christ Jesus."
