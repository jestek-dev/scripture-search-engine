# Judges — book summary (draft)

## Short

Judges covers the rough centuries between Joshua's death and Israel's first kings, and it is the Bible's most unflinching portrait of a people coming apart. Israel never finishes taking the land, settles in among the Canaanites, and slides into a repeating cycle: they abandon the LORD for other gods, he hands them over to oppressors, they cry out, and he raises up a judge — a deliverer — who rescues them, until the judge dies and the slide resumes, worse each time. The deliverers themselves grow more flawed as the book goes on, from Othniel to Gideon to Jephthah to Samson, and the closing chapters descend into idolatry, atrocity, and civil war. The book's own refrain names the disease: "In those days there was no king in Israel. Everyone did that which was right in his own eyes" — and yet, every time Israel cries out, God in his mercy answers.

## Full

Judges opens where Joshua ends: "After the death of Joshua," Israel asks the LORD who should fight first — and the answer, "Judah shall go up," launches a chapter of victories that quietly curdles into a list of failures. Tribe after tribe "didn't drive out" the peoples of the land, choosing coexistence and forced labor over obedience. At Bochim the LORD's angel announces the consequence: the nations will remain "in your sides, and their gods will be a snare to you." Chapter 2 then steps back and states the pattern the whole book will trace — a generation arises that doesn't know the LORD; Israel serves the Baals; the LORD's anger hands them to plunderers; he is grieved by their groaning and raises up judges who save them; and at each judge's death the people turn back "and dealt more corruptly than their fathers."

The middle of the book is the gallery of deliverers, and God's habit of working through unlikely people runs right through it: Othniel; left-handed Ehud with his homemade sword; Shamgar with an ox goad; Deborah the prophetess and hesitant Barak, with the battle finished by Jael and a tent peg; Gideon, "the least in my father's house," threshing wheat in a wine press, whose army God deliberately cuts to three hundred "lest Israel brag against me"; Jephthah, an outcast son of a prostitute recalled to lead the people who threw him out; and Samson, a Nazirite from the womb whose one-man war against the Philistines is tangled up in his own appetites. Again and again the text says the LORD's Spirit came on these deliverers — and again and again it refuses to airbrush them. Gideon's ephod becomes a snare; Jephthah's rash vow costs him his only child; Samson ends blind at a Philistine festival, praying for strength "only this once."

The last five chapters abandon the cycle for something darker: two extended stories with no judge and no deliverance, bracketed by the refrain "there was no king in Israel." Micah builds a private shrine with a hired Levite and a silver idol, and the tribe of Dan steals the whole operation — priest, ephod, and gods — to found an idol sanctuary that outlasts generations. Then a Levite's concubine is abused to death by the men of Gibeah, and the outrage detonates a civil war that nearly erases the tribe of Benjamin, followed by two brutal schemes to find the survivors wives. The narrator reports it all without flinching and without endorsing: this is what "Everyone did that which was right in his own eyes" looks like at full scale.

Yet the book is not finally about human failure but about the God who keeps answering it. He is provoked and grieved by Israel's idolatry, but also "grieved for the misery of Israel"; he disciplines, tests, and every single time they cry out, he delivers. Judges closes leaning forward — a people this lost needs a king, and the books that follow will take up that search, with all its own ambiguities. The grace already visible here is that God's faithfulness, not Israel's, holds the story together.

# The twenty-one chapters

## Judges 1
**Summary:** With Joshua dead, Israel asks the LORD who should go up first against the Canaanites, and Judah — joined by Simeon — leads with real victories: Bezek, where the captured Adoni-Bezek — his thumbs and big toes cut off — admits "As I have done, so God has done to me"; Jerusalem, struck and burned; Hebron and Debir, where Othniel wins Caleb's daughter Achsah as wife. But the chapter's momentum stalls into a refrain of failure: Judah cannot take the valley "because they had chariots of iron," and tribe after tribe — Benjamin, Manasseh, Ephraim, Zebulun, Asher, Naphtali — "didn't drive out" the inhabitants of the land, settling instead for living among them and putting them to forced labor, while Dan is actually forced back into the hill country by the Amorites, whom only the hand of the house of Joseph reduces to forced labor. The incomplete conquest sets the stage for everything that goes wrong in the book.
**Tags:** `guidance` — the book opens with Israel seeking God's direction: "the children of Israel asked of the LORD... 'Who should go up for us first...?'" and the LORD answers, "Judah shall go up" (1:1–2); `presence-of-god` — the victories are traced to God's being with his people: "The LORD was with Judah" (1:19) and, of the house of Joseph at Bethel, "the LORD was with them" (1:22)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings

## Judges 2
**Summary:** The LORD's angel comes up from Gilgal to Bochim and confronts Israel: God kept his covenant and they broke theirs, so the remaining nations will stay, "and their gods will be a snare to you" — and the people weep. The narrative then steps back to Joshua's death and the generation after him "who didn't know the LORD," and lays out the pattern that will govern the whole book: Israel serves the Baals and the Ashtaroth, the LORD's anger hands them to plunderers, he is grieved by their groaning and raises up judges who save them — and at each judge's death they turn back and act more corruptly than their fathers. The chapter ends with God's verdict: he will drive out no more nations, but will use them to test whether Israel will keep his way.
**Tags:** `covenant` — the chapter turns on covenant kept and covenant broken: "I will never break my covenant with you... Because this nation transgressed my covenant" (2:1, 2:20); `sin` — the pattern named: Israel "did that which was evil in the LORD's sight," abandoned the LORD, served Baal and the Ashtaroth, and "dealt more corruptly than their fathers" (2:11–13, 19); `divine-judgment` — "The LORD's anger burned against Israel... He sold them into the hands of their enemies," his hand against them "as the LORD had sworn" (2:14–15, 20–21); `the-lords-discipline` — the handing-over is corrective, not final: oppression drives the people back, God grieves over their groaning and saves them, and the nations are left "that by them I may test Israel" (2:14–19, 22); `gods-faithfulness` — even amid the unfaithfulness, "The LORD raised up judges, who saved them," moved by grief for their groaning (2:16, 18); `testing` — God's stated purpose for the remaining nations: "that by them I may test Israel, to see if they will keep the LORD's way" (2:22); `backsliding` — the book's relapse cycle stated in full: judges raised up and Israel saved, yet at each judge's death the people turned back and "dealt more corruptly than their fathers" (2:11–19); `idolatry` — the cycle's root named and enacted: Israel abandons the LORD to serve the Baals and the Ashtaroth — the foretold snare, "and their gods will be a snare to you" (2:1–3, 11–13)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings
**Sections:**
- **2:1–5 — Israel Rebuked at Bochim** (title: BSB) — The LORD's angel indicts Israel for covenant-breaking and announces that the nations will remain as a snare; the people lift up their voices, weep, and sacrifice, naming the place Bochim.
- **2:6–9 — Joshua's Death and Burial** (title: BSB) — The narrative resets to Joshua dismissing the people; he dies at one hundred ten and is buried at Timnath Heres, having led a generation that served the LORD.
- **2:10–15 — Israel's Unfaithfulness** (title: BSB) — A new generation that doesn't know the LORD serves the Baals; his anger hands them to raiders and enemies, and they are very distressed.
- **2:16–23 — Judges Raised Up** (title: BSB) — God raises judges who save Israel, but the people won't listen and relapse worse after each judge dies; God resolves to leave the nations in place to test Israel.

## Judges 3
**Summary:** The nations left in the land — Philistines, Canaanites, Sidonians, Hivites — are named as God's test for a generation that never knew the wars of Canaan, and Israel promptly fails it, intermarrying and serving their gods. Then come the first three judges. When Israel serves Cushan Rishathaim of Mesopotamia eight years and cries out, God raises Othniel, on whom the LORD's Spirit comes; he wins, and the land rests forty years. Next, after eighteen years under Eglon of Moab, left-handed Ehud hides a homemade two-edged sword on his right thigh, kills the very fat king alone in his cool upper room — "I have a message from God to you" — escapes, and leads Israel to seize the Jordan fords and strike down about ten thousand Moabites; the land rests eighty years. A single verse adds Shamgar, who saves Israel by striking six hundred Philistines with an ox goad.
**Tags:** `testing` — the chapter's own frame for the remaining nations: "left, to test Israel by them... to know whether they would listen to the LORD's commandments" (3:1, 4); `sin` — Israel "did that which was evil in the LORD's sight, and forgot the LORD their God" — twice over (3:7, 12); `prayer` — each deliverance begins when "the children of Israel cried to the LORD," and he answers with a savior (3:9, 15); `divine-judgment` — God hands his people over for their evil: he "sold them into the hand of Cushan Rishathaim" and "strengthened Eglon the king of Moab against Israel" (3:8, 12); `deliverance` — two full rescue cycles: when Israel cries out, the LORD "raised up a savior" — Othniel, then Ehud — and Shamgar too saves Israel (3:9, 15, 31); `empowered-by-the-spirit` — the deliverer's power is God's own: the LORD's Spirit comes on Othniel, the savior the LORD "raised up," and he judges Israel and prevails (3:9–11); `gods-surprising-choice` — rescue through unlikely instruments: a left-handed man with a homemade sword hidden on his right thigh, and an ox goad in Shamgar's hand (3:15–16, 21, 31)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings
**Sections:**
- **3:1–6 — Nations Left to Test Israel** (title: BSB) — The remaining nations are listed as God's test and Israel's training in war; Israel intermarries with them and serves their gods.
- **3:7–11 — Othniel** (title: BSB) — Israel's first apostasy brings eight years under Cushan Rishathaim; Israel cries out, the LORD's Spirit comes on Othniel, and his victory buys forty years of rest.
- **3:12–30 — Ehud** (title: BSB) — Left-handed Ehud assassinates fat King Eglon of Moab with a hidden sword, rallies Israel at the Jordan fords, and Moab is subdued; the land rests eighty years.
- **3:31 — Shamgar** (title: BSB) — In one verse, Shamgar son of Anath strikes six hundred Philistines with an ox goad and also saves Israel.

## Judges 4
**Summary:** After Ehud's death Israel again does evil, and the LORD sells them to Jabin king of Canaan, whose general Sisera and nine hundred iron chariots crush them for twenty years. Deborah, a prophetess who judges Israel under her palm tree, summons Barak with the LORD's command to take ten thousand men to Mount Tabor; Barak will go only if she goes, so she does — warning that the LORD will sell Sisera "into a woman's hand." At her word — "Hasn't the LORD gone out before you?" — Barak charges, the LORD throws Sisera's army into confusion, and every man falls except Sisera, who flees on foot to the tent of Jael. She welcomes him, gives him milk, covers him, and drives a tent peg through his temples as he sleeps. So God subdues Jabin before Israel.
**Tags:** `divine-judgment` — Israel's evil is answered when "The LORD sold them into the hand of Jabin king of Canaan" (4:2); `prayer` — under twenty years of mighty oppression, "The children of Israel cried to the LORD" — and the deliverance follows (4:3); `gods-faithfulness` — the victory is the LORD keeping his word through Deborah: "this is the day in which the LORD has delivered Sisera into your hand," and "God subdued Jabin the king of Canaan before the children of Israel" (4:14, 23); `deliverance` — twenty years of mighty oppression ended by God the rescuer: "the LORD has delivered Sisera into your hand," the army thrown into confusion before Barak, and Jabin subdued (4:14–16, 23–24); `gods-surprising-choice` — the honor goes to unlikely hands, exactly as Deborah foretold: the LORD will sell Sisera "into a woman's hand" — and Jael with a tent peg finishes the battle (4:9, 17–21)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings

## Judges 5
**Summary:** Deborah and Barak sing the victory: praise to the LORD who marched from Seir while the earth trembled and mountains quaked, remembrance of the desperate years when highways were empty and Israel was disarmed, and gratitude for leaders and people who "offered themselves willingly." The song honors the tribes who came — Zebulun and Naphtali "jeopardized their lives to the death" — and needles those who stayed home by their sheepfolds and ships; Meroz is cursed for not coming to the LORD's help, while Jael is "blessed above women" for striking down Sisera. It closes over Sisera's mother waiting at the lattice for a son who will never come, and with a prayer: let all the LORD's enemies perish, "but let those who love him be as the sun when it rises in its strength." The land rests forty years.
**Tags:** `praise` — the song is Israel's praise for deliverance: "I, even I, will sing to the LORD. I will sing praise to the LORD, the God of Israel" (5:3, with 5:2, 9); `worship` — a whole chapter given to declaring God's acts to God: "there they will rehearse the LORD's righteous acts, the righteous acts of his rule in Israel" (5:11, with 5:2–3); `presence-of-god` — the LORD's march remembered in theophany: "The mountains quaked at the LORD's presence, even Sinai at the presence of the LORD, the God of Israel" (5:4–5)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings

## Judges 6
**Summary:** For seven years Midian's locust-like hordes strip Israel's harvests until the people hide in dens and caves and cry out — and God first sends a prophet to tell them why: "you have not listened to my voice." Then the LORD's angel sits under the oak at Ophrah and greets Gideon, threshing wheat in a wine press to hide it: "The LORD is with you, you mighty man of valor!" Gideon protests — where are all God's wondrous works? his family is the poorest in Manasseh, he the least in his father's house — but fire from the rock consumes his offering, and he builds an altar called "The LORD is Peace." That night, at God's command and in real fear, he tears down his father's Baal altar, cuts down the Asherah — a wooden idol-object — beside it, and offers a bull on a new altar to the LORD; his father Joash deflects the lynch mob — if Baal is a god, "let him contend for himself" — and names him Jerub-Baal. When Midian masses in the Jezreel valley the LORD's Spirit comes on Gideon and the tribes rally; still unsure, he asks God for the double sign of the fleece — dew on the fleece, then dew everywhere but the fleece — and God patiently grants both.
**Tags:** `presence-of-god` — the call rests entirely on God's presence: "The LORD is with you, you mighty man of valor!" and "Surely I will be with you" (6:12, 16); `fear-not` — to Gideon's terror at seeing the LORD's angel face to face: "Peace be to you! Don't be afraid. You shall not die" (6:23); `worship` — Gideon's offering consumed by fire from the rock, the altar he names "The LORD is Peace," and the new altar built where Baal's stood (6:18–24, 26–28); `obedience-to-the-word` — costly obedience despite fear: "Gideon took ten men of his servants, and did as the LORD had spoken to him," by night because he feared his household and the city (6:27); `sin` — the chapter opens with Israel's evil and God's indictment "you have not listened to my voice," and Baal's altar stands in Gideon's own father's yard (6:1, 10, 25); `doubt` — honest doubt met with patience, not rebuke: "if the LORD is with us, why then has all this happened to us?", "show me a sign that it is you who talk with me," and the twice-granted fleece — "God did so that night" (6:13, 17, 36–40); `gods-surprising-choice` — God calls the self-described least: "my family is the poorest in Manasseh, and I am the least" — a man threshing wheat in a wine press to hide it, greeted "The LORD is with you, you mighty man of valor!" (6:11–12, 15); `idolatry` — Baal's altar stands in Gideon's own father's yard and comes down at God's command, the Asherah cut down with it, while Joash's defense — if Baal is a god, "let him contend for himself" — exposes the idol's emptiness (6:25–32)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings
**Sections:**
- **6:1–10 — Midian Oppresses Israel** (title: BSB) — Seven years of Midianite devastation drive Israel into caves and to crying out; God's answer begins with a prophet naming their disobedience.
- **6:11–24 — The Call of Gideon** (title: BSB) — The LORD's angel calls fearful Gideon a mighty man of valor and promises "I will be with you"; a fire-consumed offering convinces him, and he builds the altar "The LORD is Peace."
- **6:25–32 — Gideon Destroys Baal's Altar** (title: BSB) — By night Gideon demolishes his father's Baal altar and Asherah and builds the LORD's altar; Joash turns the mob aside — let Baal contend for himself — and Gideon is named Jerub-Baal.
- **6:33–40 — The Sign of the Fleece** (title: BSB) — As Midian encamps in Jezreel, the LORD's Spirit comes on Gideon and the tribes muster; Gideon asks for the two fleece signs, and God grants both.

## Judges 7
**Summary:** God tells Gideon his army is too big, "lest Israel brag against me, saying, 'My own hand has saved me.'" Twenty-two thousand who are fearful go home, ten thousand remain, and God sifts those to just three hundred by watching how the men drink at the water. To steady Gideon, God sends him into the enemy camp by night, where he overhears a Midianite telling a dream of a barley cake flattening a tent, and his fellow interpreting it: "This is nothing other than the sword of Gideon... God has delivered Midian into his hand" — and Gideon worships on the spot. Armed only with trumpets, empty pitchers, and torches, the three hundred surround the camp, shatter the pitchers with the cry "The sword of the LORD and of Gideon!", and the LORD sets every Midianite's sword against his fellow. The fleeing army is run down, and Ephraim captures and kills the two princes, Oreb and Zeeb.
**Tags:** `dreams-and-visions` — the barley-cake dream and its interpretation, arranged by God as the sign that turns Gideon's fear to worship (7:9–15); `humble-exaltation` — God deliberately shrinks the army so no one can boast: "The people who are with you are too many for me... lest Israel brag against me, saying, 'My own hand has saved me.'" (7:2, 4–7); `trust-in-god` — three hundred men advance against a locust-swarm army carrying no weapons but trumpets, pitchers, and torches, on the strength of "the LORD has delivered the army of Midian into your hand" (7:15–21); `worship` — Gideon's response to the overheard dream: "when Gideon heard the telling of the dream and its interpretation... he worshiped" (7:15); `deliverance` — a rout God alone works: "God has delivered Midian into his hand," and the LORD sets every Midianite's sword against his fellow while the three hundred stand holding torches and trumpets (7:14–15, 22); `gods-surprising-choice` — the army deliberately cut from thousands to three hundred so the victory cannot be claimed: "lest Israel brag against me, saying, 'My own hand has saved me.'" ## Judges 8
**Summary:** Gideon talks down Ephraim's angry pride with a soft answer — "Isn't the gleaning of the grapes of Ephraim better than the vintage of Abiezer?" — then crosses the Jordan, "faint, yet pursuing," after the Midianite kings Zebah and Zalmunna. The towns of Succoth and Penuel refuse his hungry men bread; after routing the last of the enemy army — about fifteen thousand men — and capturing the kings, Gideon returns to punish Succoth's elders with desert thorns and to break down Penuel's tower, then personally kills Zebah and Zalmunna for slaughtering his brothers at Tabor. Israel asks him to found a dynasty, and he answers with the book's great line — "I will not rule over you... The LORD shall rule over you" — yet then collects the golden earrings of the plunder and makes an ephod — a priestly vestment — that "all Israel played the prostitute with," a snare to his own house. Gideon dies in good old age with seventy sons, and Israel promptly returns to the Baals, forgetting the LORD and showing no kindness to Gideon's family.
**Tags:** `harmony-with-others` — a quarrel that could have split the tribes is dissolved by a humble answer: "Then their anger was abated toward him when he had said that" (8:1–3); `sin` — the victory sours into idolatry and relapse: the ephod Israel "played the prostitute with... a snare to Gideon and to his house," and after his death Israel "didn't remember the LORD their God" (8:24–27, 33–35); `backsliding` — the relapse the cycle predicted: as soon as Gideon is dead Israel turns straight back to the Baals, "didn't remember the LORD their God," and shows no kindness to his house (8:33–35); `idolatry` — idolatry home-made from victory: Gideon's golden ephod, which all Israel "played the prostitute with... a snare to Gideon and to his house," and the return to the Baals at his death (8:24–27, 33)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings
**Sections:**
- **8:1–21 — Gideon Defeats Zebah and Zalmunna** (title: BSB) — Gideon pacifies Ephraim, is refused bread at Succoth and Penuel, captures the two kings of Midian, punishes the two towns, and executes the kings who killed his brothers.
- **8:22–27 — Gideon's Ephod** (title: BSB) — Gideon refuses the offered crown — "The LORD shall rule over you" — but makes a golden ephod from the plunder, which becomes a snare and an object of unfaithful devotion for all Israel.
- **8:28–35 — Gideon's Death and Israel's Relapse** (title: adapted from BSB) — Midian subdued, the land rests forty years; Gideon dies with seventy sons, and Israel turns straight back to the Baals, forgetting the LORD and Gideon's house alike.

## Judges 9
**Summary:** Abimelech, Gideon's son by a Shechemite concubine, persuades his mother's city to bankroll him with silver from the house of Baal Berith, hires "vain and reckless fellows," and murders his seventy brothers on one stone — only Jotham, the youngest, escapes. As Shechem crowns Abimelech, Jotham shouts a parable from Mount Gerizim: the worthy trees refuse kingship, and only the worthless bramble accepts — so if Shechem has dealt falsely with Gideon's house, "let fire come out from Abimelech and devour the men of Shechem," and the reverse. Three years later God sends an evil spirit between Abimelech and Shechem: the city backs the rebel Gaal, and Abimelech crushes the revolt, razes Shechem, sows it with salt, and burns about a thousand people in the tower's stronghold of the house of Elberith. At Thebez a woman drops an upper millstone on his skull, and he has his armor-bearer run him through so no one can say a woman killed him. The narrator draws the moral plainly: God repaid the wickedness of Abimelech and of Shechem, and Jotham's curse came home.
**Tags:** `divine-judgment` — the chapter's own verdict: "God sent an evil spirit between Abimelech and the men of Shechem... Thus God repaid the wickedness of Abimelech... and God repaid all the wickedness of the men of Shechem on their heads" (9:23–24, 56–57); `sin` — a kingdom founded on fratricide: seventy brothers killed "on one stone" with hired "vain and reckless fellows," blood the narrative never lets out of view (9:4–5, 24)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings
**Sections:**
- **9:1–6 — Abimelech's Conspiracy** (title: BSB) — Trading on kinship, Abimelech takes silver from the house of Baal Berith, hires thugs, murders his seventy brothers on one stone, and is made king by Shechem; only Jotham survives.
- **9:7–21 — Jotham's Parable** (title: BSB) — From Mount Gerizim Jotham tells the fable of the trees who crown the bramble, and calls down mutual fire between Abimelech and Shechem if they have dealt falsely — then flees.
- **9:22–49 — The Fall of Shechem** (title: BSB) — After three years God sends an evil spirit between king and city; Gaal's revolt gives Abimelech his opening, and he destroys Shechem, sows it with salt, and burns the tower's thousand in the stronghold of the house of Elberith.
- **9:50–57 — Abimelech's Punishment** (title: BSB) — At Thebez a woman's millstone breaks Abimelech's skull; his armor-bearer finishes him at his own request, and the narrator declares that God repaid both him and Shechem — Jotham's curse fulfilled.

## Judges 10
**Summary:** Two quiet judges hold the line — Tola of Issachar for twenty-three years, and Jair the Gileadite, with his thirty sons on thirty donkeys ruling thirty towns, for twenty-two. Then Israel's apostasy hits a new breadth: they serve the Baals, the Ashtaroth, and the gods of Syria, Sidon, Moab, Ammon, and Philistia, "and didn't serve him" — so the LORD sells them to the Philistines and Ammonites, who crush Gilead eighteen years and push across the Jordan. Israel's confession — "We have sinned!" — is first met with a bracing refusal: "Go and cry to the gods which you have chosen. Let them save you..." But when they put away the foreign gods and serve the LORD, "his soul was grieved for the misery of Israel," and as Ammon masses at Gilead, Israel's leaders begin looking for a man to lead the fight.

# Decisions record — drafting pass, 2026-08-23

Delegated judgment calls made in this draft, each reversible on Jesse's word.

1. **`doubt` (PR #43 concept) used on Judges 6 — reversible delegated default.** Per the project-wide rollout ruling, the taggable vocabulary is all 131 ids on main at commit b3f491d, PR #43's nine included, with any use of those nine recorded here. Judges 6 is `doubt`'s natural narrative home: Gideon's "why then has all this happened to us?" (6:13), his request for a sign (6:17), and the double fleece test (6:36–40) all depict doubt that God answers with patience. This is the draft's only use of a PR #43 id; Jesse can strike it and the chapter still carries five tags. *2026-08-25: ratified by Jesse 2026-08-25 — no longer reversible-pending-review.*

2. **Ten of twenty-one chapters subdivided** — a higher ratio than Genesis (14/50), because Judges routinely packs disjoint episodes into single chapters (minor-judge notices beside major narratives: 3, 10, 12; multi-movement epics: 2, 6, 8, 9, 11, 16, 20). Every boundary is a BSB heading position; several adjacent BSB sections were merged where their themes do not diverge (8:28–35; 9:22–49; 10:1–5; 16:4–22), with title attributions marked accordingly.

3. **`the-lords-discipline` added on chapter 2 alongside `divine-judgment`.** The chapter carries plain judgment language (anger, selling into enemies' hands), but the handing-over is explicitly corrective and bounded — God grieves at their groaning, saves them repeatedly, and leaves the nations "to test Israel" (2:18, 22) — which is the discipline concept's substance. Both tags kept; either can be dropped.

4. **Jephthah's vow — signposted, not adjudicated.** The summary states what the text states ("did with her according to his vow," her virginity stressed, the yearly commemoration) and marks the burnt-offering vs. lifelong-virginity question as a standing interpretive division, without deciding it. The narrator reports the vow; the summary does not present the vow as approved by God, and Brooks' typological reading of the daughter as a figure of Christ's sacrifice was deliberately not carried over (an interpretive frame well beyond the plain text).

5. **Judges 16 and sexual purity — `pastoral-sexual-purity` NOT tagged.** Genesis 39 carries that tag for Joseph's refusal; Judges 16 depicts the opposite — indulgence and collapse. Per the Genesis 3 worked example (a failure to resist does not earn `resisting-the-devil`), a chapter depicting the failure of purity does not get the purity tag. The chapter's fall-and-grace shape is carried instead by `sin` and the final-prayer `prayer` tag.

6. **`pastoral-relapse-and-restoration` on chapter 16 — considered, initially applied, then DROPPED (round-1 critic).** The concept's fall-and-grace shape (16:22 hair regrowing; 16:28–30 final prayer heard) is genuinely present, but the placement contradicts the pastoral packs' own safety design: `pipeline/fixtures/web-subset.json` pins Judges 16 as a pastoral **harm gate** — its selection row reads "pastoral harm gate: Samson pulls down the house (16:30)" —, and the pastoral pack headers state that suicide narratives must never rank for crisis queries — yet this tag's audience is exactly a person in crisis ("failed again," "fell back into sin"), and tags exist to eventually link chapters to real searches. Routing that searcher to a deliberate self-death narrative is the outcome the harm gate exists to prevent. The chapter keeps `prayer` and `sin`; the grace-after-failure insight is preserved in the motif list (narrative register, harm-gate caveat noted). Reversible only if Jesse overrules the harm-gate reading.

7. **Deborah presented positively, per the text.** Brooks (1919) glosses a woman in leadership as "a sure mark of apostasy in a nation." That is Brooks' editorial frame, not the text's: Judges presents Deborah as a prophetess judging Israel to whom Israel comes for judgment, speaking the LORD's command (4:4–6), and the song is hers. The Brooks frame was not carried over.

8. **Chapter 12 tagged `envy-and-jealousy` on a plain-reading basis.** The text does not name envy; it depicts a lethal grievance over being left out of war-honor (12:1), which both public-domain sources read as jealousy/pride (Brooks' key word: "Envy"; MHCC: "Pride was at the bottom of the quarrel"). Judged an honest presence of the concept's rivalry substance; reversible to "Tags: none."

9. **Chapter 21 grief — `pastoral-grief-and-loss` NOT tagged.** The chapter's weeping (21:2, 6, 15) is national remorse over a self-inflicted breach, not the bereavement the pastoral concept serves; per the Genesis 49 precedent (a griever should not land on a text that is not a grief text), the chapter carries `prayer` for its Bethel lament instead. Jephthah's daughter (ch. 11) keeps the grief tag — there the mourning is personal, explicit, and extended.

10. **`pastoral-god-sees-my-suffering` on chapter 10 — DROPPED (round-1 critic).** The moment itself — "his soul was grieved for the misery of Israel" (10:16) — is real and stays in the summary, but the concept's register is personal crisis — its lexicon is first-person-singular ("God sees my suffering," "does God see me"), its anchors an individual met in her misery — and the project-wide register ruling bars tagging the fourteen `pastoral-*` packs on national-scale material. Judges 10:16 is corporate Israel inside a covenant-lawsuit exchange. The oppression-and-mercy theme is routed through the motif list and the `deliverance` tag-gap candidate instead. Reversible on Jesse's word.

11. **Judges 19's summary quotes the WEB's own hard phrases** ("played the prostitute against him," the mob's demand, "abused her all night") rather than euphemizing or elaborating; the atrocity is reported at the text's own level of explicitness, with the narrator's frame ("no king in Israel") doing the interpreting, as it does in the book.

12. **`salvation` deliberately not used for the judges' deliverances.** The WEB says the LORD "raised up a savior" (3:9, 15), but the vocabulary's `salvation` concept is keyed to gospel-salvation queries ("how can i be saved," "salvation in christ"); routing those to military deliverances would mislead. The gap this leaves is logged below as a `deliverance` candidate.
