## Short

Jonah is the odd book out among the prophets: instead of collecting a prophet's sermons, it tells a story about the prophet himself — and the prophet is the problem. Told to go to Nineveh, "that great city," and preach against its wickedness, Jonah boards a ship headed the other way, "to flee to Tarshish from the presence of the LORD" (1:3). A God-sent storm, a crew of frightened pagan sailors, and a huge fish turn him around; from the fish's belly he prays a psalm that ends "Salvation belongs to the LORD" (2:9). Given the word a second time, he preaches — and Nineveh repents from the king down, and God relents of the disaster. That mercy is exactly what Jonah cannot bear: he rages that he knew from the start that God was "a gracious God and merciful, slow to anger, and abundant in loving kindness" (4:2), and asks to die. God answers with a fast-growing vine, a worm, a scorching wind, and one final question — shouldn't God be concerned for a city of more than a hundred twenty thousand people? — on which the book deliberately ends, unanswered.

## Full

Jonah is a narrative, not an anthology. The other books of the Twelve preserve what their prophets said; Jonah preserves what its prophet did — mostly wrong — and records exactly one line of his preaching, seven words in our translation: "In forty days, Nineveh will be overthrown!" (3:4). Its hero is "Jonah the son of Amittai" (1:1), the same name the Old Testament gives a prophet from Gath Hepher who spoke for God in the days of Israel's king Jeroboam II (2 Kings 14:25). Nineveh, his assigned destination, was a leading city of Assyria — the empire that menaced Israel; the book itself never pauses to explain this, but it is the background its first hearers would have supplied, and chapter 4 shows Jonah had his reasons for not wanting Nineveh spared.

The story moves in two matched halves. In the first, the LORD's word says "Arise, go to Nineveh" (1:2), and Jonah arises — to flee in the opposite direction, paying his fare on a ship for Tarshish, a far-off port. The LORD sends out a great wind; the sailors panic, each crying to his own god and throwing the cargo overboard, while the runaway prophet sleeps in the ship's hold. The sailors cast lots, and the lot falls on Jonah; he confesses that he fears "the LORD, the God of heaven, who has made the sea and the dry land" (1:9) — the very sea he is fleeing across. At his own word the sailors throw him in, after first rowing hard to spare him and begging the LORD not to hold his blood against them; the sea stops raging, and the pagan crew ends the chapter fearing the LORD, offering sacrifice, and making vows. The LORD prepares "a huge fish" to swallow Jonah — three days and three nights in its belly (1:17) — and from inside it Jonah prays a psalm of thanksgiving for a rescue already under way: cast into the deep, weeds wrapped around his head, he remembered the LORD, and the prayer crests on the book's central confession, "Salvation belongs to the LORD" (2:9). The fish vomits him out on dry land.

In the second half the word comes "the second time" (3:1), and this time Jonah goes "according to the LORD's word" (3:3). His one-sentence warning lands with immediate and total effect: "The people of Nineveh believed God" (3:5). A fast is proclaimed from the greatest to the least; the king rises from his throne, trades his royal robe for sackcloth and ashes, and decrees that man and animal alike must fast, be covered with sackcloth, and "cry mightily to God", everyone turning "from his evil way and from the violence that is in his hands" (3:8) — all on a bare hope: "Who knows whether God will not turn and relent...?" (3:9). God sees their works, that they turned from their evil way, and relents of the disaster.

Chapter 4 is the book's real destination. Nineveh's reprieve "displeased Jonah exceedingly" (4:1), and his angry prayer finally explains chapter 1: he fled precisely because he knew God's character — "I knew that you are a gracious God and merciful, slow to anger, and abundant in loving kindness, and you relent of doing harm" (4:2). He asks to die, and God asks a question instead: "Is it right for you to be angry?" (4:4). Then comes the object lesson: God prepares a vine that comes up over Jonah as a shade over his head (he is sitting in a booth — a makeshift shelter — east of the city), and Jonah is exceedingly glad; God prepares a worm at dawn, and the vine withers; God prepares a sultry east wind, and Jonah begs again to die. The prophet who felt nothing for a city full of people grieved to death over a plant he neither labored over nor made grow, one that "came up in a night and perished in a night" (4:10). The book gives God the last word, and it is a question: "Shouldn't I be concerned for Nineveh, that great city, in which are more than one hundred twenty thousand persons who can't discern between their right hand and their left hand, and also many animals?" (4:11). No answer from Jonah is recorded; the question is left standing for the reader.

The book's exposure of its prophet is pointed but never cruel — Jonah is God's own servant, corrected with a storm, rescued by a fish, shaded by a vine, and reasoned with to the end. Later readers have heard more in the fish's three days and nights: Jesus points to the sign of Jonah — the prophet's three days and three nights in the belly of the fish — as a picture of his own burial and rising (Matthew 12:39–40), an interpretive frame the New Testament supplies. The book's own horizon is the question it closes on: whether God's compassion may reach a great, wicked, foreign city, and what is wrong with the heart that hopes it won't.

## Jonah 1
**Summary:** The LORD tells Jonah to go to Nineveh, "that great city," and preach against it, "for their wickedness has come up before me" (1:2) — and Jonah instead boards a ship at Joppa "to flee to Tarshish from the presence of the LORD" (1:3). The LORD sends out a great wind, and while the sailors cry each to his own god and jettison the cargo, Jonah sleeps in the ship's hold until the shipmaster rouses him: "Arise, call on your God!" (1:6). The lot falls on Jonah; he admits he is a Hebrew fleeing "the LORD, the God of heaven, who has made the sea and the dry land" (1:9), and tells the crew to throw him into the sea. The men row hard for land first, then beg the LORD not to charge them with innocent blood, throw him overboard — and the sea ceases its raging, leaving the pagan crew fearing the LORD, offering a sacrifice, and making vows. The LORD prepares "a huge fish" to swallow Jonah, and he is in the belly of the fish three days and three nights (1:17).
**Tags:** `providence` — God commands sea, storm, lot, and creature to arrest his runaway prophet: "the LORD sent out a great wind on the sea" (1:4), "the lot fell on Jonah" (1:7), the sailors confess "you, LORD, have done as it pleased you" (1:14), and "The LORD prepared a huge fish" (1:17); `the-lords-discipline` — the storm is corrective, aimed at God's own servant and owned by him — "I know that because of me this great storm is on you" (1:12) — and it ends not in the prophet's destruction but in his preservation inside the fish God prepared (1:15, 17); `running-from-god` — Scripture's defining flight-from-the-call narrative: Jonah boards a ship "to flee to Tarshish from the presence of the LORD" (1:3), then must admit he is fleeing "the LORD, the God of heaven, who has made the sea and the dry land" (1:9–10); `gods-compassion-for-outsiders` — the pagan crew's plea is heard: "you, LORD, have done as it pleased you" (1:14), the sea ceases its raging, and the sailors are left fearing the LORD, offering a sacrifice, and making vows (1:14–16)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings

## Jonah 2
**Summary:** From inside the fish Jonah prays — a psalm that looks back on a rescue already granted: "I called because of my affliction to the LORD. He answered me. Out of the belly of Sheol I cried" (2:2) — Sheol being the Hebrew name for the realm of the dead. He relives the drowning: thrown into the depths, waves and billows passing over him, "the weeds were wrapped around my head" (2:5), down "to the bottoms of the mountains" (2:6) — and then the turn: "yet you have brought my life up from the pit, LORD my God" (2:6), for "when my soul fainted within me, I remembered the LORD" (2:7). Against those who "regard vain idols" and so "forsake their own mercy" (2:8), Jonah vows sacrifice "with the voice of thanksgiving" (2:9) and lands on the book's central confession: "Salvation belongs to the LORD" (2:9). Then the LORD speaks to the fish, and it vomits Jonah out on the dry land.
**Tags:** `prayer` — the whole chapter is a prayer "to the LORD, his God, out of the fish's belly" (2:1), and its testimony is that God hears: "I called because of my affliction to the LORD. He answered me... You heard my voice" (2:2), "my prayer came in to you, into your holy temple" (2:7); `salvation` — deliverance is God's alone to give, and the psalm names it: "you have brought my life up from the pit" (2:6), "Salvation belongs to the LORD" (2:9); `pastoral-hope-in-despair` — hope rising at the bottom: banished from God's sight yet "I will look again toward your holy temple" (2:4), soul fainting yet "I remembered the LORD" (2:7), life brought "up from the pit" (2:6); `thanksgiving` — the rescued man's response: "I will sacrifice to you with the voice of thanksgiving. I will pay that which I have vowed" (2:9)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings

## Jonah 3
**Summary:** The LORD's word comes to Jonah "the second time," and now he goes to Nineveh "according to the LORD's word" (3:3) — an exceedingly great city, three days' journey across. A day's journey in, he cries out his message: "In forty days, Nineveh will be overthrown!" (3:4). The people of Nineveh believe God: they proclaim a fast and put on sackcloth "from their greatest even to their least" (3:5), and the king rises from his throne, exchanges his royal robe for sackcloth and ashes, and decrees that neither man nor animal taste anything — that all be covered with sackcloth, "cry mightily to God", and turn "from his evil way and from the violence that is in his hands" (3:8). Their only ground is a hope: "Who knows whether God will not turn and relent, and turn away from his fierce anger, so that we might not perish?" (3:9). God sees their works, that they turned from their evil way, and relents of the disaster he said he would do to them.
**Tags:** `repentance` — the chapter is Scripture's largest-scale repentance scene: a whole city turning "from his evil way and from the violence that is in his hands" (3:8), and God responding to exactly that — "God saw their works, that they turned from their evil way" (3:10); `divine-judgment` — the announced sentence drives everything: "In forty days, Nineveh will be overthrown!" (3:4), the king's dread of God's "fierce anger" (3:9), and "the disaster which he said he would do to them" — averted only when they turn (3:10); `faith` — "The people of Nineveh believed God" (3:5): they take the bare word of a foreign prophet as God's word and act on it immediately, from greatest to least; `obedience-to-the-word` — hearing and doing after failure: "So Jonah arose, and went to Nineveh, according to the LORD's word" (3:1–3), the exact commission he had fled, now carried out as given; `gods-compassion-for-outsiders` — God's mercy enacted on a pagan city: "God saw their works, that they turned from their evil way" (3:10), and he relents of "the disaster which he said he would do to them" (3:10)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings

## Jonah 4
**Summary:** Nineveh's reprieve "displeased Jonah exceedingly, and he was angry" (4:1), and his prayer finally explains his flight: "I knew that you are a gracious God and merciful, slow to anger, and abundant in loving kindness, and you relent of doing harm" (4:2) — so he asks God to take his life, "for it is better for me to die than to live" (4:3). God answers with a question — "Is it right for you to be angry?" (4:4). Then Jonah goes out and sits east of the city in a booth, waiting to see what will become of the city. God teaches by object lesson: he prepares a vine that shades Jonah's head and makes him "exceedingly glad" (4:6), a worm at dawn that withers it, and a sultry east wind that leaves the prophet faint and asking again to die. The LORD presses the comparison — Jonah was concerned for a vine he neither labored over nor grew, "which came up in a night and perished in a night" (4:10) — and the book ends on God's unanswered question: "Shouldn't I be concerned for Nineveh, that great city, in which are more than one hundred twenty thousand persons who can't discern between their right hand and their left hand, and also many animals?" (4:11).
**Tags:** `gods-love` — the chapter turns on God's self-revealed character, quoted by Jonah as the reason he fled: "you are a gracious God and merciful, slow to anger, and abundant in loving kindness" (4:2), and enacted in God's closing plea of concern for a city's one hundred twenty thousand and "also many animals" (4:10–11); `wrestling-with-god` — an entire chapter of the prophet angry at God and arguing with him — "wasn't this what I said...?" (4:2), "I am right to be angry, even to death" (4:9) — met not by dismissal but by God's patient, probing questions (4:4, 9–11); `gods-compassion-for-outsiders` — the theme's defining text, the book's closing question: "Shouldn't I be concerned for Nineveh, that great city, in which are more than one hundred twenty thousand persons who can't discern between their right hand and their left hand, and also many animals?" (4:11), grounded in the creed Jonah resents: "you are a gracious God and merciful, slow to anger, and abundant in loving kindness" (4:2)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings

## Decisions record

Delegated judgment calls, each a reversible default Jesse can overturn:

1. **Genre framing.** The book is presented as the text gives it — a narrative
   in which the LORD sends a storm, prepares a huge fish, a vine, a worm, and a
   wind — without adjudicating historicity debates in either direction. MHCC's
   apologetic note (that nothing in the story is hard for the Maker of life)
   and any parable/history classification are alike left out of display copy;
   the summary neither defends nor doubts, it describes.
2. **Charitable satire.** The book's exposure of Jonah's anger is rendered
   pointed but not mocking (see the Full summary's closing paragraph): Jonah
   remains God's own servant, corrected, rescued, shaded, and reasoned with. No
   editorial scorn was added beyond what the text's own contrasts carry.
3. **Jonah 1 not subdivided.** BSB gives three headings inside chapter 1 (1:1
   Jonah Flees from the LORD; 1:4 The Great Storm; 1:11 Jonah Cast into the
   Sea), so a 1:1–3 / 1:4–10 / 1:11–17 partition is available. Not taken:
   the three are sequential beats of one continuous episode (flight → pursuit →
   surrender), not genuine thematic divergence, and the Genesis exemplar
   subdivided sparingly (14/50). Reversible — the exact partition above is
   ready if overturned. Chapters 2–4 each carry a single chapter-initial BSB
   heading, so no subdivision arises.
4. **Assyria orientation clause.** Jonah's text never names Assyria. The Full
   summary's one clause identifying Nineveh as a leading city of Assyria, the
   empire that menaced Israel, is standard consensus background and is
   explicitly signposted in-line as what "its first hearers would have
   supplied," not as an in-Jonah claim.
5. **2 Kings 14:25 identification.** The Full summary notes that "Jonah the son
   of Amittai" (1:1) matches the prophet of 2 Kings 14:25; the wording claims a
   shared name and the standard identification, verified word-for-word against
   the staged engwebp VPL ("his servant Jonah the son of Amittai, the prophet,
   who was from Gath Hepher"). Jeroboam II as the reigning king comes from that
   passage's immediate context (2 Kings 14:23–25).
6. **Matthew 12:39–40 signposted, never read back.** The sign of Jonah appears
   once, in the Full summary's final paragraph, explicitly labeled "an
   interpretive frame the New Testament supplies," with the book's own horizon
   stated as the closing question. (Reviewer pass corrected the ref from 12:40
   alone to 12:39–40 — the sign-of-Jonah saying is v. 39, the belly-of-the-fish
   comparison v. 40 — and removed quotation marks from the phrase, since
   Matthew is outside the staged sole-accuracy-authority text.) No tag reflects it: no `resurrection`, no
   `the-cross`, no `second-coming`. Brooks' extensive Israel/Christ typology
   ("Striking Facts") was not imported anywhere.
7. **Tag kept (flagged): `the-lords-discipline` (ch 1).** The chapter never
   uses discipline vocabulary, so this is the doc's most attackable tag. Ruled
   in because the concept's substance (God correcting his own, Heb 12:7–11 /
   Rev 3:19 anchors) is what the chapter depicts, in-chapter: the storm targets
   God's own servant ("because of me this great storm is on you", 1:12), and
   God's purpose is preservation, not destruction (the prepared fish, 1:17).
   Critic may overturn to keep chapter 1 at `providence` alone.
8. **Tag kept (flagged): `salvation` (ch 2).** The concept's lexicon is
   gospel-register ("how can I be saved"), and Jonah 2 is a bodily deliverance.
   Ruled in on the chapter's own summary sentence — "Salvation belongs to the
   LORD" (2:9) is an in-text, load-bearing theological confession that
   deliverance is God's alone, not merely a wet man's escape report. Reversible.
9. **Tag kept (flagged): `pastoral-hope-in-despair` (ch 2, NOT ch 4).**
   Chapter 2 fits the concept's substance cleanly — hope rising at the bottom
   (2:4, 2:6–7), the pit-rescue register of the pack's own Psalm 40:1–3
   anchor. Chapter 4 was deliberately NOT tagged despite lexicon-level matches
   ("take, I beg you, my life from me", 4:3; "It is better for me to die than
   to live", 4:8): Jonah's death-wish there is anger at mercy shown to others,
   and the chapter's substance is God's compassion versus the prophet's anger,
   not the ministry of hope to the despairing. The striking parallel between
   Jonah 4 and the pack's 1 Kings 19:4–7 anchor (a prophet's death-wish met by
   God's patient care) is recorded in the capture file as possible future
   anchor evidence rather than claimed as a tag.
10. **Tag kept (flagged): `divine-judgment` (ch 3).** The judgment is announced
    and then averted, never executed. Ruled substantial: the announced sentence
    ("In forty days, Nineveh will be overthrown!", 3:4) and the dread of God's
    "fierce anger" (3:9) drive every action in the chapter, and the relenting
    of 3:10 is itself a statement about God's judgment — conditional on
    turning. Reversible if the critic reads "averted" as "not substantially
    present."
11. **Tag kept (flagged): `obedience-to-the-word` (ch 3).** Anchored on
