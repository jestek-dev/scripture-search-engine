## Short

Joel begins with a disaster everyone in Judah could see: a locust swarm that stripped the land bare, wave after wave, until even the grain and wine for the daily offerings at the LORD's house were cut off. The prophet names the ruin a warning of something greater — "the day of the LORD is at hand" — and in chapter 2 the locusts become the picture of an unstoppable army advancing under darkened skies, with the LORD himself thundering at its head. Into that terror God speaks an invitation: "turn to me with all your heart," for he is gracious and merciful, slow to anger. He answers his people with promises that keep widening — restored harvests first, then his Spirit poured out on all flesh, then judgment on the nations that scattered and sold his people. The book ends where its hope rests: God himself dwelling in Zion, a refuge to his people on the very day that undoes everything else.

## Full

Joel opens with the aftermath of a locust plague unlike anything living memory could match. The prophet works through the wreckage group by group — drunkards with no wine, farmers with no harvest, priests with no meal offering or drink offering (the daily grain-and-wine offerings) to bring to the LORD's house — and calls each of them to wake up, mourn, and cry to the LORD. The book tells us almost nothing about its own setting: Joel is introduced only as "the son of Pethuel," with no king named and no datable event, and proposals for its date range from the ninth century BC to after the exile. The book itself does not say, and this summary does not guess.

What Joel does say is what the disaster means. The phrase that organizes the whole book is "the day of the LORD" — the day when God himself arrives, and everything that resists him is swept away. The locusts are its herald: "Alas for the day! For the day of the LORD is at hand, and it will come as destruction from the Almighty" (1:15). Chapter 2 raises the picture to full scale — an unstoppable army advancing under darkened skies, with the LORD thundering at its head — and asks the question the whole book turns on: "the day of the LORD is great and very awesome, and who can endure it?" (2:11).

The turn comes immediately: "'Yet even now,' says the LORD, 'turn to me with all your heart, and with fasting, and with weeping, and with mourning.' Tear your heart and not your garments" (2:12–13) — an inward return, not a torn coat for show — grounded in who God is: "gracious and merciful, slow to anger, and abundant in loving kindness, and relents from sending calamity" (2:13). The whole community is called together, from elders to nursing infants; the narrative's "Then" (2:18) implies the call was heeded — the LORD was jealous for his land and answered his people with pity: the invader driven off, the rains returned, and the promise "I will restore to you the years that the swarming locust has eaten" (2:25) — restoration given as God's covenant mercy to a people who returned to him, not a formula anyone can work.

Then the promise widens beyond harvests. "It will happen afterward, that I will pour out my Spirit on all flesh" — sons and daughters prophesying, old men dreaming dreams, young men seeing visions, even servants included (2:28–29) — with wonders in the heavens before the great and terrible day, and an open door through it: "whoever will call on the LORD's name shall be saved" (2:32). The New Testament reads this passage as beginning to be fulfilled at Pentecost, where Peter quotes it (Acts 2) — a fulfillment Joel's own text does not narrate, but which Christian readers have heard in it from the church's first sermon onward. Chapter 3 completes the day of the LORD from the other side: the nations that scattered God's people, divided his land, and trafficked their children — a boy traded for a prostitute, a girl sold for wine — are summoned to the valley of Jehoshaphat (the name means "the LORD judges") — the "valley of decision" — where God sits in judgment. For his people the same roar from Zion is shelter: "the LORD will be a refuge to his people" (3:16). The book ends with God dwelling in Zion, Jerusalem holy, the mountains dripping sweet wine, and a fountain flowing from the LORD's house.

## Joel 1
**Summary:** The LORD's word comes to Joel, son of Pethuel, after a locust disaster without precedent — what one swarm left, the next devoured, until vine, fig tree, grain, new wine, and oil were all gone, and with them the daily meal offering and drink offering at the LORD's house. Joel calls each group in turn to face it: drunkards to weep for the vanished wine, farmers and vineyard keepers to wail over a perished harvest, priests to put on sackcloth and mourn. He tells the priests to sanctify a fast and call a solemn assembly — the whole land gathered at God's house to cry to the LORD — because the disaster is a herald: "the day of the LORD is at hand, and it will come as destruction from the Almighty." The chapter closes in the prophet's own prayer, "LORD, I cry to you," as fire and drought finish what the locusts began and the animals groan for pasture and pant for the dried-up brooks.

Call a solemn assembly. Gather the elders and all the inhabitants of the land to the house of the LORD, your God, and cry to the LORD" (1:14) — mourning in sackcloth turned toward God (1:13–14); `prayer` — the prophet models the cry he commands: "LORD, I cry to you" (1:19), after summoning the whole land to "cry to the LORD" (1:14); `day-of-the-lord` — the book's organizing phrase, sounded first here: "the day of the LORD is at hand, and it will come as destruction from the Almighty" (1:15); `fasting` — the commanded corporate fast: "Sanctify a fast. ## Joel 2
**Summary:** A trumpet sounds in Zion: the day of the LORD is close — "a day of darkness and gloominess," bringing an army before which the land is "as the garden of Eden" and behind which lies "a desolate wilderness," while the earth quakes, the skies go dark, and the LORD thunders at the head of his own forces, so that Joel asks, "who can endure it?" Yet even now, God says, there is a way to turn: "Tear your heart and not your garments" — an inward turning rather than an outward show — "for he is gracious and merciful, slow to anger, and abundant in loving kindness." The whole people is called together — elders, nursing infants, even bride and bridegroom — and the priests are told to plead, "Spare your people, LORD." The LORD answers with pity: the northern army driven away, the early and latter rain restored, threshing floors full, and the promise "I will restore to you the years that the swarming locust has eaten" — covenant mercy to a returning people, and they "will never again be disappointed." Then the promise widens: afterward God will pour out his Spirit on all flesh — sons and daughters, old and young, even servants and handmaids — with wonders before the great and terrible day, and "whoever will call on the LORD's name shall be saved."

**Sections:**
- **2:1–11 — The Army of Locusts** (title: BSB) — The alarm sounds in Zion as an unstoppable army advances like the locust swarm writ large — climbing walls, entering windows, darkening sun and stars — with the LORD's own voice thundering before it on the day of the LORD.
- **2:12–17 — Return with All Your Heart** (title: BSB) — God himself interrupts the terror with an invitation: turn with all your heart, tearing heart and not garments, because he is gracious and merciful; the whole community, priests to infants, is called together to fast, and the priests are told to weep and plead, "Spare your people, LORD."
- **2:18–27 — Restoration Promised** (title: BSB) — The LORD, jealous for his land, answers: the invader removed, grain and new wine and oil sent, the rains returned as before, and the locust-eaten years restored — so that his people will know that he is among Israel and never again be disappointed.

Peter quotes this passage at Pentecost (Acts 2) as beginning to be fulfilled — a New Testament reading of the promise; in Joel's own book it stands as the horizon beyond the restored harvests.

## Joel 3
**Summary:** "In those days," when God restores the fortunes of Judah and Jerusalem, he will gather all nations down into the valley of Jehoshaphat (the name means "the LORD judges") to answer for what they did to his people: scattering them among the nations, dividing his land, casting lots for them, and trading "a boy for a prostitute" and "a girl for wine." Tyre, Sidon, and Philistia, who carried off the LORD's silver and gold and his finest treasures and sold Judah's children to the Greeks, will have their repayment returned on their own heads. The nations are summoned to war — "Beat your plowshares into swords," the mirror image of the famous peace saying in Isaiah 2:4 and Micah 4:3 — but the valley they crowd into is "the valley of decision," where the LORD sits to judge and the harvest of their wickedness is ripe for the sickle. The LORD roars from Zion, shaking heavens and earth, yet that same roar is shelter for his own: "the LORD will be a refuge to his people, and a stronghold to the children of Israel." The book closes with God dwelling in Zion — Jerusalem holy, the mountains dripping sweet wine, a fountain flowing from the LORD's house, and Judah inhabited forever.
**Tags:** `divine-judgment` — "I will gather all nations, and will bring them down into the valley of Jehoshaphat; and I will execute judgment on them there" (3:2); "there I will sit to judge all the surrounding nations" (3:12; the valley of decision, 3:13–14); `refuge-in-trouble` — "the LORD will be a refuge to his people, and a stronghold to the children of Israel" (3:16); `restoration` — "when I restore the fortunes of Judah and Jerusalem" (3:1); the land flowing with sweet wine, milk, and water, and "Judah will be inhabited forever" (3:18–20); `presence-of-god` — "I am the LORD, your God, dwelling in Zion, my holy mountain" (3:17); "for the LORD dwells in Zion" (3:21); `day-of-the-lord` — the day completed from the judgment side: multitudes crowd "the valley of decision" (3:14), where the text names the day of the LORD near, and the LORD roars from Zion (3:16)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings
**Sections:**
- **3:1–16 — The LORD Judges the Nations** (title: BSB) — All nations are gathered into the valley of Jehoshaphat and indicted for scattering God's people and trafficking their children; summoned to war, they arrive instead at the valley of decision, where the LORD roars from Zion in judgment — and is a refuge to his own people.
- **3:17–21 — Blessings for God's People** (title: BSB) — With judgment finished, God dwells in Zion: Jerusalem holy and secure, the mountains dripping sweet wine and the hills flowing with milk, a fountain streaming from the LORD's house, Egypt and Edom desolate for their violence, and Judah inhabited forever, Jerusalem from generation to generation.

## Decisions record

Delegated judgment calls, each a reversible default Jesse can overturn:

1. **Joel 1 not subdivided.** BSB places three headings in the chapter (1:1
   The Invasion of Locusts; 1:8 A Call to Mourning; 1:13 A Call to
   Repentance), but the chapter is one continuous movement — devastation
   described, then lamented, then turned toward God — not genuinely divergent
   themes. Per the subdivide-only-where-themes-diverge rule, it stays whole.
   Joel 2 (four BSB sections) and Joel 3 (two) do genuinely diverge and are
   subdivided on BSB boundaries.
2. **Day-of-the-LORD framing.** The doc presents "the day of the LORD" as
   Joel's own organizing theme operating at two ranges — the locust disaster
   as its herald (1:15) and a greater day still ahead (2:11, 31; 3:14) —
   which is the book's own movement, described without committing to any
   scheme of eschatological timetabling (Brooks's end-times chart was not
   imported).
3. **Acts 2 signposted, not tagged.** The Pentecost fulfillment of 2:28–32 is
   named in prose (book summary; section description for 2:28–32) and
   explicitly framed as the New Testament's reading ("Peter quotes this
   passage... as beginning to be fulfilled"), never asserted as Joel's own
   horizon. No NT-register tag (`holy-spirit-the-comforter` is the Johannine
   Comforter concept) is applied to the chapter.
4. **`dreams-and-visions` on Joel 2.** The anchor is two clauses in one verse
   (2:28), which is thinner than most tags in this doc; it is applied because
   the promise of prophetic dreams and visions is the concrete content of the
   Spirit-outpouring promise, and the `dreams-and-visions` concept pack
   itself names "the Joel promise quoted at Pentecost" among its anchor
   themes. Reversible if Jesse judges the presence insufficient.
5. **`gods-love` considered and dropped for Joel 2.** "abundant in loving
   kindness" (2:13) is a direct statement of God's character and the ground
   of the call to return, but it is one creedal clause and the chapter was at
   the 6-tag cap with tags whose substance fills whole sections. Dropped;
   reversible.
6. **Joel 2:25 and the prosperity guardrail.** "I will restore to you the
   years that the swarming locust has eaten" is a favorite
   personal-material-restoration proof-text. The summary and the
   `restoration`/`gods-provision` justifications keep it in its covenant

   a returning people," per DOCTRINAL-BASIS: blessing never formula or
   entitlement). Calibration (revision round 1): 2:15–17 only command the
   assembly, so the doc keeps summons language ("is called together") and
   signposts once, in the book Full summary, that 2:18's "Then" implies the
   call was heeded — the standard reading, on which the "returning people"
   phrasing rests. No promise-formula phrasing anywhere in the doc.
7. **`restoration` register.** The concept's lexicon is the personal
   renewal-prayer register ("restore my soul"), while Joel's restoration is
   covenantal and national (2:25; 3:1). Tagged anyway because the teaching
   substance — God restores what judgment and loss have eaten — is genuinely
   and centrally depicted, and 2:25 is among the most-searched restoration
   texts. Reversible.
8. **`pastoral-refuge-and-justice` NOT tagged on Joel 3.** The chapter does
   depict God executing justice for the wronged (3:2–8, 19, child-trafficking
   named) and being a refuge (3:16), but that concept is the
   personal-abuse-crisis register (its lexicon: domestic violence, assault);
   tagging a national-eschatological judgment scene would mislead the
   searcher it serves. `divine-judgment` + `refuge-in-trouble` carry the
   chapter honestly.
9. **No PR-#43 ids used.** None of the nine (`asking-in-gods-will`,
