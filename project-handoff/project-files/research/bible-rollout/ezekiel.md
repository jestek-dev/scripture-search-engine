# Ezekiel — book summary

## Short

Ezekiel is a priest among the exiles in Babylonia: in the fifth year of King Jehoiachin's captivity, standing "among the captives by the river Chebar" (1:1), he sees the heavens open and God's throne — carried on living creatures and wheels within wheels — comes to him in exile. Made a watchman answerable for every unwarned life, he spends the book's first half acting out and announcing Jerusalem's fall: sign-acts, allegories of the unfaithful bride, the charter of individual responsibility ("The soul who sins, he shall die," 18:4), and the slow, terrible departure of God's glory from the temple, until on the day the siege begins his own wife dies and he becomes a sign of a grief too deep for mourning. Oracles against the gloating nations follow, and then — once the city has fallen — the same voice turns to hope: God himself will shepherd his flock, give his people "a new heart" and "a new spirit" (36:26), raise the dry bones of the whole house of Israel, and defeat every last enemy. The book closes with a vision of a measured temple filled again with the glory that once left, a river of life flowing from its threshold, and a city named "The LORD is there" (48:35). Through judgment and restoration alike runs one refrain, the book's stated purpose: "Then they will know that I am the LORD" (38:23).

## Full

Ezekiel is unusually precisely dated for a prophetic book — oracle after oracle carries its own date line. A priest, the son of Buzi, Ezekiel receives the LORD's word "in the land of the Chaldeans by the river Chebar" (1:3) — Babylonia — among the exiles, in "the fifth year of King Jehoiachin's captivity" (1:2). Its chapters fall into three ordered movements: judgment on Jerusalem (1–24), judgment on the nations (25–32), and restoration (33–48), with dated oracles marking the road from the fifth year of the captivity (1:2) to the twenty-fifth (40:1) — with one late oracle dated later still, in the twenty-seventh year (29:17). And through all three movements one sentence tolls dozens of times as the declared point of everything God does: "Then they will know that I am the LORD."

The book begins with its most famous scene: a stormy wind out of the north, four living creatures with four faces each, wheels within wheels with rims full of eyes, and above them a throne bearing "the appearance of the likeness of the LORD's glory" (1:28) — God's throne, mobile, arriving in Babylon of all places. The prostrate prophet is addressed as "son of man," fed a scroll of lamentation that tastes sweet, and appointed "a watchman to the house of Israel" (3:17), accountable for the blood of anyone he fails to warn. What follows is warning made visible: Ezekiel besieges a tile, lies bound on his side, shaves his head with a sword, packs an exile's baggage, and trembles over his food, each sign decoded as Jerusalem's coming fall. In vision he is carried to the temple itself to watch its secret idolatries, and then watches the thing the sign-acts have all implied: the glory of the God of Israel rises from the cherubim, pauses at the threshold, moves to the east gate, and leaves the city for the mountain to its east (chapters 8–11). The indictments that follow spare no one — false prophets who whitewash flimsy walls, elders with idols in their hearts, Jerusalem as a rescued foundling turned faithless wife (16), as useless vine wood, as two adulterous sisters (23) — yet among them stands chapter 18's charter of individual responsibility: no one dies for a father's sins, the wicked who turns will live, and God takes no pleasure in the death of anyone — "Therefore turn yourselves, and live!" (18:32). The movement ends on the day Babylon closes on Jerusalem, when God takes Ezekiel's wife, "the desire of your eyes" (24:16), in a single stroke, and the prophet's silent grief becomes the people's sign.

While the siege runs its course, the book turns outward: Ammon, Moab, Edom, and the Philistines are judged for gloating and revenge in the day of Judah's calamity; Tyre, the magnificent merchant city, is sunk like one of her own ships — her prince, who said "I am a god" (28:2), dies the death of a man, and a lament over her king borrows the language of Eden; Egypt, the "staff of reed" (29:6) that broke under Israel's hand, is judged in the person of Pharaoh — the great river monster netted, dragged out, and cast down with all the once-terrifying nations to the world of the dead. These oracles insist that the God of a defeated people still governs every nation, and that no power that mocks his people mocks him for free.

Then the fugitive arrives with the news — "The city has been defeated!" (33:21) — and the whole book pivots. The watchman's charge is renewed, now bearing God's own self-disclosure: "I have no pleasure in the death of the wicked... Turn, turn from your evil ways! For why will you die, house of Israel?" (33:11). The shepherds who fed themselves are dismissed, and God announces, "I myself will be the shepherd of my sheep" (34:15), promising one shepherd, "my servant David," and a covenant of peace. The mountains of Israel will bear fruit again, and the promise goes deeper than land or safety: for the sake of his own profaned holy name God will sprinkle his people clean, "give you a new heart," put "a new spirit within you," and put his own Spirit in them so that they walk in his ways (36:25–27). In the book's best-known vision a valley of dry bones becomes a living army — and the text supplies its own interpretation: "these bones are the whole house of Israel" (37:11), a nation as good as dead and buried, brought up out of its grave and home. The oracles against Gog, of the land of Magog (38–39) — a last, vast coalition destroyed by God on the mountains of Israel — carry the promise to its edge: after this, God says, "I won't hide my face from them any more" (39:29).

The final vision (40–48) answers the tragedy of chapters 8–11 point for point. In the twenty-fifth year of the captivity Ezekiel is set on a high mountain and walked through a temple measured gate by gate, wall by wall, its architecture preaching one sentence: "to make a separation between that which was holy and that which was common" (42:20). Then the glory that departed toward the east returns "from the way of the east" and fills the house, and a voice declares, "this is the place of my throne... where I will dwell among the children of Israel forever" (43:7). Ordinances for priests, prince, feasts, and honest scales follow; a river flows from under the threshold, deepening from ankles to "waters to swim in" (47:5), healing the sea and feeding ever-bearing trees — "Its fruit will be for food, and its leaf for healing" (47:12); the land is allotted to the tribes — resident foreigners inheriting like the native-born — and the book's last words name the city: "The LORD is there." (48:35). Faithful readers have long differed over how this closing vision is fulfilled, and these summaries do not decide that question; what the vision itself insists on is the arc of the whole book — the God whose glory left a defiled house comes back to dwell among a cleansed people, and then the exiles, and the nations, and the reader will know that he is the LORD.

# The forty-eight chapters

## Ezekiel 1
**Summary:** The book opens among the captives by the river Chebar in the land of the Chaldeans — Babylonia, where the exiles have been settled — in the fifth year of King Jehoiachin's captivity, when "the heavens were opened" and Ezekiel the priest "saw visions of God" (1:1). Out of a stormy wind from the north comes a great cloud flashing with lightning, and from its center four living creatures, each with four faces — a man, a lion, an ox, an eagle — and four wings, going "straight forward" wherever the spirit goes (1:10–12). Beside each creature stands a high, dreadful wheel, "a wheel within a wheel" with rims "full of eyes all around", moving and rising exactly as the creatures move, "for the spirit of the living creature was in the wheels" (1:16–20). Over their heads stretches an expanse "like an awesome crystal", and above it "the likeness of a throne, as the appearance of a sapphire stone", and on the throne "a likeness as the appearance of a man", wrapped in fire and in brightness like a rainbow in the clouds on a rainy day (1:22–28). Only at the end does the chapter say what it has been describing — "This was the appearance of the likeness of the LORD's glory" — and Ezekiel falls on his face and hears a voice speaking (1:28): far from the ruined homeland and the temple, God's throne has come to an exile.
**Tags:** `dreams-and-visions` — "the heavens were opened, and I saw visions of God" (1:1); the whole chapter is that opening vision, from the stormy wind to the throne (1:4–28); `presence-of-god` — the vision climaxes in "the appearance of the likeness of the LORD's glory" (1:28) — God's glorious presence appearing to a captive by the river Chebar, prostrating him on his face; `angels` — the throne-bearing living creatures (the register here is cherubim carrying God's throne, not messengers): four faces and four wings each, going "straight forward" wherever the spirit goes, the wheels rising exactly as they rise (1:10–12, 15–21); `glory-of-god` — the manifest glory itself, the vision's own closing name for everything it described: "This was the appearance of the likeness of the LORD's glory" (1:28) — the glory register beside the `presence-of-god` tag; `sojourners-and-strangers` — the setting the whole book lives in: "among the captives by the river Chebar" (1:1), God's throne arriving to an exile far from homeland and temple
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings

## Ezekiel 2
**Summary:** The voice tells the fallen prophet, "Son of man, stand on your feet, and I will speak with you" (2:1) — "son of man" is God's habitual address to Ezekiel throughout the book, marking him as a human one, a mortal man standing before the divine glory — and the Spirit enters him and sets him on his feet. He is sent to the children of Israel, "a nation of rebels" whose children are "impudent and stiff-hearted", to say "This is what the Lord GOD says"; whether they hear or refuse, "they will know that there has been a prophet among them" (2:3–5). Three times God tells him not to be afraid of them or their words, "though briers and thorns are with you, and you dwell among scorpions" (2:6). The charge turns personal — "Don't be rebellious like that rebellious house. Open your mouth, and eat that which I give you" — and a hand stretches out holding a scroll, written on both sides with "lamentations, mourning, and woe" (2:8–10).
**Tags:** `fear-not` — "don't be afraid of them, neither be afraid of their words... Don't be afraid of their words, nor be dismayed at their looks" (2:6); `obedience-to-the-word` — where the rebellious house refuses to hear, the prophet must hear and do: "hear what I tell you. Don't be rebellious like that rebellious house. Open your mouth, and eat that which I give you" (2:8); `hardness-of-heart` — the audience diagnosed before a word is preached: "a nation of rebels" whose children are "impudent and stiff-hearted" (2:3–5), free to hear or refuse but rebellious either way (2:5, 7)

## Ezekiel 3
**Summary:** Ezekiel eats the scroll, and the words of lamentation are "as sweet as honey in my mouth" (3:3) — God's word received into his inmost being before it is ever preached. He is sent not to peoples of a strange speech, who would have listened, but to the "obstinate and hard-hearted" house of Israel; so God hardens his messenger to match them — "I have made your forehead as a diamond, harder than flint" (3:7–9). The Spirit lifts him up amid the sound of the living creatures and a great rushing — "Blessed be the LORD's glory from his place" (3:12) — and he sits overwhelmed for seven days among the captives at Tel Aviv by the river Chebar. Then comes the charge that defines his office: "I have made you a watchman to the house of Israel" — if he fails to warn the wicked, the wicked man dies in his iniquity "but I will require his blood at your hand"; if he warns and is refused, "you have delivered your soul" (3:17–19). Finally, in the plain, the glory appears again, and God shuts the prophet in his house, bound with ropes and mute — his tongue stuck to the roof of his mouth — to speak only when God opens his mouth: "He who hears, let him hear; and he who refuses, let him refuse" (3:26–27).
**Tags:** `delight-in-the-word` — he takes God's words in and finds them "as sweet as honey in my mouth" (3:3), told to "receive in your heart and hear with your ears all my words" (3:10); `gods-protection` — God equips his prophet against the hard faces of a rebellious house: "I have made your face hard against their faces... your forehead as a diamond, harder than flint" (3:8–9); `fear-not` — "Don't be afraid of them, neither be dismayed at their looks, though they are a rebellious house" (3:9); `watchman-and-warning` — the charge that defines the office: "I have made you a watchman to the house of Israel" — warn, or the unwarned man dies and "I will require his blood at your hand"; warn and be refused, and "you have delivered your soul" (3:17–19); `hardness-of-heart` — the diagnosis of the hearers: an "obstinate and hard-hearted" house of Israel that will not listen (3:7) — the hardness God answers by hardening his messenger to match (3:8–9)

## Ezekiel 4
**Summary:** God commands the first of Ezekiel's sign-acts — pieces of commanded street-theater performed before the exiles' eyes. The prophet is to take a tile, portray Jerusalem on it, and lay siege to it with forts, a mound, camps, and battering rams, with an iron pan set as a wall between him and the city: "This shall be a sign to the house of Israel" (4:1–3). He must lie bound on his left side three hundred ninety days to "bear the iniquity of the house of Israel", then forty days on his right side for the house of Judah, "each day for a year" (4:4–6). His food acts out the siege too: bread of mixed grains eaten by weight — twenty shekels a day — and water by measure, baked in the people's sight over dung; when Ezekiel protests that he has never eaten anything unclean, God permits cow's dung instead of human (4:9–15). The meaning is spelled out: "I will break the staff of bread in Jerusalem. They will eat bread by weight, and with fearfulness" — lacking bread and water, dismayed, they will "pine away in their iniquity" (4:16–17).
**Tags:** `divine-judgment` — the siege of Jerusalem enacted in miniature (4:1–3) and declared: "I will break the staff of bread in Jerusalem. They will eat bread by weight, and with fearfulness" (4:16); `sin` — the sign's weight is Israel's guilt: "lay the iniquity of the house of Israel on it... you shall bear their iniquity" (4:4–5), until the people "pine away in their iniquity" (4:17)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings

- **4:1–8 — A Sign of Jerusalem's Siege** (title: BSB) — The tile-city under siege and the prophet lying bound on his side, bearing Israel's three hundred ninety days and Judah's forty, a day for each year of iniquity.

## Ezekiel 5
**Summary:** In a further sign-act, Ezekiel takes a sharp sword "as a barber's razor", shaves his head and beard, and divides the hair by weight into thirds: one third burned in the middle of the city, one third struck with the sword around it, one third scattered to the wind — with only a few hairs bound in the folds of his robe, and some of even those thrown into the fire (5:1–4). The interpretation is blunt: "This is Jerusalem" (5:5) — set by God in the middle of the nations, she has rebelled against his ordinances "in doing wickedness more than the nations," so God himself turns against her: "Behold, I, even I, am against you; and I will execute judgments among you in the sight of the nations" (5:6–8). The horrors of siege are named without flinching — fathers eating sons and sons eating fathers (5:10) — because "you have defiled my sanctuary with all your detestable things"; "My eye won't spare, and I will have no pity" (5:11). The thirds of hair become thirds of people — pestilence and famine, sword, and scattering with a drawn sword behind — until Jerusalem is left "a reproach and a taunt, an instruction and an astonishment" to the nations around her: "I, the LORD, have spoken it" (5:12–17).
**Tags:** `divine-judgment` — "Behold, I, even I, am against you; and I will execute judgments among you in the sight of the nations" (5:8), spelled out in pestilence, famine, sword, and scattering (5:12); `sin` — "She has rebelled against my ordinances in doing wickedness more than the nations" (5:6), and has "defiled my sanctuary with all your detestable things, and with all your abominations" (5:11)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings

## Ezekiel 6
**Summary:** Ezekiel sets his face toward the mountains of Israel — the high country dotted with hilltop shrines — and prophesies: "Behold, I, even I, will bring a sword on you, and I will destroy your high places" (6:3). Altars will be desolate, idols broken, and the slain will fall before the very idols they served, their bones scattered around the altars, "and you will know that I am the LORD" (6:4–7) — the recognition refrain that will sound through the whole book. Yet judgment is not the last word: "Yet I will leave a remnant," survivors among the nations who "will remember me" and "will loathe themselves in their own sight for the evils which they have committed" (6:8–9). Then the prophet must strike his hand, stamp his foot, and cry "Alas!" over the house of Israel as sword, famine, and pestilence run their course, until the land lies desolate "from the wilderness toward Diblah" and they know that the LORD has not spoken in vain (6:10–14).
**Tags:** `divine-judgment` — "Behold, I, even I, will bring a sword on you, and I will destroy your high places" (6:3), with sword, famine, and pestilence accomplishing God's wrath (6:11–12); `repentance` — the promised remnant "will remember me among the nations... they will loathe themselves in their own sight for the evils which they have committed in all their abominations" (6:9); `idolatry` — the high-places oracle: "I will destroy your high places," altars made desolate, idols broken, and the slain fallen before the very idols they served (6:3–7, 13); `remnant` — "Yet I will leave a remnant": survivors scattered among the nations who will remember God there (6:8–10)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings

## Ezekiel 7
**Summary:** The chapter is a single hammering oracle of doom on the land of Israel: "An end! The end has come on the four corners of the land" (7:2), "A disaster! A unique disaster! Behold, it comes" (7:5). The day is near — "a day of tumult, and not of joyful shouting" (7:7) — and it is not arbitrary: "The rod has blossomed. Pride has budded", and God will judge "according to your ways", bringing their own abominations on them (7:3–11). Ordinary life collapses — buyer and seller, trumpet and battle line, all overtaken (7:12–14) — and wealth proves worthless: "They will cast their silver in the streets... Their silver and their gold won't be able to deliver them in the day of the LORD's wrath", because it had become "the stumbling block of their iniquity" (7:19). For a land "full of bloody crimes" and a city "full of violence" (7:23), even the sources of help fail — "They will seek peace, and there will be none"; vision perishes from the prophet, the law from the priest, counsel from the elders — "Then they will know that I am the LORD" (7:25–27).
**Tags:** `divine-judgment` — "Now the end is on you, and I will send my anger on you, and will judge you according to your ways" (7:3); "My eye won't spare, neither will I have pity" (7:9); `sin` — the judgment answers "all your abominations" (7:3, 8), a land "full of bloody crimes" and a city "full of violence" (7:23), with silver and gold become "the stumbling block of their iniquity" (7:19); `day-of-the-lord` — the day-oracle register: "a day of tumult, and not of joyful shouting" is near (7:7), the day come with its rod blossomed (7:10–12), and wealth worthless "in the day of the LORD's wrath" (7:19)

## Ezekiel 8
**Summary:** A year into his sign-acts — the sixth year, with the elders of Judah sitting before him in his house — the Lord GOD's hand falls on Ezekiel, and a fiery figure lifts him by a lock of hair: "the Spirit lifted me up between earth and the sky, and brought me in the visions of God to Jerusalem" (8:1–3). There, with the glory of the God of Israel present as in the plain, he is walked through a guided tour of the temple's secret corruptions, each stage capped with the promise of "yet greater abominations" still to come (8:6, 13, 15). At the north gate stands "the image of jealousy, which provokes to jealousy" (8:3–5); through a hole dug in the wall he finds seventy elders offering incense to creeping things and idols portrayed on the walls, telling themselves in their dark "rooms of imagery": "The LORD doesn't see us. ## Ezekiel 15
**Summary:** God puts a blunt question to Ezekiel: what is vine wood good for, compared with any other tree? ## Ezekiel 16

## Ezekiel 17
**Summary:** Ezekiel poses a riddle: a great, many-colored eagle crops the top of a cedar of Lebanon and carries it to "a city of merchants" (17:4), then plants seed of the land that grows into a low, spreading vine — until the vine bends its roots and branches toward a second great eagle, and God asks, "Will it prosper?" (17:9). The explanation is current events: Babylon's king carried Jerusalem's king and princes to Babylon, set one of the royal offspring on the throne under covenant and oath, "that the kingdom might be brought low... but that by keeping his covenant it might stand" (17:14) — and that king rebelled, sending ambassadors to Egypt for horses and armies. Because "he has despised the oath by breaking the covenant" (17:18), God takes the treachery personally — "my oath that he has despised and my covenant that he has broken" (17:19) — and will spread his net over him and bring him to Babylon to die there; Pharaoh will be no help. Then the eagle imagery turns to promise: God himself will take "a tender one" from the top of the cedar and plant it "in the mountain of the height of Israel" (17:22–23), where it becomes a good cedar sheltering birds of every kind — "I, the LORD, have brought down the high tree, have exalted the low tree" (17:24). Where kings plant and pluck up for politics, God plants a kingdom that will actually stand.

## Ezekiel 18
**Summary:** God confronts a proverb the exiles use to call him unfair: "The fathers have eaten sour grapes, and the children's teeth are set on edge" (18:2). His answer is a charter of individual responsibility: "Behold, all souls are mine; as the soul of the father, so also the soul of the son is mine. The soul who sins, he shall die" (18:4). Three generations make the case — a just man who does what is lawful and right shall surely live; his violent, oppressive son shall die for his own deeds; and that man's son, who sees his father's sins "and fears, and doesn't do likewise" (18:14), shall live — for "The son shall not bear the iniquity of the father, neither shall the father bear the iniquity of the son" (18:20). Nor is anyone locked into their past: the wicked who turns from all his sins shall live — "None of his transgressions that he has committed will be remembered against him" (18:22), while the righteous who turns to iniquity forfeits his record too — and behind it all stands God's own heart: "Have I any pleasure in the death of the wicked?... and not rather that he should return from his way, and live?" (18:23). To the complaint "The way of the Lord is not equal" (18:25) God returns the charge and closes with an open door: "Return, and turn yourselves from all your transgressions... make yourself a new heart and a new spirit... For I have no pleasure in the death of him who dies... Therefore turn yourselves, and live!" (18:30–32). It is the book's hinge for every exile tempted to blame the past: your life before God is your own, and it is not too late.

His blood will be on him" (18:13); `repentance` — turning is the whole appeal: "if the wicked turns from all his sins... he shall surely live" (18:21), "Return, and turn yourselves from all your transgressions" (18:30), "Therefore turn yourselves, and live!" (18:32); `forgiveness-of-sins` — the turned sinner's past is wiped: "None of his transgressions that he has committed will be remembered against him. In his righteousness that he has done, he shall live" (18:22); `divine-judgment` — "I will judge you, house of Israel, everyone according to his ways" (18:30), each soul answering for itself, not for father or son (18:20); `individual-responsibility` — the charter text itself: "The soul who sins, he shall die" (18:4) and "The son shall not bear the iniquity of the father, neither shall the father bear the iniquity of the son" (18:20), argued through three generations and defended against the fairness charge (18:25, 29); `justice-and-oppression` — the ledgers that define righteous and wicked are acts of justice to the vulnerable: pledges restored, no one robbed, bread given to the hungry (18:7–8, 16–17), while the violent, oppressing son hears "He has done all these abominations. ## Ezekiel 19
**Summary:** Ezekiel is told to "take up a lamentation for the princes of Israel" (19:1) — a funeral song for a royal house still technically alive. Their mother is pictured as a lioness raising cubs among lions: one cub learned to catch prey and "devoured men" (19:3), and the nations took him "with hooks to the land of Egypt" (19:4); she raised another, who laid waste cities until the nations snared him, caged him, and "brought him to the king of Babylon" (19:9), silencing his roar on the mountains of Israel. Then the image changes: the mother was a vine planted by waters, fruitful, with "strong branches for the scepters of those who ruled" (19:11) — but it was "plucked up in fury" (19:12), withered by the east wind, replanted in the wilderness, and fire from its own branches has devoured its fruit, "so that there is in it no strong branch to be a scepter to rule" (19:14). The chapter mourns Judah's last kings — captivity to Egypt, captivity to Babylon, a dynasty burned out from within — and closes over its own form: "This is a lamentation, and shall be for a lamentation" (19:14).
**Tags:** `lament` — the chapter is a commanded dirge from first line to last: "take up a lamentation for the princes of Israel" (19:1), closing over its own form, "This is a lamentation, and shall be for a lamentation" (19:14). ## Ezekiel 20
**Summary:** In the seventh year of exile, elders come to inquire of the LORD, and God refuses — "As I live... I will not be inquired of by you" (20:3) — telling Ezekiel instead to make them know "the abominations of their fathers" (20:4). The history reads as one long pattern in three rounds: in Egypt God chose Israel, swore to bring them to a land "flowing with milk and honey" (20:6), and commanded them to throw away Egypt's idols — "But they rebelled against me and wouldn't listen to me" (20:8); in the wilderness he gave statutes by which a man "will live" and Sabbaths as "a sign between me and them" (20:11–12), and both the first generation and their children rebelled and profaned them; in the land they ran to every high hill and thick tree with sacrifices (20:28). Each round, wrath was stayed for one reason: "I worked for my name's sake, that it should not be profaned in the sight of the nations" (20:9, 14, 22). Now the present generation's plan — "We will be as the nations... to serve wood and stone" (20:32) — "will not be at all": "with a mighty hand, with an outstretched arm, and with wrath poured out, I will be king over you" (20:33). God will bring them into "the wilderness of the peoples" for a face-to-face judgment like the exodus, make them "pass under the rod" into "the bond of the covenant" (20:37), purge out the rebels, and then gather a people who serve him on his holy mountain, accepted "as a pleasant aroma" (20:41), loathing their old ways and knowing he has dealt with them "for my name's sake" (20:44). A closing oracle sets fire to "the forest of the south" (20:46–48), and Ezekiel sighs that his hearers dismiss him: "Isn't he a speaker of parables?" (20:49).

## Ezekiel 21
**Summary:** The parable of the burning forest is unsheathed into plain steel: "Behold, I am against you, and will draw my sword out of its sheath, and will cut off from you the righteous and the wicked" (21:3) — coming judgment on Jerusalem will not sort by neighborhood. ## Ezekiel 22
**Summary:** God asks Ezekiel, "Will you judge the bloody city?" (22:2), and the indictment of Jerusalem reads like a broken Ten Commandments: princes shed blood, the people have "treated father and mother with contempt," oppressed the foreigner, and "wronged the fatherless and the widow" (22:7), holy things despised and Sabbaths profaned (22:8), bribery, slander, sexual abominations within families, and interest, increase, and dishonest gain — they "have forgotten me," says the Lord GOD (22:12). The sentence is scattering among the nations to purge out her filthiness (22:15). A second oracle recasts the nation as metal: "the house of Israel has become dross to me" (22:18), and God will gather them into Jerusalem as into a furnace and melt them in the fire of his wrath. A third surveys every rank — prophets like "a roaring lion ravening the prey" (22:25), priests who "have done violence to my law" and blur holy and common (22:26), princes like wolves after dishonest gain, prophets plastering it all with whitewash, and the people of the land oppressing "the poor and needy" (22:29). ## Ezekiel 23
**Summary:** In the book's second and harshest marriage allegory, two sisters — "Oholah the elder, and Oholibah her sister... Samaria is Oholah, and Jerusalem Oholibah" (23:4) — became the LORD's, yet had "played the prostitute in Egypt... in their youth" (23:3). The chapter's deliberately graphic language of prostitution and lust portrays each capital's idolatries and foreign entanglements: Oholah gave herself to the Assyrians and their idols, so God "delivered her into the hand of her lovers" (23:9), who exposed and killed her; Oholibah watched, "yet she was more corrupt in her lusting than she" (23:11) — chasing Assyrians, then the Chaldeans whose painted images she saw on a wall, until her soul was alienated and she reached back to the ways of her youth in Egypt. Because "you have forgotten me and cast me behind your back" (23:35), the allegory insists, Jerusalem's fall is not politics but betrayal of her covenant husband.

## Ezekiel 24
**Summary:** On the very day the siege begins, Ezekiel is told to "write the name of the day, this same day. The king of Babylon drew close to Jerusalem this same day" (24:2) — the ninth year, tenth month, tenth day. The parable of the cauldron pictures the city under siege: choice pieces and choice bones boiled in a pot, but this is a "cauldron whose rust is in it" (24:6) — "Woe to the bloody city" — her shed blood left "on the bare rock," uncovered, crying for wrath (24:7–8); when the meat is gone the pot itself is set empty on the coals until its bronze burns and its rust melts away, for "Because I have cleansed you and you weren't cleansed, you won't be cleansed from your filthiness any more, until I have caused my wrath toward you to rest" (24:13) — "I, the LORD, have spoken it... I won't go back. I won't spare" (24:14). Then the word turns unbearably personal: "Son of man, behold, I will take away from you the desire of your eyes with one stroke; yet you shall neither mourn nor weep" (24:16). "So I spoke to the people in the morning, and at evening my wife died. So I did in the morning as I was commanded" (24:18) — sighing, "but not aloud" (24:17), turban on, sandals on, no mourner's bread. When the people ask what his behavior means, the answer lands like the siege itself: God is about to profane his own sanctuary, "the pride of your power, the desire of your eyes" (24:21), and their sons and daughters will fall by the sword; like Ezekiel they will not mourn aloud but "pine away in your iniquities, and moan one toward another" (24:23) — "Thus Ezekiel will be a sign to you" (24:24). When the fugitive brings news of the fall, the prophet's long muteness will end (24:26–27), closing the book's first movement: from here the judgment oracles turn outward to the nations.

## Ezekiel 25
**Summary:** With Jerusalem's fall pronounced, the LORD turns Ezekiel's face outward to Judah's nearest neighbors: four short oracles, each built the same way — "because" of what a nation did when Judah fell, "therefore" its own judgment. Ammon, which said "Aha!" against God's profaned sanctuary and Judah's captivity, will be handed to the children of the east, its capital Rabbah "a stable for camels" (25:5); Moab, which sneered that "the house of Judah is like all the nations" (25:8), is opened to the same invaders; Edom, which "has dealt against the house of Judah by taking vengeance" (25:12), will be laid desolate by the hand of God's own people; and the Philistines, who destroyed "with perpetual hostility" (25:15), will lose even "the remnant of the sea coast" (25:16). The refrain that closes oracle after oracle states what these judgments are for — not national spite but recognition: "Then you will know that I am the LORD" (25:5, 7).

## Ezekiel 27
**Summary:** Ezekiel is told to take up a lamentation over Tyre, and the dirge pictures the city as a magnificent trading ship: planks of cypress from Senir, a cedar mast from Lebanon, oars of Bashan oak, benches of inlaid ivory, a sail of fine embroidered Egyptian linen — "Your builders have perfected your beauty" (27:4). (27:32); the merchants hiss, "You have come to a terrible end, and you will be no more" (27:36).

## Ezekiel 28
**Summary:** The prophecy narrows from the city to its ruler. Because the prince of Tyre's heart is lifted up — "you have said, 'I am a god, I sit in the seat of God, in the middle of the seas;' yet you are man, and no god" (28:2) — the wisdom and trading that built his riches will not save him: God will bring "the terrible of the nations" (28:7) against his beauty, and he will die asking whether he can still say "I am God" before the one who kills him (28:9). Then comes a lamentation over the king of Tyre in the language of Eden: "You were the seal of full measure" (28:12), "You were in Eden, the garden of God" (28:13), "the anointed cherub who covers" (28:14) — perfect from the day he was created "until unrighteousness was found in you" (28:15), when violence-filled commerce and beauty-fed pride ended with him cast as profane from God's mountain and turned to ashes before watching kings.
