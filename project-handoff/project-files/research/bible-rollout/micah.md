## Short

Micah is a small-town prophet with a two-city audience: his book opens "concerning Samaria and Jerusalem," and it swings, three times over, from judgment to hope. The judgment is aimed at people with power — land-grabbers who seize fields and houses, rulers who judge for bribes, priests who teach for a price, prophets who preach whatever they are paid to say — and it climaxes in the shocking sentence that Zion will be plowed like a field. The hope is just as concrete: the mountain of the LORD's temple raised above the hills with nations streaming to it and swords beaten into plowshares, a ruler from little Bethlehem whose origins are from ancient times, and a remnant gathered home. In between stands the book's most quoted question and answer — what does the LORD require of you, but to act justly, to love mercy, and to walk humbly with your God? — and at the end its most astonished one: "Who is a God like you, who pardons iniquity?"

## Full

Micah came from Morasheth, a small town in Judah's countryside, and prophesied "in the days of Jotham, Ahaz, and Hezekiah, kings of Judah" (1:1) — the same decades Isaiah was preaching in the capital. His book is addressed to both capitals at once, Samaria in the north and Jerusalem in the south, and its opening scene is a courtroom the size of the world: the LORD comes down from his holy temple, the mountains melt under him, and the charge is not what the nations have done but "the disobedience of Jacob" and "the sins of the house of Israel" (1:5).

The book moves in three cycles, each running from judgment into hope. The first (chapters 1–2) announces Samaria's ruin and a blow that "reaches to the gate of my people, even to Jerusalem" (1:9), then names the sin behind it: men who lie awake devising evil, coveting fields and seizing them, driving women and children out of their homes — and who tell the prophets to stop prophesying. Yet the cycle ends with God gathering "the remnant of Israel" like a flock, their king passing on before them "with the LORD at their head" (2:12–13). The second cycle (chapters 3–5) is the fiercest. Rulers who should know justice devour the people they govern; prophets cry "Peace!" for whoever feeds them; leaders lean on the LORD while abhorring justice — "Therefore Zion for your sake will be plowed like a field" (3:12). Immediately the same mountain is shown transfigured: "in the latter days" the mountain of the LORD's temple is established above the hills, nations stream to it to learn his ways, swords are beaten into plowshares, and everyone sits unafraid under vine and fig tree (4:1–4). Out of besieged Judah comes the promise of a ruler from Bethlehem Ephrathah, "small among the clans of Judah," whose "goings out" — his origins — "are from of old, from ancient times" (5:2), a shepherd-king who "will be our peace" (5:5). Matthew's Gospel later cites this verse at Jesus' birth in Bethlehem (Matthew 2:5–6); within Micah's own horizon the promise answers Assyria's threat with a coming ruler in whom the LORD's strength dwells.

The third cycle (chapters 6–7) is a lawsuit and a lament. The LORD calls the mountains as witnesses — "the LORD has a case against his people" (6:2) — recites his acts of rescue from Egypt onward, and answers the worshiper's escalating offers (burnt offerings, thousands of rams, even a firstborn) with the book's center of gravity: "He has shown you, O man, what is good. What does the LORD require of you, but to act justly, to love mercy, and to walk humbly with your God?" (6:8). The city's dishonest scales and violent rich earn the old futility curses — you will sow, but won't reap — and chapter 7 opens with the prophet gleaning an empty vineyard: the godly have perished, and a man's enemies are the men of his own house. But the lament pivots on a resolve — "But as for me, I will look to the LORD. I will wait for the God of my salvation" (7:7) — and moves through defiant hope ("When I fall, I will arise," 7:8) and honest confession ("I will bear the indignation of the LORD, because I have sinned against him," 7:9) into the confidence that God "will bring me out to the light" (7:9).

Micah's own name is a question — in Hebrew it is short for "Who is like the LORD?" — and the book ends by answering it. "Who is a God like you, who pardons iniquity, and passes over the disobedience of the remnant of his heritage?" He does not retain his anger forever, "because he delights in loving kindness"; he will tread our iniquities under foot and "cast all their sins into the depths of the sea," keeping the mercy he swore to Abraham and Jacob "from the days of old" (7:18–20). Judgment is real in this book, but pardon gets the last word.

## Micah 1
**Summary:** The book opens by naming its moment: the LORD's word came to Micah of Morasheth in the days of Jotham, Ahaz, and Hezekiah, "concerning Samaria and Jerusalem" (1:1). The LORD himself comes down from his holy temple to tread the high places of the earth, and the mountains melt under him — and the cause is not the nations' wickedness but "the disobedience of Jacob" and "the sins of the house of Israel" (1:2–5). Samaria will become "a rubble heap of the field," her idols beaten to pieces and her temple gifts burned (1:6–7). The blow does not stop at the north: her wounds are incurable, and it "reaches to the gate of my people, even to Jerusalem" (1:9), so Micah goes stripped and wailing through a dirge over a string of Judean towns — in Hebrew, many of their names are grim wordplays on their fate — ending with a call to shave the head in mourning, "for they have gone into captivity" (1:8–16).
**Tags:** `divine-judgment` — the LORD's descent in judgment, "behold, the LORD comes out of his place, and will come down and tread on the high places of the earth," and the sentence on Samaria, "I will make Samaria like a rubble heap of the field" (1:3–7); `sin` — the stated cause of it all: "All this is for the disobedience of Jacob, and for the sins of the house of Israel" (1:5, 13); `idolatry` — Samaria's sentence falls on her images: "I will make Samaria like a rubble heap of the field" (1:6), her idols beaten to pieces and her temple gifts — gathered as a prostitute's hire — burned (1:7)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings

## Micah 2
**Summary:** Micah turns from capitals to neighborhoods: woe to those who devise iniquity on their beds and practice it when morning is light, who "covet fields and seize them, and houses, then take them away," oppressing a man and his heritage — the family land handed down to him (2:1–2). God answers the land-grabbers in kind: he is "planning against these people a disaster," and the fields they stole will be divided away from them, with no one left to divide land for them "in the LORD's assembly" (2:3–5). The comfortable tell the prophets, "Don't prophesy!" — and Micah answers that a man walking in a spirit of falsehood and lying, promising wine and strong drink, "would be the prophet of this people" (2:6–11). Then, without warning, judgment gives way to hope: God will surely gather "the remnant of Israel" like a flock in the middle of its pasture, and one who breaks open the way will go up before them: "Their king passes on before them, with the LORD at their head" (2:12–13).
**Tags:** `sin` — sin planned deliberately and carried out with power: "Woe to those who devise iniquity and work evil on their beds! … They covet fields and seize them" (2:1–2, 8–9); `divine-judgment` — the measured recompense: "Behold, I am planning against these people a disaster, from which you will not remove your necks" (2:3–5, 10); `justice-and-oppression` — the land-grab indictment at the theme's OT core: they "covet fields and seize them, and houses, then take them away," oppressing a man and his heritage (2:1–2, 8–9); `false-prophets` — the comfortable demand "Don't prophesy!" (2:6), and Micah answers that a man walking in falsehood and lying, promising wine and strong drink, "would be the prophet of this people" (2:6–11); `remnant` — the abrupt hope-turn: God will surely gather "the remnant of Israel" like a flock in the middle of its pasture, their king passing on before them (2:12–13)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings
**Sections:**
- **2:1–5 — Woe to Oppressors** (title: BSB) — Those who plot on their beds and seize fields and houses at dawn will have the same measured back to them: their stolen fields assigned to others, and no portion left for them among the LORD's people.
- **2:6–11 — Reproof of False Prophets** (title: BSB) — The oppressors demand that prophesying stop; Micah answers that a liar promising wine and strong drink is exactly the prophet this people wants.
- **2:12–13 — The Remnant of Israel** (title: BSB) — An abrupt turn to hope: God will assemble the remnant of Israel like a swarming flock, and their king will pass on before them with the LORD at their head.

## Micah 3
**Summary:** Micah confronts the leadership directly: the heads of Jacob, whose job is "to know justice," hate the good and love the evil, and he pictures their exploitation as butchery — they eat the flesh of his people and chop them up "as meat within the cauldron" (3:1–3). When disaster comes, they will cry to the LORD and he will not answer; he will hide his face, "because they made their deeds evil" (3:4). The prophets are no better: they proclaim "Peace!" for whoever feeds them and prepare war against whoever does not, so night falls on them — no vision, no answer from God (3:5–7). Micah stands apart, "full of power by the LORD's Spirit," to declare to Jacob his disobedience (3:8). The indictment peaks in verse 11 — leaders judge for bribes, priests teach for a price, prophets tell fortunes for money, yet they lean on the LORD and say, "No disaster will come on us" — and draws the book's most shocking sentence: "Zion for your sake will be plowed like a field, and Jerusalem will become heaps of rubble" (3:9–12).
**Tags:** `divine-judgment` — the sentence on the city built with blood: "Therefore Zion for your sake will be plowed like a field, and Jerusalem will become heaps of rubble" (3:12), with God refusing to answer when the guilty cry to him (3:4, 6–7); `self-deception` — leaders who "abhor justice, and pervert all equity" yet assure themselves, "Isn't the LORD among us? No disaster will come on us" (3:9–11) — treating God's presence as a guarantee while doing the evil he condemns; `justice-and-oppression` — leaders whose job is "to know justice" hate the good, eat the flesh of the people "as meat within the cauldron" (3:1–3), and judge for bribes while priests teach for a price (3:9–11); `false-prophets` — the classic prophet-for-hire profile: prophets who proclaim "Peace!" for whoever feeds them and prepare war against whoever does not, answered with night, no vision, and no answer from God (3:5–7), while prophets tell fortunes for money (3:11)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings

## Micah 4
**Summary:** The rubble of 3:12 becomes the site of the book's brightest vision: "in the latter days" the mountain of the LORD's temple will be established on top of the mountains, and peoples will stream to it, many nations saying, "Come! Let's go up to the mountain of the LORD," so that "he will teach us of his ways" (4:1–2). God himself will judge between many peoples; they will beat their swords into plowshares and their spears into pruning hooks, no nation will learn war any more, and every man will sit unafraid under his own vine and fig tree — a picture of covenant peace and security, "for the mouth of the LORD of Armies has spoken" (4:3–4). In that day God will gather the lame and the driven-away and make of them a remnant and a strong nation, and "the LORD will reign over them on Mount Zion from then on, even forever" (4:6–8). But the road there runs through pain: daughter Zion must writhe like a woman in labor, leave the city, and go "even to Babylon" — and there, Micah insists, "the LORD will redeem you from the hand of your enemies" (4:9–10). The many nations now gloating over Zion do not know the LORD's thoughts — he has gathered them "like the sheaves to the threshing floor," and the daughter of Zion will thresh them (4:11–13).
**Tags:** `nations-and-peoples` — the nations' pilgrimage to be taught by Israel's God: "Come! Let's go up to the mountain of the LORD," for "he will teach us of his ways, and we will walk in his paths" (4:1–3, with 4:5); `divine-judgment` — the nations assembled against Zion are gathered by the LORD "like the sheaves to the threshing floor" for judgment (4:11–13); `peace-among-nations` — the theme's defining vision: nations streaming to be taught — "Come! Let's go up to the mountain of the LORD" (4:2) — swords beaten into plowshares and spears into pruning hooks, war learned no more, and every man unafraid under his own vine and fig tree (4:3–4); `remnant` — the outcasts remade into the hope-bearing people: God gathers the lame and the driven-away and makes of them a remnant and a strong nation, and "the LORD will reign over them on Mount Zion from then on, even forever" (4:6–8)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings
**Sections:**
- **4:1–5 — The Mountain of the House of the LORD** (title: BSB) — In the latter days the mountain of the LORD's temple is raised above the hills; nations stream to it for teaching, weapons become farm tools, and everyone sits unafraid under vine and fig tree.
- **4:6–13 — The Restoration of Zion** (title: BSB) — God regathers the lame and outcast into a remnant he will reign over on Mount Zion — but first daughter Zion must labor like a woman in childbirth, go out to Babylon and be redeemed there, then rise to thresh the nations gathered against her.

## Micah 5
**Summary:** The chapter opens under siege, with Israel's judge struck on the cheek with a rod — and against that humiliation comes the book's most famous promise: "But you, Bethlehem Ephrathah, being small among the clans of Judah, out of you one will come out to me who is to be ruler in Israel; whose goings out are from of old, from ancient times" (5:1–2) — a ruler whose "goings out" (his origins) reach back into ancient days. After a time of abandonment "until the time that she who is in labor gives birth," this ruler "shall stand, and shall shepherd in the strength of the LORD," his people "will live," and "he will be great to the ends of the earth"; indeed, "He will be our peace" when Assyria invades (5:3–6). (Matthew's Gospel later cites 5:2 at Jesus' birth in Bethlehem, Matthew 2:5–6 — a later interpretive frame; within Micah the promise answers Assyria's threat with a coming shepherd-ruler.) The remnant of Jacob will live among many peoples as two things at once: dew from the LORD that waits for no man, and a lion that none can deliver from (5:7–9). Finally the LORD promises to strip his own people of everything they trust in place of him — horses and chariots, fortified cities, witchcraft and soothsayers, engraved images and Asherah poles — and to "execute vengeance in anger and wrath on the nations that didn't listen" (5:10–15).
**Tags:** `gods-protection` — the promised ruler shepherds "in the strength of the LORD" so that his people "will live," and "He will be our peace when Assyria invades our land"; "He will deliver us from the Assyrian" (5:4–6); `divine-judgment` — the purge of Israel's false securities ("I will cut off your horses … I will destroy witchcraft from your hand") and vengeance "on the nations that didn't listen" (5:10–15); `idolatry` — the purge of what Israel trusts in place of God: "I will destroy witchcraft from your hand" (5:12), with engraved images and Asherah poles cut off from among the people (5:13–14); `remnant` — a whole section on the remnant's future: the remnant of Jacob set among many peoples as dew from the LORD that waits for no man and as a lion none can deliver from (5:7–9), under the ruler who shepherds "in the strength of the LORD" (5:4)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings
**Sections:**
- **5:1–6 — A Ruler from Bethlehem** (title: BSB) — Out of besieged, humiliated Judah, God promises a ruler from little Bethlehem whose origins are from ancient times; he will shepherd in the LORD's strength and be his people's peace against Assyria.
- **5:7–15 — The Remnant of Jacob** (title: BSB) — The remnant becomes dew and lion among the nations, while the LORD strips Israel of its military hardware, sorceries, and idols, and takes vengeance on the nations that didn't listen.

## Micah 6
**Summary:** The LORD summons his people to court with the mountains for a jury: "the LORD has a case against his people, and he will contend with Israel" (6:1–2). His testimony is a history of rescue — "I brought you up out of the land of Egypt, and redeemed you out of the house of bondage. I sent before you Moses, Aaron, and Miriam" — and the foiling of Balak's scheme with Balaam, "that you may know the righteous acts of the LORD" (6:3–5). The worshiper's anxious counter-offers escalate from burnt offerings to thousands of rams to "Shall I give my firstborn for my disobedience?" — and are answered by the book's best-known sentence: "He has shown you, O man, what is good. What does the LORD require of you, but to act justly, to love mercy, and to walk humbly with your God?" (6:6–8). Then the LORD's voice turns to the city and finds none of the three: treasures of wickedness, a short ephah (a skimped measure), dishonest scales and deceitful weights, rich men full of violence, and tongues that speak lies (6:9–12). The sentence is futility — "You shall eat, but not be satisfied … You will sow, but won't reap" — because the city keeps "the statutes of Omri" and all the works of Ahab's house, walking "in their counsels" (6:13–16).
**Tags:** `humble-exaltation` — the required posture before God: "What does the LORD require of you, but to act justly, to love mercy, and to walk humbly with your God?" (6:8), set against the exalted God of 6:6; `honesty` — dishonest commerce and speech condemned by God himself: "Shall I tolerate dishonest scales, and a bag of deceitful weights? … her inhabitants speak lies" (6:10–12); `divine-judgment` — the court's sentence: "Therefore I also have struck you with a grievous wound. I have made you desolate because of your sins" (6:13–16); `justice-and-oppression` — the court finds none of what 6:8 requires: treasures of wickedness, a short ephah, "dishonest scales, and a bag of deceitful weights" (6:10–11), rich men full of violence (6:12) — with the standard stated in the chapter's own words: "to act justly, to love mercy, and to walk humbly with your God" (6:8); `conscience` — the good as shown, addressed generically: "He has shown you, O man, what is good. What does the LORD require of you, but to act justly, to love mercy, and to walk humbly with your God?" (6:8) — with the caveat that the courtroom scene addresses covenant Israel, who had special revelation; not overclaimed as a pure natural-law text
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings
**Sections:**
- **6:1–8 — The Case against Israel** (title: BSB) — The LORD's lawsuit before the mountains: he recites his saving acts from Egypt onward and answers the question of what to bring him with 6:8 — act justly, love mercy, walk humbly with your God.
- **6:9–16 — The Punishment of Israel** (title: BSB) — The LORD's voice to the city convicts its skimped measures, dishonest scales, violence, and lies, and sentences it to futility and reproach for walking in the ways of Omri and Ahab.

## Micah 7
**Summary:** Micah gives the book's last chapter to lament, and then to hope. "Misery is mine!" — the land is like a vineyard already gleaned, the godly man has perished, rulers and judges ask for bribes, and no neighbor, friend, or family member can be trusted, for "a man's enemies are the men of his own house" (7:1–6). The turn comes at 7:7: "But as for me, I will look to the LORD. I will wait for the God of my salvation. My God will hear me." Speaking as one who has sinned — the voice reads naturally as penitent Zion's — the speaker resolves to "bear the indignation of the LORD, because I have sinned against him, until he pleads my case," confident that "When I fall, I will arise. When I sit in darkness, the LORD will be a light to me," while the gloating enemy will be put to shame (7:8–13). The prophet prays that God will shepherd his people with his staff as in the days of old, and God answers: "As in the days of your coming out of the land of Egypt, I will show them marvelous things," while the nations come trembling to the LORD (7:14–17). The book ends in astonishment at pardon: "Who is a God like you, who pardons iniquity …? He doesn't retain his anger forever, because he delights in loving kindness … You will cast all their sins into the depths of the sea," keeping the mercy sworn to Abraham "from the days of old" (7:18–20).
**Tags:** `forgiveness-of-sins` — the book's closing wonder: "Who is a God like you, who pardons iniquity, and passes over the disobedience of the remnant of his heritage? … You will cast all their sins into the depths of the sea" (7:18–19); `gods-love` — "He doesn't retain his anger forever, because he delights in loving kindness. He will again have compassion on us" (7:18–19); `trust-in-god` — the lament's turning point: "But as for me, I will look to the LORD. I will wait for the God of my salvation. My God will hear me" (7:7); `repentance` — the penitent's confession and submission: "I will bear the indignation of the LORD, because I have sinned against him" (7:9); `gods-faithfulness` — "You will give truth to Jacob, and mercy to Abraham, as you have sworn to our fathers from the days of old" (7:20)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings
**Sections:**
- **7:1–6 — Israel's Great Misery** (title: BSB) — The prophet laments a society stripped bare: the godly gone, officials for sale, and trust dead even inside the household.
- **7:7–13 — Israel's Confession and Comfort** (title: BSB) — The voice turns to wait on the God of salvation, confessing sin and bearing the LORD's indignation in confidence that light and vindication will come, and that ruined walls will be rebuilt.
- **7:14–20 — God's Compassion on Israel** (title: BSB) — A prayer that God shepherd his people as in the days of old is answered with exodus-scale marvels, and the book closes praising the God who pardons iniquity and casts sins into the depths of the sea.
