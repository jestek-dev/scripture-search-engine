# Exodus — book summary (draft)

## Short

Exodus tells how the God of Abraham keeps his promise by force. Jacob's family has grown into a nation enslaved in Egypt, and when their cry comes up to God, he remembers his covenant and comes down to act — calling Moses from a burning bush, breaking Pharaoh's grip through ten plagues, sparing Israel's households behind the Passover lamb's blood, and opening the sea itself. But rescue is only half the book. At Mount Sinai the freed slaves are made a covenant people — "a kingdom of priests and a holy nation" — given the Ten Commandments and a pattern for a tent where God himself will live among them. Even the golden-calf disaster cannot end the story: Moses pleads, the LORD proclaims himself merciful and gracious, and the book closes with his glory filling the finished tabernacle in the middle of the camp.

## Full

Exodus means "the departure," and the departure from Egypt is the event the rest of Scripture keeps returning to. The book opens where Genesis closed — the sons of Israel in Egypt — but four hundred years have passed, seventy people have become a multitude, and a king who never knew Joseph has turned them into slaves and ordered their baby boys drowned. The first movement (chapters 1–15) is the fight for their release. God hears Israel's groaning, remembers his covenant, and calls Moses — a Hebrew raised in Pharaoh's own palace, then hidden away as a shepherd in Midian — to carry one demand to the throne: let my people go. Pharaoh answers, "Who is the LORD, that I should listen to his voice to let Israel go?" and the plagues are the LORD's reply, a mounting sequence of judgments on Egypt and its gods that spares the land of Goshen, until the night when death strikes every Egyptian firstborn but passes over every house marked with a lamb's blood. Israel marches out, the sea splits before them and closes over Pharaoh's army, and the movement ends in the Bible's first great song: "I will sing to the LORD, for he has triumphed gloriously."

The road from the sea to Sinai (chapters 15–18) tests the freed slaves and shows them who now provides for them. Three days from the miracle they are thirsty and complaining; then hungry; then thirsty again. Each time God answers rather than abandons: bitter water made sweet, bread from the sky every morning with a double portion before each Sabbath, water struck from a rock, victory over the raiders of Amalek while Moses' hands are held up in dependence. The section closes with Moses' Midianite father-in-law confessing that the LORD is greater than all gods — and teaching Moses to share the load of leading.

At Sinai (chapters 19–24) the rescue gets its purpose. God has not freed Israel merely from slavery but for himself: "I bore you on eagles' wings, and brought you to myself." He offers a covenant — obey my voice and you will be my own possession, a kingdom of priests and a holy nation — and the people consent. The mountain burns and quakes as God speaks the Ten Commandments, then gives a body of case law shaping justice, mercy, and worship for the new nation, and the covenant is sealed in blood and a meal in God's presence.

The last movement (chapters 25–40) answers the question the whole rescue has been building toward: can a holy God actually live among the people he redeemed? God gives Moses a pattern for a sanctuary — "Let them make me a sanctuary, that I may dwell among them" — with its ark, lampstand, altars, and priesthood. While Moses is still on the mountain, Israel breaks the covenant at its first commandment by worshiping a golden calf, and only Moses' intercession stands between the nation and its end. The LORD's answer to that crisis is the book's theological summit: he proclaims his own name — merciful and gracious, slow to anger, abundant in loving kindness and truth, forgiving iniquity yet by no means clearing the guilty — and renews the covenant. The people then build the tabernacle exactly as commanded, and Exodus closes with the cloud covering the Tent of Meeting and the LORD's glory filling it: the God who brought Israel out now travels with them, in fire and cloud, in the sight of all the house of Israel.

# The forty chapters

## Exodus 1
**Summary:** Genesis's seventy travelers have become a people so numerous that Egypt is afraid of them. A new king who never knew Joseph enslaves Israel with brutal forced labor — yet the more they are oppressed, the more they multiply. Pharaoh escalates to murder, ordering the Hebrew midwives to kill every newborn boy; but the midwives fear God more than the king, refuse, and are given families of their own. So Pharaoh commands his whole people to throw every Hebrew son into the river — the crisis into which the next chapter's baby will be born.
**Tags:** `providence` — every scheme against Israel is quietly overturned: "the more they afflicted them, the more they multiplied and the more they spread out" (1:12), and "God dealt well with the midwives, and the people multiplied" (1:20); `pleasing-god-not-people` — "the midwives feared God, and didn't do what the king of Egypt commanded them" (1:17), and "because the midwives feared God, he gave them families" (1:21); `justice-and-oppression` — national enslavement: taskmasters, brutal forced labor, and lives made bitter in bondage — the oppression the rest of the book answers (1:11–14)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings
**Motifs (candidates):** fearing God above rulers (1:15–21) — "civil disobedience in the Bible", "Hebrew midwives", "obeying God rather than man in the Old Testament".

## Exodus 2
**Summary:** Under the drowning decree a Levite couple hides their baby three months, then sets him afloat in a papyrus basket among the reeds — where Pharaoh's own daughter finds him, pities him, unknowingly pays his mother to nurse him, and raises him as her son, Moses. Grown, Moses kills an Egyptian he finds beating a Hebrew, is rejected by his own people, and flees Pharaoh to Midian, where he defends a priest's daughters at a well, marries Zipporah, and names his son Gershom — "I have lived as a foreigner in a foreign land." The chapter ends with the hinge of the whole book: Israel's cry under bondage comes up to God, and God hears, remembers his covenant with Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, sees, and understands.
**Tags:** `gods-faithfulness` — "God heard their groaning, and God remembered his covenant with Abraham, with Isaac, and with Jacob," and "God saw the children of Israel, and God understood" (2:23–25); `providence` — the deliverer is saved from the river by Pharaoh's own daughter, and his mother is paid to nurse him (2:5–10); `justice-and-oppression` — the hinge of the book: Israel's cry under national bondage rises to God, who hears the groaning, remembers his covenant, sees, and understands (2:23–25); `sojourners-and-strangers` — Moses names his son Gershom, "I have lived as a foreigner in a foreign land" — the deliverer himself living as a stranger in Midian (2:15–22)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings
**Sections:**

## Exodus 3
**Summary:** Keeping his father-in-law's flock at Horeb, God's mountain, Moses turns aside to a bush that burns without burning up, and God calls him by name from the middle of it — warning him to take off his sandals, "for the place you are standing on is holy ground." The LORD declares that he has surely seen his people's affliction, heard their cry, and come down to deliver them and bring them to a land flowing with milk and honey — and that Moses is the one he is sending to Pharaoh. He foretells the whole road ahead: Pharaoh's refusal, God's wonders, and an exodus in which Israel will not leave empty-handed.

## Exodus 4
**Summary:** Moses raises objection after objection — they won't believe me; I am slow of speech; please send someone else. God answers each: the rod (the WEB's word for the familiar "staff") that becomes a snake and the hand made leprous and healed are given as signs, "Who made man's mouth?" answers the slow tongue, and Aaron is appointed as his brother's spokesman — though Moses' final refusal kindles the LORD's anger. Moses returns toward Egypt with his family and God's rod; at a lodging place on the way the LORD confronts him with death, a danger that passes only when Zipporah circumcises their son. In Egypt, Aaron speaks and performs the signs, and the people believe: hearing that the LORD has seen their affliction, they bow their heads and worship.
**Tags:** `doubt` — "they will not believe me, nor listen to my voice" (4:1) and "Oh, Lord, please send someone else" (4:13): Moses meets God's call with objections; `pastoral-strength-in-weakness` — to "I am slow of speech, and of a slow tongue" God answers, "Who made man's mouth?... ## Exodus 5

## Exodus 6
**Summary:** God answers Moses' complaint with a declaration of his name and his intent: he appeared to the fathers as God Almighty, but now, remembering his covenant, he will act as what his name the LORD means. The promise runs in a chain of "I will"s — I will bring you out from under the burdens of the Egyptians, rid you out of their bondage, redeem you with an outstretched arm, take you to myself for a people, be your God, and bring you into the land I swore to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. But Israel cannot hear it "for anguish of spirit, and for cruel bondage," and Moses doubts that Pharaoh will listen when his own people don't. A genealogy then interrupts the narrative to anchor Moses and Aaron in Israel's family records before the confrontation resumes.
**Tags:** `covenant` — "I have also established my covenant with them, to give them the land of Canaan... **Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings

## Exodus 7

Then the first plague falls: the Nile — struck with the rod in Pharaoh's sight — turns to blood, its fish die, and Egypt digs for water for seven days, while Pharaoh turns into his house and does not take even this to heart.

## Exodus 8
**Summary:** Three plagues tighten the vise. Frogs swarm out of the river into houses, beds, and ovens until Pharaoh begs Moses to entreat the LORD; Moses prays, the frogs die on Pharaoh's chosen timetable "that you may know that there is no one like the LORD our God" — and Pharaoh, seeing respite, hardens his heart. Lice (the WEB's word for the third plague, where many Bibles read "gnats") rise from the very dust, and the magicians, unable to copy it, confess "This is God's finger." Flies then corrupt the whole land except Goshen, which God sets apart for his people; Pharaoh bargains — sacrifice here, or don't go far — and breaks his word the moment the plague lifts.
**Tags:** `prayer` — Pharaoh twice begs for intercession — "Entreat the LORD" (8:8), "Pray for me" (8:28) — and Moses' intercession is answered to the letter: "The LORD did according to the word of Moses" (8:12–13, 30–31); `divine-judgment` — frogs, lice, and flies fall as escalating judgments, until the magicians concede "This is God's finger" (8:6, 17, 19, 24); `gods-protection` — "I will set apart in that day the land of Goshen, in which my people dwell... **Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings
**Motifs (candidates):** half-way bargains with God (8:25–29) — "partial obedience", "bargaining with God"; relief that undoes repentance (8:15) — "going back to old ways when the pressure is off".

## Exodus 9
**Summary:** The plagues turn deadly. A pestilence kills Egypt's livestock in the field while not one animal of Israel's dies — Pharaoh checks, finds it so, and stays stubborn. Boils break out on man and animal from furnace ashes tossed skyward, driving even the magicians from the field, and this time the text says the LORD hardened Pharaoh's heart. Before the seventh plague God explains why Pharaoh still stands at all — "to show you my power, and that my name may be declared throughout all the earth" — and even offers Egypt shelter from what is coming; then hail unlike anything in Egypt's history shatters the land, sparing only Goshen. Pharaoh confesses "I have sinned this time. The LORD is righteous," but when the thunder stops, he sins yet more and hardens his heart again.

## Exodus 10
**Summary:** God tells Moses he has hardened Pharaoh's heart so that these signs will be told to sons and grandsons — "that you may know that I am the LORD" — and sends the question that names Pharaoh's real sickness: "How long will you refuse to humble yourself before me?" Pharaoh's last bargain (go, but leave the livestock) fails against Moses' "not a hoof shall be left behind," and the king banishes Moses on pain of death.

## Exodus 12
**Summary:** On the night judgment falls, God institutes the meal that will define Israel's memory forever. Each household takes a year-old lamb without defect, kills it at evening, puts its blood on the doorposts and lintel, and eats it roasted with unleavened bread and bitter herbs, dressed to travel: "it is the LORD's Passover." At midnight the LORD strikes every Egyptian firstborn — "there was not a house where there was not one dead" — but passes over every blood-marked house, and Pharaoh drives Israel out by night. About six hundred thousand men besides children march from Rameses with a mixed multitude, ending four hundred thirty years in Egypt to the day; and the chapter closes with the Passover's permanent ordinance, open to any foreigner who joins Israel by circumcision — one law for the native and the stranger.

## Exodus 13
