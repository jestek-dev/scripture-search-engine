# Ecclesiastes — book summary (draft)

## Short

Ecclesiastes is the Bible's unflinching audit of life "under the sun." Its speaker, "the Preacher, the son of David, king in Jerusalem," sets out to test by wisdom everything a human being can chase — pleasure, building projects, wealth, work, even wisdom itself — and files the same report on all of it: "Vanity of vanities, all is vanity" — vanity in the old sense of a vapor, something fleeting and impossible to grasp, not self-admiration. Death levels the wise man and the fool; injustice sits in the courts; no one can see what God is doing from beginning to end. Yet running through the bleak ledger is a quieter refrain the Preacher keeps reaching: eat your bread, drink your wine, enjoy your work and the wife you love — received as gifts from God's hand, not prizes wrung from the world. The book refuses to resolve its own tension early; only its last words come to rest: "Fear God and keep his commandments; for this is the whole duty of man. For God will bring every work into judgment."

## Full

Ecclesiastes names itself by its speaker: "The words of the Preacher, the son of David, king in Jerusalem." The book opens and closes in another voice that speaks *about* the Preacher — introducing his verdict in the first verses and, at the very end, describing him as a wise teacher who "pondered, sought out, and set in order many proverbs" — while nearly everything between is the Preacher's own first-person report — a single third-person tag ("says the Preacher," 7:27) surfaces once mid-book. His verdict brackets the whole: "Vanity of vanities, all is vanity." The word is the old one for a vapor or breath — the point is not that everything is worthless, but that everything under the sun is fleeting, slippery, and finally beyond human control, "a chasing after wind."

The report begins as an experiment only a king could run (chapters 1–2). With unmatched resources and his wisdom still guiding him, the Preacher tries everything: laughter and wine, houses, vineyards, gardens, pools, servants, silver and gold, singers — "Whatever my eyes desired, I didn't keep from them." The result is the book's first great deflation: looking over all his hands had done, he finds "no profit under the sun." Wisdom is genuinely better than folly, "as far as light excels darkness" — and yet the same death comes for the wise man and the fool, and everything built must be left to a successor who may be either. Out of that dead end comes the book's surprising counter-theme: the best available good is to eat, to drink, and to find enjoyment in one's work — and even this, the Preacher insists, is "from the hand of God."

The middle chapters (3–10) widen the audit from one king's experiment to everything the Preacher has "seen under the sun." The famous poem of chapter 3 — "For everything there is a season, and a time for every purpose under heaven" — sets the frame: God "has made everything beautiful in its time" and "set eternity in their hearts," yet no one can find out his work from beginning to end. Under that veiled sovereignty the Preacher looks hard at what actually happens: oppression with no comforter, toil driven by envy, the aching solitude of a man with no one to share his gain, wealth that cannot satisfy the one who loves it, riches lost before they can be enjoyed, the righteous perishing while the wicked live long, justice delayed until hearts grow bold in evil, and death — the "one event" — coming to righteous and wicked alike. He does not sand the edges off any of it, and he does not pretend to a formula: "the race is not to the swift, nor the battle to the strong... time and chance happen to them all." But neither does he let go of God: "God will judge the righteous and the wicked"; "it will be better with those who fear God"; and the repeated counsel still stands — take your portion with joy, for God is the giver.

The closing movement (11–12) turns the audit into counsel. Since you cannot know what God is doing or which venture will prosper, be openhanded and be diligent — "Cast your bread on the waters," give portions "to seven, yes, even to eight," sow morning and evening. Rejoice in every year you are given, and start young: "Remember also your Creator in the days of your youth," before old age arrives — pictured in the book's haunting allegory of a great house failing, keepers trembling, grinders ceasing, windows darkened — and "the dust returns to the earth as it was, and the spirit returns to God who gave it." The vanity refrain sounds one last time, and then the closing voice delivers the conclusion the whole search has earned: "Fear God and keep his commandments; for this is the whole duty of man. For God will bring every work into judgment, with every hidden thing, whether it is good, or whether it is evil."

# The twelve chapters

## Ecclesiastes 1
**Summary:** The book opens with its speaker — "the Preacher, the son of David, king in Jerusalem" — and his verdict over everything: "Vanity of vanities, all is vanity." "What does man gain from all his labor in which he labors under the sun?" Generations come and go while the sun circles, the wind turns, and rivers pour into a sea that is never full; everything is wearisome, and "there is no new thing under the sun." The Preacher then introduces himself and his project: as king he made a study, guided by wisdom, of everything done under the sky, and found it "a chasing after wind" — even wisdom itself carries a sting, "for in much wisdom is much grief; and he who increases knowledge increases sorrow."
**Tags:** `vanity-of-life` — the book's thesis pronounced over everything: "Vanity of vanities, all is vanity" (1:2), "there is no new thing under the sun" (1:9), and the king's own study of all done under the sky found "a chasing after wind" (1:14). (This chapter's Tags line read "none" before 2026-08-25 — the vanity/futility theme was then a logged vocabulary gap; the concept adopted 2026-08-25 now carries it.)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings

## Ecclesiastes 2
**Summary:** The Preacher runs the experiment in full: he tests pleasure — laughter, wine, great works, houses, vineyards, gardens, pools, servants, herds, silver and gold, singers — withholding nothing his eyes desired, and looking back finds "all was vanity and a chasing after wind, and there was no profit under the sun." Wisdom, he grants, "excels folly, as far as light excels darkness" — yet "one event happens to them all," and "the wise man must die just like the fool" — with no lasting memory of either. So he hates his labor, since everything he built must be left to a successor who may be wise or a fool. The chapter turns at its close: "There is nothing better for a man than that he should eat and drink, and make his soul enjoy good in his labor" — a possibility that comes "from the hand of God," who "gives wisdom, knowledge, and joy" to the one who pleases him.
**Tags:** `wisdom-from-god` — wisdom named as God's gift, not an achievement: "to the man who pleases him, God gives wisdom, knowledge, and joy" (2:26); `vanity-of-life` — the experiment's verdict over all the king's works: "all was vanity and a chasing after wind, and there was no profit under the sun" (2:11), with the hatred of labor that follows from it (2:17–18); `enjoying-gods-gifts` — the chapter's turn out of the dead end: "There is nothing better for a man than that he should eat and drink, and make his soul enjoy good in his labor," a possibility that comes "from the hand of God" (2:24–26). (The first tag was this chapter's only honest tag from the pre-2026-08-25 vocabulary; the vanity and enjoyment themes, logged then as vocabulary gaps, are carried by the concepts adopted 2026-08-25.)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings

## Ecclesiastes 3
**Summary:** The chapter opens with the Bible's best-known poem on time: "For everything there is a season, and a time for every purpose under heaven" — "a time to be born, and a time to die," times to weep and to laugh, to keep silence and to speak, for war and for peace. Behind the seasons stands God: "He has made everything beautiful in its time. He has also set eternity in their hearts," yet no one can find out his work from beginning to end, and whatever God does lasts forever, "that men should fear before him." Then the Preacher reports what he sees under the sun: wickedness sitting in the very place of justice — answered by his conviction that "God will judge the righteous and the wicked" — and death making no visible distinction between man and animal: "they have all one breath," and "all are from the dust, and all turn to dust again," while "Who knows the spirit of man, whether it goes upward?" remains, from where he stands, a question. So he returns to his refrain — "there is nothing better than that a man should rejoice in his works, for that is his portion."
**Tags:** `providence` — God's sovereign ordering of every time and season: "For everything there is a season," "He has made everything beautiful in its time," and "whatever God does, it shall be forever" (3:1–8, 3:11, 3:14); `divine-judgment` — the Preacher's answer to courtroom wickedness: "God will judge the righteous and the wicked; for there is a time there for every purpose and for every work" (3:16–17); `seasons-of-life` — the poem itself, every purpose given its appointed time: "For everything there is a season, and a time for every purpose under heaven" (3:1–8); `enjoying-gods-gifts` — the refrain in its chapter-3 form: that "every man should eat and drink, and enjoy good in all his labor, is the gift of God" (3:13), and "there is nothing better than that a man should rejoice in his works, for that is his portion" (3:22); `mortality` — death's visible universality: "they have all one breath," and "All go to one place. All are from the dust, and all turn to dust again" (3:19–21); `conscience` — the implanted inner witness, an inward pointer toward what outlasts us rather than a full conscience doctrine: "He has also set eternity in their hearts," paired honestly with the verse's own limit — no one can find out God's work from beginning to end (3:11)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings
**Sections:**
- **3:1–8 — To Everything There Is a Season** (title: BSB) — Fourteen matched pairs — birth and death, planting and plucking up, mourning and dancing, war and peace — declare that every purpose under heaven has its appointed time.
- **3:9–15 — God's Works Remain Forever** (title: BSB) — God "has made everything beautiful in its time" and "set eternity in their hearts," yet keeps his work beyond human finding out; the gift within the mystery is to rejoice, do good, and enjoy the fruit of labor, fearing the God whose work stands forever.
- **3:16–22 — From Dust to Dust** (title: BSB) — Wickedness in the place of justice, and one breath shared by man and animal: the Preacher holds that God will judge, while conceding how little can be seen from under the sun — all go to one place, and man's portion is to rejoice in his works.

## Ecclesiastes 4
**Summary:** The Preacher looks again and sees "the tears of those who were oppressed, and they had no comforter" — power on the oppressors' side, and no one to comfort — until he praises the dead over the living, and counts the one "who has not yet been" better off than both. He sees toil, too, spring from a bitter root — "all the labor and achievement that is the envy of a man's neighbor" — and sets against it a small, sane measure: "Better is a handful, with quietness, than two handfuls with labor and chasing after wind." He sees a man utterly alone, without son or brother, laboring without end and asking too late, "For whom then do I labor and deprive my soul of enjoyment?" — which turns him to the goodness of companionship: "Two are better than one," for they lift each other when they fall, keep each other warm, stand together against attack, "and a threefold cord is not quickly broken." A closing sketch tracks a poor and wise youth who rises from prison to a throne — and is forgotten by the generation that follows.
**Tags:** `friendship` — the classic companionship text: "Two are better than one... For if they fall, the one will lift up his fellow," ending "a threefold cord is not quickly broken" (4:9–12); `loneliness` — the man with "neither son nor brother," laboring alone, and the verdict "woe to him who is alone when he falls, and doesn't have another to lift him up" (4:8–12); `contentment` — "Better is a handful, with quietness, than two handfuls with labor and chasing after wind" (4:6); `envy-and-jealousy` — toil diagnosed as driven by "the envy of a man's neighbor," and named vanity (4:4); `justice-and-oppression` — the observer's register, oppression seen and grieved: "the tears of those who were oppressed, and they had no comforter," with power on the oppressors' side (4:1–3)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings

## Ecclesiastes 5
**Summary:** The Preacher turns to worship: "Guard your steps when you go to God's house; for to draw near to listen is better than to give the sacrifice of fools." Words before God should be few and unhurried — "for God is in heaven, and you on earth" — and a vow once made must be paid: "When you vow a vow to God, don't defer to pay it," for "It is better that you should not vow, than that you should vow and not pay"; "you must fear God." Then wealth: bureaucracies of officials watch the poor being oppressed; "He who loves silver shall not be satisfied with silver"; the laborer sleeps sweetly while the rich man's abundance keeps him awake; and the Preacher has seen "wealth kept by its owner to his harm" — lost in one misfortune, so that a man leaves the world as naked as he entered it. The good he has seen is this — "to eat and to drink, and to enjoy good in all his labor" through the days of the life God gives — and when God gives wealth *with* the power to enjoy it, "this is the gift of God," and such a man is kept busy with the joy of his heart.
**Tags:** `worship` — approaching God's house with reverence: "Guard your steps when you go to God's house; for to draw near to listen is better than to give the sacrifice of fools" (5:1–2, 5:7); `contentment` — the diagnosis of money-love and its cure in the enjoyed portion: "He who loves silver shall not be satisfied with silver" (5:10–12), "to eat and to drink, and to enjoy good... for this is his portion" (5:18–19); `taming-the-tongue` — restraint of speech before God: "Don't be rash with your mouth... Therefore let your words be few," and "Don't allow your mouth to lead you into sin" (5:2–3, 5:6–7); `oaths-and-vows` — the Bible's most direct vow-keeping teaching: "When you vow a vow to God, don't defer to pay it," for "It is better that you should not vow, than that you should vow and not pay" (5:4–6); `money-and-possessions` — the danger-of-riches register: "He who loves silver shall not be satisfied with silver" (5:10–12), and "wealth kept by its owner to his harm," lost in one misfortune (5:13–17); `enjoying-gods-gifts` — enjoyment received as gift, never wrung from the world: "to eat and to drink, and to enjoy good in all his labor" through the days God gives, and when God adds the power to enjoy wealth, "this is the gift of God" (5:18–20)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings
**Sections:**
- **5:1–7 — Approaching God with Awe** (title: BSB) — Reverence in God's house: draw near to listen rather than offer a fool's sacrifice, keep words few before the God of heaven, and pay every vow — "you must fear God."
- **5:8–20 — The Futility of Wealth** (title: BSB) — Oppression watched by layers of officials, silver that never satisfies its lover, sweet sleep for the laborer and none for the rich, hoarded wealth lost to misfortune — against which stands the good the Preacher has seen: enjoying one's portion as the gift of God.

## Ecclesiastes 6
**Summary:** The Preacher names an evil that lies heavy on humanity: God gives a man "riches, wealth, and honor" — everything his soul desires — "yet God gives him no power to eat of it, but an alien eats it." A man might father a hundred children and live long, but if his soul is never filled with good, the Preacher's verdict is stark: "a stillborn child is better than he," for the stillborn at least has rest. All human labor is for the mouth, "and yet the appetite is not filled"; "Better is the sight of the eyes than the wandering of the desire" — and this too, the Preacher says, is vanity and a chasing after wind. The chapter closes the book's first half at the limit it keeps hitting — no one can "contend with him who is mightier than he" — and on its open question: "who knows what is good for man in life, all the days of his vain life which he spends like a shadow?"
**Tags:** `money-and-possessions` — the chapter's stated evil, riches without the power to enjoy them: God gives a man "riches, wealth, and honor," "yet God gives him no power to eat of it, but an alien eats it" (6:1–2), while all labor is for the mouth "and yet the appetite is not filled" (6:7). (This chapter's Tags line read "none" before 2026-08-25 — the unenjoyed-wealth theme then fed the money gap row; the concept adopted 2026-08-25 now carries it.)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings

## Ecclesiastes 7
**Summary:** A chain of "better than" proverbs opens the chapter: "A good name is better than fine perfume; and the day of death better than the day of one's birth"; the house of mourning beats the house of feasting, "for that is the end of all men, and the living should take this to heart"; the rebuke of the wise beats the song of fools, and patience beats pride: "The patient in spirit is better than the proud in spirit." Don't be hasty to anger, the Preacher adds, and don't ask why the former days were better. Wisdom shelters like an inheritance, and God's crooked work cannot be straightened: "In the day of prosperity be joyful, and in the day of adversity consider; yes, God has made the one side by side with the other." The Preacher has seen it in his own days of vanity — "there is a righteous man who perishes in his righteousness, and there is a wicked man who lives long in his evildoing" — and counsels against being "overly righteous" or ruinously wicked — "he who fears God will come out of them all" — adding the book's plainest word on human nature: "Surely there is not a righteous man on earth who does good and doesn't sin." His own search for wisdom's depths ends in failure and one bitter finding — the ensnaring woman "whose heart is snares and traps," a tally of "one man among a thousand" and no woman among them — before the chapter's real conclusion: "God made mankind upright; but they search for many inventions."
**Tags:** `sin` — universal sinfulness stated flat out: "Surely there is not a righteous man on earth who does good and doesn't sin" (7:20), with the diagnosis "God made mankind upright; but they search for many inventions" (7:29); `prosperity-of-the-wicked` — the observed inversion stated in the Preacher's own days of vanity: "there is a righteous man who perishes in his righteousness, and there is a wicked man who lives long in his evildoing" (7:15); `suffering-of-the-righteous` — the same observed verse read from the sufferer's side: the Preacher's data point against any tidy righteousness-equals-outcomes scheme, "there is a righteous man who perishes in his righteousness" (7:15). ## Ecclesiastes 8
**Summary:** Wisdom brightens a man's face, and the Preacher counsels keeping the king's command "because of the oath to God" — "the king's word is supreme," and no one can demand "What are you doing?" of him. Yet every human power has limits: no one "has power over the spirit to contain the spirit; neither does he have power over the day of death." Justice delays, and the delay corrupts — "Because sentence against an evil work is not executed speedily, therefore the heart of the sons of men is fully set in them to do evil" — but the Preacher holds his ground: "surely I know that it will be better with those who fear God... But it shall not be well with the wicked." Still he refuses to pretend the ledger balances now: the righteous sometimes receive what the wicked deserve and the wicked what the righteous deserve, so he commends joy — eating, drinking, gladness in labor — and ends at the limit of wisdom itself: however much even a wise man labors to comprehend God's work under the sun, "he won't be able to find it."
**Tags:** `divine-judgment` — judgment delayed but certain, affirmed by the Preacher himself: "sentence against an evil work is not executed speedily," yet "it will be better with those who fear God... it shall not be well with the wicked" (8:11–13); `fear-of-the-lord` — the Preacher's held ground amid the imbalance: "surely I know that it will be better with those who fear God... But it shall not be well with the wicked" (8:12–13); `prosperity-of-the-wicked` — the unbalanced ledger seen and not sanded off: "there are righteous men to whom it happens according to the work of the wicked," and the wicked receiving what the righteous deserve (8:10–14). ## Ecclesiastes 9
**Summary:** The Preacher lays it all to heart: the righteous and the wise and their works "are in the hand of God," yet under the sun "there is one event to the righteous and to the wicked" — death comes to all alike, and from where the living stand, "the dead don't know anything," their love and hatred and envy long perished, with no more portion "in anything that is done under the sun"; that is why "a living dog is better than a dead lion." The conclusion is not paralysis but urgency: "eat your bread with joy, and drink your wine with a merry heart; for God has already accepted your works"; keep your garments white, "live joyfully with the wife whom you love," and "Whatever your hand finds to do, do it with your might; for there is no work, nor plan, nor knowledge, nor wisdom, in Sheol, where you are going." Nor does effort guarantee outcome — "the race is not to the swift, nor the battle to the strong... time and chance happen to them all," and disaster falls suddenly, like fish taken in a net. A closing story drives home wisdom's strange lot: a poor wise man delivers a little besieged city and is forgotten — "Wisdom is better than strength," yet the poor man's wisdom is despised, while "one sinner destroys much good."
**Tags:** `work-and-diligence` — wholehearted work while there is time to work: "Whatever your hand finds to do, do it with your might; for there is no work, nor plan, nor knowledge, nor wisdom, in Sheol, where you are going" (9:10); `mortality` — the "one event" laid to heart: "there is one event to the righteous and to the wicked," and from where the living stand "the dead don't know anything," with no more portion under the sun (9:2–6, 9:10); `enjoying-gods-gifts` — mortality's conclusion is urgency, not paralysis: "eat your bread with joy, and drink your wine with a merry heart; for God has already accepted your works," and "live joyfully with the wife whom you love" (9:7–9). ## Ecclesiastes 10
**Summary:** "Dead flies cause the oil of the perfumer to produce an evil odor; so does a little folly outweigh wisdom and honor" — and the chapter strings proverbs on that theme. The fool advertises himself wherever he walks; a ruler's error seats folly in high places, so that "servants on horses" pass "princes walking like servants"; every trade has its hazards — the pit-digger falls in, the wood-splitter is endangered — and "If the ax is blunt, and one doesn't sharpen the edge, then he must use more strength; but skill brings success." Speech divides the wise from the foolish: "The words of a wise man's mouth are gracious; but a fool is swallowed by his own lips," multiplying words though no one knows what will come. The closing proverbs turn political and practical: "Woe to you, land, when your king is a child"; "By slothfulness the roof sinks in; and through idleness of the hands the house leaks"; and don't curse the king even in your thoughts, "for a bird of the sky may carry your voice."
**Tags:** `taming-the-tongue` — speech as the fool's undoing and the wise man's grace: "The words of a wise man's mouth are gracious; but a fool is swallowed by his own lips" (10:12–14), with the closing caution against even secret curses (10:20); `work-and-diligence` — diligence taught by its opposite: "By slothfulness the roof sinks in; and through idleness of the hands the house leaks" (10:18), and "If the ax is blunt... skill brings success" (10:10)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings

## Ecclesiastes 11
**Summary:** Since no one knows what God is doing, the Preacher counsels openhanded venture rather than paralysis: "Cast your bread on the waters; for you shall find it after many days," and "Give a portion to seven, yes, even to eight; for you don't know what evil will be on the earth." Waiting for perfect conditions is its own failure — "He who observes the wind won't sow; and he who regards the clouds won't reap" — and just as you cannot trace the wind's way or how bones grow in the womb, "even so you don't know the work of God who does all"; so sow in the morning and don't withhold your hand in the evening, since either sowing may prosper. Light is sweet, and "if a man lives many years, let him rejoice in them all" — while remembering "the days of darkness, for they shall be many." The chapter closes turning to the young: "Rejoice, young man, in your youth... but know that for all these things God will bring you into judgment," for youth and the dawn of life are as fleeting as everything else.
**Tags:** `generosity` — openhanded giving in the face of an unknown future: "Give a portion to seven, yes, even to eight; for you don't know what evil will be on the earth" (11:1–2); `work-and-diligence` — sow despite uncertainty: "He who observes the wind won't sow...
