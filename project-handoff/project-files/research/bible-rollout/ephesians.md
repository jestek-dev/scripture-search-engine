# Ephesians — book summary (draft)

## Short

Ephesians is Paul's letter about what the church is, written while he was a prisoner, and it splits cleanly in two. The first half (chapters 1–3) is all gift: before the foundation of the world God chose believers and predestined them for adoption, made them alive when they were dead in sins — "for by grace you have been saved through faith… not of works" — and out of Jew and Gentile created "one new man," one body and one growing temple with Christ as head and chief cornerstone. The second half (chapters 4–6) begins at "therefore": walk worthily of that calling — in unity and Spirit-given gifts, in a new self instead of the old, in love, light, and wisdom, in households reshaped by Christ, and finally standing in the whole armor of God, because the real struggle is not against flesh and blood. Grace first, walk second: that order is the letter's whole shape.

## Full

Ephesians opens not with a problem to fix but with praise. Its first long sentence blesses "the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, who has blessed us with every spiritual blessing in the heavenly places in Christ" (1:3), and the phrase "in the heavenly places" becomes a thread that runs the whole letter — where believers are blessed (1:3), where Christ is enthroned (1:20), where believers are seated with him (2:6), where God's wisdom is displayed (3:10), and where the spiritual forces of wickedness still operate (6:12). Paul writes as "the prisoner of Christ Jesus on behalf of you Gentiles" (3:1), to "the saints who are at Ephesus" (1:1), and his stated horizon is as wide as it gets: God's plan "to sum up all things in Christ, the things in the heavens and the things on the earth" (1:10).

Chapters 1–3 tell what God has done. Chapter 1 traces it from eternity — chosen "before the foundation of the world," "predestined… for adoption as children," redeemed through Christ's blood, "sealed with the promised Holy Spirit" — and then prays that the readers would actually know the hope, riches, and power that are theirs, a power measured by God raising Christ from the dead and seating him "far above all rule, authority, power, dominion" as "head over all things for the assembly, which is his body" (an assembly is the WEB's word for a church). Chapter 2 states the rescue in the sharpest terms in Paul's letters: dead in transgressions, "by nature children of wrath," then "But God, being rich in mercy," made us alive together with Christ — "for by grace you have been saved through faith, and that not of yourselves; it is the gift of God, not of works, that no one would boast" (2:8–9). The same grace ends an ancient hostility: Christ "broke down the middle wall of separation" between Jew and Gentile to "create in himself one new man of the two, making peace," building both into one household and holy temple on the chief cornerstone. Chapter 3 names this Jew-and-Gentile inclusion "the mystery of Christ," hidden for ages and now revealed, entrusted to Paul to preach as "the unsearchable riches of Christ" — and then bows the knee in a second prayer, that Christ would dwell in their hearts and that they would "know Christ's love which surpasses knowledge."

Chapters 4–6 turn the gift into a walk — the word "walk" paces the whole second half. "I therefore, the prisoner in the Lord, beg you to walk worthily of the calling with which you were called" (4:1). The walk is first corporate: humility and patience keeping "the unity of the Spirit in the bond of peace," one body served by differing gifts of the ascended Christ until everyone grows up into the head. It is then personal: the old man put away, the new man put on, worked out in truth-telling, honest labor, clean and building speech, and kindness that forgives "just as God also in Christ forgave you." Chapter 5 continues: walk in love on the pattern of Christ's self-giving; walk as children of light, having once been darkness; walk carefully, "redeeming the time," filled with the Spirit, overflowing in song and thanksgiving. Then the gospel pattern enters the Roman household. Wives are addressed first, called to be subject to their own husbands "as to the Lord"; husbands are given the heavier sentence — "love your wives, even as Christ also loved the assembly and gave himself up for her" — and the "one flesh" of Genesis is declared a great mystery — "I speak concerning Christ and the assembly." Children and fathers, servants and masters each receive their own charge, and in every pair the text binds both sides: fathers must not provoke, masters must "give up threatening," because heaven's Master shows "no partiality."

The letter ends on its feet. "Finally, be strong in the Lord": the enemy is not human — "our wrestling is not against flesh and blood" — so the believer's equipment is God's own armor, named piece by piece from truth to righteousness to the Good News (the WEB's term for the gospel) of peace, faith, salvation, and "the sword of the Spirit, which is the word of God," all sustained by "praying at all times in the Spirit." Paul, "an ambassador in chains," asks prayer for boldness, sends Tychicus with his news, and closes the way the letter opened — with peace, love, faith, and grace "with all those who love our Lord Jesus Christ with incorruptible love."

# The six chapters

## Ephesians 1
**Summary:** Paul opens with one long outpouring of praise: God "has blessed us with every spiritual blessing in the heavenly places in Christ" — believers chosen "before the foundation of the world," predestined for adoption as children, redeemed and forgiven through Christ's blood, and let in on the mystery of God's plan "to sum up all things in Christ." Jewish believers "who had before hoped in Christ" and Gentile readers who later heard and believed are both included, and all who believed were "sealed with the promised Holy Spirit," the pledge of their inheritance. Paul then turns the blessing into a prayer: that God would give "a spirit of wisdom and revelation" to know the hope of his calling, "the riches of the glory of his inheritance in the saints," and "the exceeding greatness of his power toward us who believe." That power is measured by what it has already done — raising Christ from the dead and seating him "far above all rule, authority, power, dominion, and every name that is named," with all things under his feet, "head over all things for the assembly, which is his body."

## Ephesians 2
**Summary:** Paul reminds his readers what they were: dead in transgressions and sins, walking "according to the course of this world," under "the prince of the power of the air," and "by nature children of wrath, even as the rest." Then comes the turn — "But God, being rich in mercy, for his great love with which he loved us," made the dead alive together with Christ, raised them up, and seated them with him in the heavenly places. The summary is the letter's most famous sentence: "for by grace you have been saved through faith, and that not of yourselves; it is the gift of God, not of works, that no one would boast" — and the saved are "his workmanship, created in Christ Jesus for good works." The same grace heals a human divide: Gentiles, once "separate from Christ… having no hope and without God in the world," have been "made near in the blood of Christ," because Christ "broke down the middle wall of separation" to "create in himself one new man of the two, making peace," reconciling "both in one body to God through the cross." So Gentiles are strangers no longer but "fellow citizens with the saints and of the household of God," built "on the foundation of the apostles and prophets, Christ Jesus himself being the chief cornerstone," growing into a holy temple, "a habitation of God in the Spirit."

## Ephesians 3
**Summary:** Paul pauses to explain why he, "the prisoner of Christ Jesus on behalf of you Gentiles," can speak this way: by revelation God made known to him "the mystery of Christ," hidden from earlier generations and now revealed — "that the Gentiles are fellow heirs and fellow members of the body, and fellow partakers of his promise in Christ Jesus through the Good News." To Paul, "the very least of all saints," was given the grace of preaching "the unsearchable riches of Christ" to the Gentiles, so that now, through the assembly, "the manifold wisdom of God" is displayed even "to the principalities and the powers in the heavenly places." Because of all this, he asks his readers not to lose heart at his troubles for them, "which are your glory." Then he resumes praying: bowing his knees to the Father, he asks that they be "strengthened with power through his Spirit in the inner person," that Christ may dwell in their hearts through faith, and that — "rooted and grounded in love" — they may comprehend "the width and length and height and depth, and to know Christ's love which surpasses knowledge." The chapter closes in a doxology to the one "able to do exceedingly abundantly above all that we ask or think."

**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings; bibleproject-framing
**Sections:**
- **3:1–13 — The Mystery of the Gospel** (title: BSB) — Paul's stewardship: the mystery hidden for ages — Gentiles as fellow heirs in one body — revealed to apostles and prophets, entrusted to the least of all saints to preach, and now displaying God's manifold wisdom to the powers in the heavenly places.
- **3:14–21 — Paul's Prayer for the Ephesians** (title: BSB) — A prayer for inner strength by the Spirit, Christ dwelling in the heart through faith, and a grasp of the love that surpasses knowledge — sealed with a doxology to him who is able to do exceedingly abundantly above all we ask or think.

## Ephesians 4
**Summary:** The letter's hinge: "I therefore, the prisoner in the Lord, beg you to walk worthily of the calling with which you were called." The walk begins with unity — lowliness, patience, "bearing with one another in love," being eager "to keep the unity of the Spirit in the bond of peace" — grounded in a sevenfold oneness: one body, one Spirit, one hope, "one Lord, one faith, one baptism, one God and Father of all." Within that one body the ascended Christ gives differing gifts — some "apostles; and some, prophets; and some, evangelists; and some, shepherds and teachers" — "for the perfecting of the saints," until all attain "to a full grown man" and, no longer children "tossed back and forth… with every wind of doctrine," the body grows up into its head by "speaking truth in love." The second half contrasts two ways of walking: no longer "as the rest of the Gentiles" in futility, darkened understanding, and hardened hearts, but as those who "put away… the old man" and "put on the new man, who in the likeness of God has been created in righteousness and holiness of truth." The chapter lands in specifics — speak truth with your neighbor; "Be angry, and don't sin," and "don't give place to the devil"; steal no more but labor in order to give; let no corrupt speech out, only what builds up; "Don't grieve the Holy Spirit" — and closes: "be kind to one another, tender hearted, forgiving each other, just as God also in Christ forgave you."

- **4:17–32 — New Life in Christ** (title: BSB) — The old Gentile walk — futile, darkened, callous — put away with the old man; the new man put on; and the change made concrete in truthful speech, harmless anger, honest work, edifying words, and tender-hearted forgiveness that mirrors God's own.

## Ephesians 5
**Summary:** "Be therefore imitators of God, as beloved children," and "Walk in love, even as Christ also loved us and gave himself up for us, an offering and a sacrifice to God." That love-walk excludes what once defined the readers: sexual immorality, all uncleanness, and covetousness — the covetous man, Paul says, "is an idolater" — must "not even be mentioned among you, as becomes saints," and no one should be deceived by empty words, "for because of these things the wrath of God comes on the children of disobedience." "For you were once darkness, but are now light in the Lord. Walk as children of light": "the fruit of the Spirit is in all goodness and righteousness and truth," the light exposes the unfruitful deeds of darkness, and the sleeper is called to "Awake… and arise from the dead, and Christ will shine on you." Walking "not as unwise, but as wise" means "redeeming the time, because the days are evil," understanding the Lord's will, and being "filled with the Spirit" — overflowing in "psalms, hymns, and spiritual songs," constant thanksgiving, and "subjecting yourselves to one another in the fear of Christ." Paul then addresses marriage from both sides: wives are to be subject to their own husbands "as to the Lord," as the assembly is subject to Christ its head and savior, and husbands are to love their wives "even as Christ also loved the assembly and gave himself up for her" — sanctifying her, nourishing and cherishing her as his own body — because the "one flesh" of Genesis is, Paul says, "This mystery is great, but I speak concerning Christ and the assembly."

## Ephesians 6
**Summary:** The household instructions continue: "Children, obey your parents in the Lord, for this is right" — "Honor your father and mother" being "the first commandment with a promise" — and fathers in turn must not provoke their children to wrath but "nurture them in the discipline and instruction of the Lord." Servants (in Paul's Roman world, household slaves) are told to obey their earthly masters "in singleness of your heart, as to Christ" — not serving only when eyes are on them, as men pleasers, but "doing the will of God from the heart," knowing the Lord repays every good thing "whether he is bound or free" — and masters are told to "do the same things to them" and to "give up threatening," because the same Master in heaven is over both, "and there is no partiality with him." Then the letter's famous close: "Finally, be strong in the Lord and in the strength of his might," then "Put on the whole armor of God," because "our wrestling is not against flesh and blood" but against principalities, powers, and "the spiritual forces of wickedness in the heavenly places." Paul names the armor piece by piece — the utility belt of truth (the WEB's rendering of the soldier's belt), the breastplate of righteousness, feet fitted "with the preparation of the Good News of peace," the shield of faith that quenches "all the fiery darts of the evil one," the helmet of salvation, and "the sword of the Spirit, which is the word of God" — all carried by "praying at all times in the Spirit," including for Paul, "an ambassador in chains," to speak boldly. Tychicus will bring news of Paul's affairs, and the letter ends as it began: "Peace be to the brothers, and love with faith," and grace to "all those who love our Lord Jesus Christ with incorruptible love."
