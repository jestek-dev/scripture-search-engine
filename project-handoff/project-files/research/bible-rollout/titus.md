# Titus — book summary (draft)

## Short

Titus is Paul's field manual for a cleanup among newly planted assemblies (the WEB's word for churches): he has left Titus, his "true child according to a common faith," on the island of Crete to "set in order the things that were lacking" — appointing elders of blameless character in every city and silencing deceivers who teach for dishonest gain and were upending whole households. The letter's constant refrain is that sound teaching must produce a sound life: older men and women, young wives and younger men, servants and citizens each get instruction that fits "sound doctrine," so that God's word is not blasphemed and opponents are left with "no evil thing to say about us." Twice Paul compresses the whole Good News into a few lines — grace has appeared, bringing salvation and training its people to live self-controlled, upright lives while they wait for the blessed hope; God saved us by mercy and washing, not by our works — and each time the conclusion is the same: a people redeemed at Christ's own cost will be "zealous for good works."

## Full

Titus opens with a greeting outsized for so short a letter: four dense verses tracing Paul's commission back to "hope of eternal life, which God, who can't lie, promised before time began" (1:2). That phrase is load-bearing for the whole letter. On Crete — an island whose own prophet said "Cretans are always liars, evil beasts, and idle gluttons" (1:12) — the God who cannot lie is planting communities that tell the truth and live it.

The first task is leadership. Titus is to appoint elders in every city, and the qualification list (1:5–9) is almost entirely character: blameless, faithful in marriage and household, "not self-pleasing, not easily angered, not given to wine, not violent, not greedy for dishonest gain," but hospitable, self-controlled, and holding to "the faithful word" so he can both encourage with sound doctrine and answer those who contradict it. The reason follows immediately: many "vain talkers and deceivers," especially from the circumcision party, are teaching for dishonest gain and overthrowing whole houses. Their mouths must be stopped and the assemblies reproved sharply, "that they may be sound in the faith" — for these teachers "profess that they know God, but by their deeds they deny him" (1:16), the exact disease the letter is written to cure.

Chapter 2 turns to the congregation. Titus must "say the things which fit sound doctrine" (2:1) — concrete, age-and-station instruction: temperate older men sound in faith, love, and perseverance; reverent older women who train the young wives; sober-minded younger men; Titus himself a model of good works and irreproachable speech; servants showing "all good fidelity." Three times the motive is what outsiders will conclude about the message — "that God's word may not be blasphemed" (2:5), that the opponent "may be ashamed" (2:8), "that they may adorn the doctrine of God, our Savior" (2:10). Then comes the letter's first Good News summary (2:11–14): the grace of God has appeared bringing salvation, and that same grace is a teacher — instructing us to deny ungodliness, to live "soberly, righteously, and godly in this present age," and to keep "looking for the blessed hope and appearing of the glory of our great God and Savior, Jesus Christ," who gave himself to redeem and purify "a people for his own possession, zealous for good works."

Chapter 3 widens the circle to Cretan society: subjection to rulers, readiness for every good work, gentleness and "all humility toward all men" — grounded in memory ("we were also once foolish, disobedient, deceived," 3:3) and in the letter's second Good News summary (3:4–7): when the kindness of God our Savior appeared, he saved us "not by works of righteousness which we did ourselves, but according to his mercy," through the washing of regeneration and renewing by the Holy Spirit, poured out richly through Jesus Christ, "that being justified by his grace, we might be made heirs according to the hope of eternal life." This saying is faithful, Paul says, and it must be insisted on so believers "maintain good works." Foolish questionings, genealogies, and law-disputes are to be shunned; a factious man gets a first and second warning, then avoidance. The letter closes with travel logistics — Artemas or Tychicus to relieve Titus, winter at Nicopolis, Zenas the lawyer and Apollos sped on their journey — one last call for "our people" to learn productive good works, and the grace.

Read as a pair with 1 Timothy, Titus is the shorter, sharper edition: the same concern that the Good News become visible in ordered communities, compressed for a rougher mission field. Its distinctive note is the good-works refrain — the phrase recurs in every chapter (1:16; 2:7, 14; 3:1, 8, 14) — always downstream of grace, never a means of earning it: salvation comes "not by works of righteousness which we did ourselves," and what it makes is a people "zealous for good works."

# The three chapters

## Titus 1
**Summary:** Paul greets Titus, his "true child according to a common faith," anchoring his own apostleship in "hope of eternal life, which God, who can't lie, promised before time began" (1:2). He states the assignment: Titus was left in Crete to "set in order the things that were lacking" and appoint elders in every city — and the qualification list that follows is nearly all character, for the overseer (the WEB's word where older translations say "bishop") is "God's steward": blameless at home, free of temper, drink, violence, and dishonest gain, hospitable, self-controlled, and holding to the faithful word so he can teach sound doctrine and answer those who contradict it. The reason is urgent: many "vain talkers and deceivers," especially of the circumcision, are overthrowing whole houses, teaching what they should not "for dishonest gain's sake." Paul quotes one of Crete's own prophets — "Cretans are always liars, evil beasts, and idle gluttons" — affirms the testimony, and tells Titus to reprove them sharply so that they may become "sound in the faith," turning from Jewish fables and human commandments. The chapter closes on the letter's central disease: the defiled "profess that they know God, but by their deeds they deny him."
**Tags:** `self-deception` — religious profession contradicted by life: "They profess that they know God, but by their deeds they deny him, being abominable, disobedient, and unfit for any good work" (1:16), with "both their mind and their conscience" defiled (1:15); `leadership` — Titus's first task: "set in order the things that were lacking" and appoint elders in every city, the overseer qualified almost entirely by character as "God's steward" (1:5–9); `false-teachers` — the urgency behind the task: many "vain talkers and deceivers" overthrowing whole houses, teaching what they should not "for dishonest gain's sake," whose mouths must be stopped (1:10–16). (The first tag was this chapter's only honest tag from the pre-2026-08-25 vocabulary; the elder-qualification and false-teacher themes, logged then as vocabulary gaps, are carried by the two concepts adopted 2026-08-25.)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings; bibleproject-framing
**Sections:**
- **1:1–4 — Paul's Greeting to Titus** (title: BSB) — The commission traced from God's before-time promise of eternal life, through the message entrusted to Paul, to Titus on Crete: grace, mercy, and peace.
- **1:5–9 — Appointing Elders on Crete** (title: BSB) — Titus's first task: elders in every city, qualified almost entirely by character at home and in temperament, and by a firm hold on the faithful word for teaching and rebuttal.
- **1:10–16 — Correcting False Teachers** (title: BSB) — The urgency behind the task: deceivers teaching for dishonest gain must be silenced and the assemblies sharply reproved, for these men profess God and deny him by their deeds.

## Titus 2
**Summary:** Against the deceivers of chapter 1, Titus is to "say the things which fit sound doctrine" (2:1) — and what fits it turns out to be concrete instruction for each group in the assembly. Older men should be temperate, sensible, and sound in faith, love, and perseverance; older women reverent, not slanderers or "enslaved to much wine," and teachers of good who train the young wives to love their husbands and children; younger men sober minded; Titus himself "an example of good works" whose sound speech leaves an opponent "having no evil thing to say about us"; servants faithful and honest, "that they may adorn the doctrine of God, our Savior, in all things." Then Paul grounds it all in the letter's first Good News summary: "For the grace of God has appeared, bringing salvation to all men" — a grace that itself instructs us to deny ungodliness and worldly lusts and to live "soberly, righteously, and godly in this present age," while "looking for the blessed hope and appearing of the glory of our great God and Savior, Jesus Christ." He "gave himself for us, that he might redeem us from all iniquity and purify for himself a people for his own possession, zealous for good works." Titus is to say these things "with all authority" and let no one despise him.
**Tags:** `grace-not-earned` — salvation arrives as grace before it asks anything: "For the grace of God has appeared, bringing salvation to all men," and grace itself does the instructing (2:11–12); `self-control` — the chapter's refrain across every group: older men "temperate, sensible, sober minded" (2:2), young wives "sober minded, chaste" (2:5), "exhort the younger men to be sober minded" (2:6), and all taught to "live soberly" (2:12); `second-coming` — "looking for the blessed hope and appearing of the glory of our great God and Savior, Jesus Christ" (2:13); `the-cross` — "who gave himself for us, that he might redeem us from all iniquity and purify for himself a people for his own possession" (2:14); `deity-of-christ` — the WEB's rendering titles Jesus directly: "our great God and Savior, Jesus Christ" (2:13); `godly-marriage` — older women are to "train the young wives to love their husbands, to love their children," living "in subjection to their own husbands, that God's word may not be blasphemed" (2:4–5); `discipleship` — the older-training-younger pattern: older women as "teachers of that which is good, that they may train the young wives," with Titus himself "an example of good works" (2:3–7); `bondservants-and-masters` — the servants' instruction among the groups: subjection and "all good fidelity," so "that they may adorn the doctrine of God, our Savior, in all things" (2:9–10)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings; bibleproject-framing
**Sections:**
- **2:1–10 — Teaching Sound Doctrine** (title: BSB) — Sound doctrine translated into household shape: instruction for older men, older women, young wives, younger men, Titus himself, and servants, each tied to how the message will look to those watching from outside.
- **2:11–15 — God's Grace Brings Salvation** (title: BSB) — The first Good News summary: grace appears bringing salvation, teaches a new life in the present age, waits for the blessed hope, and rests on Christ's self-giving to redeem and purify a people zealous for good works.

## Titus 3
**Summary:** The circle widens from household to society: believers are to be "in subjection to rulers and to authorities," obedient, "ready for every good work," slandering no one, not contentious but gentle, "showing all humility toward all men" (3:1–2). The motive is memory — "we were also once foolish, disobedient, deceived" (3:3) — and then the letter's second Good News summary: "when the kindness of God our Savior and his love toward mankind appeared," he saved us "not by works of righteousness which we did ourselves, but according to his mercy, he saved us through the washing of regeneration and renewing by the Holy Spirit," poured out richly through Jesus Christ, "that being justified by his grace, we might be made heirs according to the hope of eternal life" (3:4–7). Paul calls this a faithful saying to be insisted on, so that believers "may be careful to maintain good works." By contrast, foolish questionings, genealogies, strife, and law-disputes are "unprofitable and vain," and a factious man is to be avoided after a first and second warning, being "self-condemned." The letter ends with travel plans — Artemas or Tychicus coming, Paul wintering at Nicopolis, Zenas and Apollos to be sped on their way lacking nothing — a final charge that "our people" learn to maintain good works to meet necessary needs, and greetings with grace for all.
**Tags:** `salvation` — the chapter's center: "he saved us through the washing of regeneration and renewing by the Holy Spirit" (3:5), poured out "through Jesus Christ our Savior" (3:6); `grace-not-earned` — "not by works of righteousness which we did ourselves, but according to his mercy" (3:5); `justification-by-faith` — "that being justified by his grace, we might be made heirs according to the hope of eternal life" (3:7); `harmony-with-others` — "to speak evil of no one, not to be contentious, to be gentle, showing all humility toward all men" (3:2), with quarrels over questionings and genealogies shunned as "unprofitable and vain" (3:9); `good-works` — the letter's refrain pressed home: "ready for every good work" and, as the faithful saying's stated aim, "careful to maintain good works" — always downstream of the mercy that saved (3:1, 8, 14); `governing-authorities` — the chapter's opening charge: "in subjection to rulers and to authorities," obedient toward the society believers live in (3:1–2); `church-discipline` — the avoid-after-warnings procedure: a factious man gets a first and second warning, then avoidance, "being self-condemned" (3:10–11); `outpouring-of-the-spirit` — the letter-side outpouring text: the Holy Spirit "whom he poured out on us richly through Jesus Christ our Savior" in the washing of regeneration (3:5–6)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings; bibleproject-framing
**Sections:**
- **3:1–8 — Heirs of Grace** (title: BSB) — Good citizenship and gentleness toward all, grounded in what we were and in the second Good News summary: saved by mercy, washed and renewed by the Spirit, justified by grace, heirs of eternal life — a faithful saying that funds good works.
- **3:9–11 — Avoid Divisions** (title: BSB) — Foolish questionings, genealogies, and law-disputes are unprofitable; a factious man gets two warnings, then avoidance, being self-condemned.
- **3:12–15 — Final Remarks and Greetings** (title: BSB) — Travel logistics for Artemas, Tychicus, Zenas, and Apollos; a last call to productive good works; greetings and grace.
