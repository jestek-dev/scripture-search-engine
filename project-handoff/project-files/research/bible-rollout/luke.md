# Luke — book summary (draft)

## Short

Luke is the Gospel written as a careful, orderly account. Its author has “traced the course of all things accurately from the first” and writes so that Theophilus — and every reader after him — “might know the certainty concerning the things in which you were instructed.” The story it tells has the widest embrace of the four Gospels: a Savior conceived by the Holy Spirit and laid in a feeding trough, announced first to shepherds; good news preached to the poor; tax collectors, Samaritans, women, and notorious sinners welcomed until the complaint becomes a summary — “This man welcomes sinners, and eats with them.” Jesus prays at every turning point, rejoices in the Holy Spirit, and sets his face resolutely toward Jerusalem to die and rise — summing up his own mission on the way there, at a tax collector's house in Jericho: “the Son of Man came to seek and to save that which was lost.”

## Full

Luke opens with the only preface in the four Gospels (1:1–4): many have undertaken to set the story in order, eyewitnesses have handed it down, and now the author — having traced everything accurately from the first — writes an orderly account “to you… most excellent Theophilus; that you might know the certainty concerning the things in which you were instructed.” That stated purpose governs the whole book. Luke is the fullest of the Gospels: only here do we get the songs around Jesus' birth, the shepherds and the feeding trough, the boy Jesus in the temple, and a long central journey packed with parables found nowhere else — the Good Samaritan, the prodigal son, the rich man and Lazarus, the persistent widow, the Pharisee and the tax collector.

The book moves in four broad movements. Chapters 1–4 prepare: two miraculous births are announced and celebrated in song, John preaches a baptism of repentance, Jesus is baptized, tested in the wilderness, and announces his mission in the Nazareth synagogue from Isaiah — “to preach good news to the poor… release to the captives, recovering of sight to the blind.” Chapters 4–9 show that mission at work in Galilee: teaching with authority, healing, forgiving sins, calling disciples, stilling a storm, raising the dead. At 9:51 the book pivots on a single sentence — “he intently set his face to go to Jerusalem” — and for ten chapters Jesus travels toward the city, teaching about prayer, money, mercy, and the cost of following him. Chapters 19–24 bring the passion week: the weeping King enters Jerusalem, is betrayed, denied, condemned, and crucified, and on the third day rises — opening the Scriptures on the Emmaus road, commissioning witnesses, and ascending while they worship.

Certain notes sound all through Luke. The Holy Spirit fills and leads people from the first chapter to the last movement — John, Elizabeth, Zacharias, Simeon, and above all Jesus, who returns from the Jordan “full of the Holy Spirit” and ministers “in the power of the Spirit.” Jesus is constantly at prayer — at his baptism, before choosing the Twelve, before Peter's confession, on the mountain of transfiguration — and teaches his disciples to pray with boldness and persistence. Joy keeps breaking out: “good news of great joy” at the birth, and the book's last line has the disciples “continually in the temple, praising and blessing God.” And Mary's song sets the book's social key signature early: God “has put down princes from their thrones, and has exalted the lowly. He has filled the hungry with good things. He has sent the rich away empty.” Luke's Jesus blesses the poor and warns the rich, touches lepers and corpses, honors Samaritans, names women among his traveling company, and eats — again and again — with sinners.

The ending turns the story outward without finishing it. The risen Jesus shows from Moses, the prophets, and the psalms that the Christ had to suffer and rise, “and that repentance and remission of sins should be preached in his name to all the nations, beginning at Jerusalem” (24:47). He promises the Father's promised power, blesses his disciples, and is carried up into heaven — and Luke's account continues in his second volume, the book of Acts, where that mission to all the nations actually begins.

# The twenty-four chapters

## Luke 1
**Summary:** Luke opens with his preface: having traced everything accurately from the first, he writes an orderly account so Theophilus “might know the certainty concerning the things in which you were instructed.” In the temple the angel Gabriel promises the aged priest Zacharias a son, John, who will go before the Lord “in the spirit and power of Elijah” — and Zacharias, who doesn't believe it, is struck mute until it comes true, while barren Elizabeth conceives and blesses the Lord for taking away her reproach. Six months later Gabriel comes to a virgin in Nazareth: Mary will conceive by the Holy Spirit and bear Jesus, the Son of the Most High, whose Kingdom will have no end; she answers, “Behold, the servant of the Lord; let it be done to me according to your word.” Visiting Elizabeth, Mary sings the song known as the Magnificat: God has looked at his servant's humble state, scattered the proud, exalted the lowly, and filled the hungry with good things. John is born, Zacharias's tongue is freed, and his prophecy blesses the Lord who “has visited and redeemed his people,” remembering his holy covenant and preparing the way for the dawn from on high.

## Luke 2
**Summary:** A census decree from Caesar Augustus brings Joseph and pregnant Mary from Nazareth to Bethlehem, David's city, where Jesus is born, wrapped in bands of cloth, and laid in a feeding trough — our WEB translation's plain rendering of the familiar "manger" — because there was no room in the inn. An angel brings shepherds “good news of great joy”: born this day is “a Savior, who is Christ the Lord” — and a multitude of the heavenly army praises God; the shepherds find the baby and return glorifying God, while Mary keeps all these sayings, pondering them in her heart. Circumcised and named Jesus, the child is presented at the temple in Jerusalem, where aged Simeon — promised by the Holy Spirit that he would see the Lord's Christ before dying — takes him in his arms, saying “my eyes have seen your salvation,” and warns Mary that a sword will pierce her own soul; the elderly prophetess Anna gives thanks and speaks of him to all who look for redemption. Years later, the twelve-year-old Jesus stays behind at Passover and is found in the temple among the teachers — “Didn’t you know that I must be in my Father’s house?” — then returns to Nazareth, subject to his parents, and increases “in wisdom and stature, and in favor with God and men.”

## Luke 3
**Summary:** Dated by emperor, rulers, and high priests, “the word of God came to John, the son of Zacharias, in the wilderness,” and he goes through the Jordan region “preaching the baptism of repentance for remission of sins” — the voice Isaiah promised, making ready the way of the Lord. He calls the crowds an “offspring of vipers,” warns that “the ax also lies at the root of the trees,” and answers the question “What then must we do?” with concrete commands for crowds, tax collectors, and soldiers: share your coats and food, collect no more than appointed, extort from no one. To those wondering whether he is the Christ, John points to one mightier: “He will baptize you in the Holy Spirit and fire,” gathering wheat and burning chaff — preaching that eventually lands John in Herod's prison. When Jesus is baptized and praying, the sky opens, the Holy Spirit descends “in a bodily form like a dove,” and a voice from the sky says, “You are my beloved Son. In you I am well pleased”; Luke then traces Jesus' line backward from Joseph through David and Abraham all the way to “the son of Adam, the son of God.”

## Luke 4
**Summary:** Jesus, “full of the Holy Spirit,” is led by the Spirit into the wilderness, where for forty days he is “tempted by the devil” — to turn a stone to bread, to gain the world's kingdoms by worship, to leap from the temple's pinnacle. He answers each temptation with “It is written,” and the devil departs “until another time.” Returning “in the power of the Spirit,” Jesus reads Isaiah in the Nazareth synagogue — “The Spirit of the Lord is on me, because he has anointed me to preach good news to the poor” — and announces, “Today, this Scripture has been fulfilled in your hearing”; but when he reminds his hometown that Elijah and Elisha were sent to outsiders like the widow of Zarephath and Naaman the Syrian, the synagogue fills with wrath and tries to throw him off a cliff. In Capernaum his word carries authority: he expels an unclean demon in the synagogue, rebukes the fever of Simon's mother-in-law, heals all the evening's sick with his hands, and silences demons who cry, “You are the Christ, the Son of God!” — then moves on, because “I must preach the good news of God’s Kingdom to the other cities also.”

## Luke 5
**Summary:** Teaching from Simon's boat, Jesus tells the fisherman to “put out into the deep and let down your nets for a catch”; the nets fill to breaking, Simon Peter falls at his knees — “Depart from me, for I am a sinful man, Lord” — and Jesus answers, “Don’t be afraid. From now on you will be catching people alive.” Simon, James, and John leave everything and follow him. A man full of leprosy begs, “Lord, if you want to, you can make me clean,” and Jesus touches him — “I want to. Be made clean” — while a paralyzed man, lowered through the roof tiles by friends, hears something greater than his friends asked: “Man, your sins are forgiven you,” a claim Jesus defends against charges of blasphemy by healing him, “that you may know that the Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins.” He then calls the tax collector Levi, who leaves everything and throws a great feast, and answers the murmuring Pharisees: “I have not come to call the righteous, but sinners, to repentance” — new wine that old wineskins cannot hold.

## Luke 6
**Summary:** Two Sabbath disputes open the chapter: Jesus defends his grain-plucking disciples from David's example — “The Son of Man is lord of the Sabbath” — then, watched by hostile scribes, asks whether the Sabbath is for doing good or harm and restores a man's withered hand, filling his enemies with rage. After a whole night in prayer on the mountain, he chooses twelve apostles, then comes down to a level place where crowds from all over come to hear and be healed. There he preaches the sermon often called the Sermon on the Plain: blessings on the poor, the hungry, the weeping, and the hated — with woes on the rich, the full, the laughing, and the well-spoken-of — then the call to love enemies, do good, bless, lend expecting nothing, and be merciful as the Father is merciful. Don't judge; give and it will be given “good measure, pressed down, shaken together, and running over”; a tree is known by its fruit; and the one who hears these words and does them is like a man whose house is founded on rock, while the hearer who does nothing builds on bare earth, “and the ruin of that house was great.”

## Luke 7

## Luke 8
**Summary:** Jesus travels through cities and villages “bringing the good news of God’s Kingdom,” accompanied by the twelve and by women he had healed — Mary Magdalene, Joanna, Susanna, and many others — who served them from their possessions. He tells the parable of the sower and explains it: “The seed is the word of God,” received variously by the trampled path, the rootless rock, the choking thorns, and the honest and good heart that holds it tightly and bears fruit with perseverance — so “be careful therefore how you hear,” for a lamp is lit to be seen, and his true family are “these who hear the word of God and do it.” Crossing the lake, he sleeps through a windstorm, wakes to rebuke wind and water — “Where is your faith?” — and leaves the disciples asking who this is that even winds and water obey. In the country of the Gadarenes he frees a man possessed by a legion of demons — a man who had broken chains and lived naked among the tombs — sending the demons into a herd of pigs; the frightened townspeople beg Jesus to leave, but the delivered man is sent home to “declare what great things God has done for you.” Back across the lake, a woman with twelve years of bleeding touches the fringe of his cloak and is healed — “Daughter, cheer up. Your faith has made you well” — and Jairus's dead twelve-year-old daughter is raised with the words, “Child, arise!”
