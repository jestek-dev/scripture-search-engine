# Job — book summary

## Short

Job is the Bible's longest, hardest look at the suffering of the righteous. God himself calls Job "a blameless and an upright man, one who fears God, and turns away from evil" — and then, in a heavenly scene Job never gets to see, permits Satan to strip him of children, wealth, and health to test whether Job fears God for nothing. Three friends come to comfort him and end up prosecuting him, insisting his pain must be punishment for hidden sin; Job refuses the charge, refuses to renounce God, and demands his day in court with the Almighty. When the LORD finally answers out of the whirlwind, he explains nothing about chapters 1 and 2 — he unrolls creation instead, from the sea's boundaries to the wild donkey to Leviathan, until Job repents in dust and ashes. The verdict lands where no one expected: the pious-sounding friends "have not spoken of me the thing that is right, as my servant Job has," and the LORD restores Job's fortunes as he prays for the men who tormented him.

## Full

Job opens in the land of Uz, outside Israel's story and (by its setting) before it — a patriarch-like figure who offers his own sacrifices and measures wealth in livestock. The first two chapters run on two stages at once. On earth, Job is blameless, prosperous, and careful even for his children's hearts. In heaven, where "God's sons" present themselves before the LORD, Satan answers the LORD's praise of Job with the question that drives the whole book: "Does Job fear God for nothing?" Twice the LORD permits Satan to test the accusation — first Job's possessions and children, then his health — and twice Job holds: "The LORD gave, and the LORD has taken away. Blessed be the LORD's name." The reader is shown what Job is never shown; his suffering is a test he cannot see, not a sentence he deserves.

Then the poetry begins, and with it the long argument (chapters 3–31). Job's opening lament curses the day of his birth, and his three friends — Eliphaz, Bildad, and Zophar, who began so well by weeping and sitting silent with him seven days — take turns answering him through three cycles of speeches. Their theology is tidy: God prospers the righteous and breaks the wicked, so great suffering must mean great sin; repent, and prosperity will return. Job's experience contradicts the system, and he says so — the wicked often flourish (chapter 21), the poor are crushed while heaven seems silent (chapter 24), and his own conscience is clean. Caught between a God he will not renounce and a suffering he cannot explain, Job does something the friends never do: he turns and speaks *to* God — complaining, pleading, demanding a hearing, wishing for an umpire between them, insisting that his witness is in heaven and that "I know that my Redeemer lives." The dialogue collapses in the third cycle (Bildad manages six verses; Zophar never takes his third turn), Job closes with a sweeping oath of innocence, and "the words of Job are ended."

Two more voices remain. Elihu, an angry young bystander, speaks for six chapters (32–37): furious at Job for justifying himself rather than God and at the friends for condemning without an answer, he argues that God is never unjust, that he speaks through dreams and through pain, and that affliction can be discipline that rescues rather than wrath that repays. Then the LORD himself answers Job out of the whirlwind (38–41) — not with the explanation the reader holds from chapters 1–2, but with questions: where were you when I laid the earth's foundations? Can you command the morning, bind the Pleiades, feed the lion, tame the war-horse, draw out Leviathan with a fish hook? The tour of creation neither convicts Job of the friends' charges nor unveils the heavenly wager; it confronts him with a wisdom and government vaster than his case, and it is enough. "I had heard of you by the hearing of the ear," Job answers, "but now my eye sees you. Therefore I abhor myself, and repent in dust and ashes."

The epilogue (42:7–17) delivers the book's double verdict. The LORD's wrath burns against the three friends — "you have not spoken of me the thing that is right, as my servant Job has" — and their restoration runs through the man they condemned: seven bulls, seven rams, and Job's prayer. Then the LORD restores Job's prosperity — twice what he had, ten more children, a hundred forty more years — not as a payout the system predicted, but as free kindness after the test is done; the narrator can even say Job's family comforted him "concerning all the evil that the LORD had brought on him." The book leaves the deepest question — why the righteous suffer — without a formula, and gives instead what Job finally got: not an answer, but God.

# The forty-two chapters

## Job 1
**Summary:** Job of Uz is "blameless and upright, and one who feared God, and turned away from evil" — the greatest man of the east, rich in flocks and children, offering sacrifices for his sons in case they have "renounced God in their hearts" (renounced — our WEB translation's word where many Bibles read "cursed"). The scene shifts to heaven: among "God's sons" Satan answers the LORD's praise of Job with a challenge — "Does Job fear God for nothing?" — claiming Job is pious only because God has hedged and blessed him. The LORD puts all Job has in Satan's power, and in one day four messengers bring word that Sabeans, fire from the sky, Chaldeans, and a great wind have taken Job's oxen and donkeys, sheep, camels, servants, and all ten children. Job tears his robe, shaves his head, falls to the ground — and worships: "The LORD gave, and the LORD has taken away. Blessed be the LORD's name." In all this, Job doesn't sin or charge God with wrongdoing.

## Job 2
**Summary:** The heavenly scene repeats: the LORD points to Job's unbroken integrity — "although you incited me against him, to ruin him without cause" — and Satan answers, "Skin for skin," betting that Job will renounce God if his own body is struck. His wife tells him to "renounce God, and die"; Job answers, "Shall we receive good at the hand of God, and shall we not receive evil?" — and doesn't sin with his lips. Three friends — Eliphaz the Temanite, Bildad the Shuhite, and Zophar the Naamathite — come to sympathize and comfort, don't recognize him from a distance, weep, and sit with him on the ground seven days and nights without a word, "for they saw that his grief was very great."

## Job 3
**Summary:** After the seven days of silence, Job opens his mouth — not to renounce God, but to curse the day of his birth: "Let the day perish in which I was born." He wishes the day unmade, wishes he had died from the womb, and imagines the grave as rest, where "the wicked cease from troubling" and "the servant is free from his master." His questions widen to every sufferer: why is light given to the miserable, "life to the bitter in soul, who long for death, but it doesn't come"? The chapter ends with the thing he feared having come upon him: "I am not at ease, neither am I quiet, neither do I have rest; but trouble comes." The long argument of the book starts here, in a lament the text neither censors nor condemns.

## Job 4
**Summary:** Eliphaz the Temanite, first and gentlest of the three friends, begins the answering: Job has strengthened others in trouble — "But now it has come to you, and you faint." His theory is the friends' whole case in miniature: "who ever perished, being innocent?... those who plow iniquity and sow trouble, reap the same." He backs it with a private revelation — a night vision in which fear came on him, a spirit passed before his face, and a voice asked, "Shall mortal man be more just than God? Shall a man be more pure than his Maker?" The question is true enough; the use Eliphaz will make of it — reasoning back from Job's suffering to Job's guilt — is what the book's last chapter overturns.

## Job 5
**Summary:** Eliphaz continues: trouble doesn't sprout from nowhere — "man is born to trouble, as the sparks fly upward" — so Job should do what Eliphaz would do: "I would seek God. I would commit my cause to God," the God who does great, unsearchable things, lifts the lowly, and traps the crafty in their own schemes. He urges Job to welcome the affliction as correction — "happy is the man whom God corrects. Therefore do not despise the chastening of the Almighty" — and paints the restored life that will follow: deliverance in six troubles and seven, a tent in peace, abundant offspring, a full old age. It is eloquent, much of it true in other mouths and other settings — and it is aimed at a man whose suffering, the reader already knows, is not chastening for sin; Eliphaz closes, "we have researched it. It is so," certain of a system the book is about to break.

## Job 6
**Summary:** Job answers Eliphaz: "Oh that my anguish were weighed" — it would prove "heavier than the sand of the seas," for "the arrows of the Almighty are within me" — so let his rash words be read as the cry of a crushed man, not the confession of a hidden sinner. He would rather God crush him and cut him off, for one consolation remains: "I have not denied the words of the Holy One." Then he turns on his friends: "To him who is ready to faint, kindness should be shown from his friend," but his brothers have failed like desert brooks that vanish in the heat just when the caravans need them. He asks for the one thing they haven't offered — "Teach me, and I will hold my peace. Cause me to understand my error" — and insists his cause is righteous.

## Job 7
**Summary:** Job's reply turns from the friends to the human condition and then to God himself. Life is a hired hand's forced labor; his nights are tossing, his flesh clothed with worms, his days "swifter than a weaver's shuttle... spent without hope"; whoever goes down to Sheol comes up no more. So he will not keep silent: "I will complain in the bitterness of my soul" — and the complaint goes straight to God: am I the sea, or a sea monster, that you guard me, and "What is man, that you should magnify him... and test him every moment?" Even if I have sinned, what does that do to you, "you watcher of men" — why not simply pardon it before I lie down in the dust? The chapter is raw, and the book keeps it: Job's wrestling is conducted face-to-God, which is exactly what the friends never do.

## Job 8
**Summary:** Bildad the Shuhite speaks with less tact than Eliphaz and the same system: "Does God pervert justice? Or does the Almighty pervert righteousness?" He applies it without flinching even to Job's dead children — if they sinned, God "delivered them into the hand of their disobedience" — and offers Job the standard bargain: seek God, be pure and upright, and your latter end will greatly increase. His authority is tradition, the learning of past generations; his imagery is memorable — the papyrus withering without water, the godless man's trust "a spider's web," the flourishing plant torn from its place — and his conclusion is confident: God won't cast away a blameless man, so laughter waits for Job if only he qualifies. The reader knows what Bildad doesn't: Job already is the blameless man (1:8), and the system can't see it.

## Job 9
**Summary:** Job concedes Bildad's premise in principle — "Truly I know that it is so" — and asks the question under it: "how can man be just with God?" No one can summon the Almighty to court: he removes mountains, shakes the earth, commands the sun, "stretches out the heavens, and treads on the waves of the sea," and "makes the Bear, Orion, and the Pleiades." Overmatched, Job says even his own mouth would condemn him though he is blameless — and in his anguish he goes further than he will be able to sustain, charging that God "destroys the blameless and the wicked" alike and mocks at the trial of the innocent. What he wants is not acquittal by power but a fair hearing: "There is no umpire between us, that might lay his hand on us both" — the first reach toward a go-between who could bring man and God to one table.

## Job 10
**Summary:** "My soul is weary of my life" — so Job gives his complaint free course and rehearses to God's face what he would say in court: "Do not condemn me. Show me why you contend with me." Wickedness or righteousness seems to make no difference to his misery, and the chapter sinks back toward chapter 3: "Why, then, have you brought me out of the womb?" He asks only for a little comfort before going to "the land of darkness and of the shadow of death."

## Job 11
**Summary:** Zophar the Naamathite, bluntest of the three, is ashamed that Job's "boastings" have gone unanswered: he wishes God would open his lips against Job and show him wisdom's secrets — for, he says, "God exacts of you less than your iniquity deserves." The offer ends with a threat: for the wicked, no way of escape — "their hope will be the giving up of the spirit." Zophar has told a man God called blameless that he deserves worse than he has gotten.

## Job 12
**Summary:** Job answers all three with an edge — "No doubt, but you are the people, and wisdom will die with you" — insisting he understands as well as they do, and that their maxims are common knowledge: even the just man has become "a joke to his neighbor." Then he out-doxologizes them: ask the animals, the birds, the earth, the fish, and they will teach you that "the LORD's hand has done this, in whose hand is the life of every living thing, and the breath of all mankind." "With God is wisdom and might": he tears down what can't be rebuilt, withholds and sends the waters, strips counselors, kings, priests, and judges, "pours contempt on princes," and makes nations great and destroys them. Job's point cuts against the friends' tidy system: the God who governs everything is freer, stranger, and less domesticated than their theology allows.

## Job 13
**Summary:** Job dismisses the friends' counsel as worthless medicine — "you are forgers of lies. You are all physicians of no value" — and warns them that defending God with falsehood is itself sin: "Will you speak unrighteously for God, and talk deceitfully for him?... He will surely reprove you if you secretly show partiality." Silence would be their best wisdom; his own course lies elsewhere: "I desire to reason with God," whatever it costs — "Behold, he will kill me. I have no hope. Nevertheless, I will maintain my ways before him" (the WEB's rendering of the verse many Bibles word as "though he slay me, yet will I trust in him"). Elihu's principle stands nearer the book's verdict than the friends' arithmetic — though his closing wish, that Job "were tried to the end" for answering like wicked men, is harsher than the LORD's own sentence will be.
**Tags:** `providence` — God's just, unpartial government of every creature: "if he gathered to himself his spirit and his breath, all flesh would perish together... He is over a nation or a man alike" (34:12–15, 34:19–29; Elihu speaking). (Only one honest tag from the current vocabulary.)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings

## Job 35
**Summary:** Elihu next takes Job's bitter arithmetic — what advantage is righteousness "more than if I had sinned?" — and points at the sky: God is higher than the clouds; your sin doesn't wound him and your righteousness gives him nothing; both land on your fellow humans. Why then do the oppressed cry out and go unanswered? Because, Elihu argues, they cry for relief without seeking the Giver: "no one says, 'Where is God my Maker, who gives songs in the night'" — and God won't hear "an empty cry." So Job's impatience is misplaced: the cause is before God — wait for him — and Elihu closes with the charge the LORD will echo: Job "multiplies words without knowledge."
**Tags:** none — no concept in the current vocabulary is genuinely present in this chapter ("songs in the night," 35:10, is captured as a motif candidate)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings

## Job 36
**Summary:** Elihu asks a little more patience — "I still have something to say on God's behalf" — and states his thesis plainly: "God is mighty, and doesn't despise anyone." He gives justice to the afflicted; and when the righteous are "bound in fetters... taken in the cords of afflictions," he is showing them their work and opening "their ears to instruction" — listen and serve, and days end in prosperity; refuse, and perish without knowledge. The hinge of Elihu's whole argument comes here: "He delivers the afflicted by their affliction, and opens their ear in oppression" — so Job should beware turning to iniquity under the pressure. From there the speech lifts into the approaching storm: God "is exalted in his power. Who is a teacher like him?" — the one who draws up the water drops, spreads the clouds, and covers his hands with the lightning.
**Tags:** `the-lords-discipline` — affliction as God's opened classroom for the righteous: "He also opens their ears to instruction... He delivers the afflicted by their affliction" (36:8–15; Elihu speaking — see Decisions record #3); `creation` — the rain cycle and the thundercloud as God's visible greatness: "he draws up the drops of water, which distill in rain from his vapor" (36:26–33; Elihu speaking)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings

## Job 37
**Summary:** The storm arrives in Elihu's speech before the LORD speaks from it. "God thunders marvelously with his voice. He does great things, which we can't comprehend" — snow and rain sealing up every hand, animals driven to their dens, ice given by God's breath, clouds loaded with moisture and turned around by his guidance "whether it is for correction, or for his land, or for loving kindness." Elihu turns the display on Job: "Stand still, and consider the wondrous works of God. Do you know how God controls them?... Can you, with him, spread out the sky?" His closing summary is nearly a preface for the whirlwind: "With God is awesome majesty. We can't reach the Almighty... In justice and great righteousness, he will not oppress. Therefore men revere him."
**Tags:** `creation` — the weather itself as God's craftsmanship: "he says to the snow, 'Fall on the earth'... By the breath of God, ice is given" (37:5–13; Elihu speaking); `providence` — the storm steered to purpose: "It is turned around by his guidance... whether it is for correction, or for his land, or for loving kindness" (37:12–13; Elihu speaking)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings

## Job 38
**Summary:** "Then the LORD answered Job out of the whirlwind" — the answer Job demanded, though not the one he scripted: "Who is this who darkens counsel by words without knowledge? Brace yourself like a man, for I will question you." The questions unroll creation: where was Job when the earth's foundations were laid, "when the morning stars sang together"; who shut the sea behind doors and told its proud waves, "You may come here, but no further"; has Job commanded the morning, entered the storehouses of the snow, bound the Pleiades, or sent rain "on a land where there is no man"? The catalog ends with hunting prey for the lioness and providing for the raven — a world God runs in detail, much of it beyond human sight or use. Nothing about chapters 1–2 is explained; Job is being shown the Ruler, not the reasons.
**Tags:** `creation` — the Creator's own tour of foundations, sea, dawn, snow, rain, and stars: "Where were you when I laid the foundations of the earth?... when the morning stars sang together" (38:4–38); `gods-provision` — the wild world fed by God: "Who provides for the raven his prey, when his young ones cry to God?" (38:39–41); `design-in-creation` — the whirlwind's architectural imagery presents the earth as deliberately laid and man as unqualified to audit it: "Where were you when I laid the foundations of the earth?" (38:4–6; the deliberate-orderedness register beside `creation`'s survey of the same speech)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings

## Job 39
**Summary:** The LORD's questions move from cosmos to creatures — wild ones, useless to man and cared for anyway. Does Job know when the mountain goats give birth, who freed the wild donkey to scorn the city and roam the salt lands, or whether the wild ox will consent to pull his plow? The ostrich flaps proudly and abandons her eggs, fearless "because God has deprived her of wisdom" — yet when she lifts herself up, "she scorns the horse and his rider." The war-horse, God's own portrait of fearless strength, "mocks at fear... neither does he stand still at the sound of the trumpet," and the hawk and eagle soar and nest on the cliff at no man's command. Every portrait presses the same question: a wildness Job can't manage is still governed and provided for — so who is Job to audit its Governor?
**Tags:** `creation` — the wild creatures as God's exhibit: mountain goat, wild donkey, wild ox, ostrich, horse, hawk, and eagle, each made and known (39:1–30). (Only one honest tag from the current vocabulary.)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings

## Job 40
**Summary:** The LORD pauses for an answer — "Shall he who argues contend with the Almighty?" — and Job, who once planned to approach God like a prince, covers his mouth instead: "Behold, I am of small account... I have spoken once, and I will not answer; yes, twice, but I will proceed no further." Out of the whirlwind the LORD then asks the question under the whole book: "Will you even annul my judgment? Will you condemn me, that you may be justified?" If Job can do God's job — deck himself with majesty, look at everyone who is proud and bring him low — then God will admit Job's own right hand can save him. As exhibit, "See now behemoth, which I made as well as you": grass-eating, cedar-tailed, bones like bronze, "the chief of the ways of God," unfrightened by a swelling Jordan and beyond any snare.
**Tags:** `humble-exaltation` — Job's hand over his mouth, and the challenge that only God abases the proud: "I am of small account"; "Look at everyone who is proud, and humble him" (40:3–5, 40:9–14); `creation` — behemoth, God's own colossal creature: "See now behemoth, which I made as well as you" (40:15–24); `leviathan-and-behemoth` — the first of the named pair, exhibited by his Maker: "See now behemoth," grass-eating, bones like bronze, "the chief of the ways of God," beyond any snare (40:15–24)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings
**Sections:**
- **40:1–5 — Job Humbles Himself before the LORD** (title: BSB) — Challenged to answer, Job declares himself of small account, lays his hand on his mouth, and retracts his intent to keep contending.
- **40:6–24 — The LORD Challenges Job Again** (title: BSB) — A second whirlwind speech: will Job condemn God to justify himself? Let him try governing the proud — and consider behemoth, the untameable chief of the ways of God.

## Job 41
**Summary:** The final exhibit is Leviathan: "Can you draw out Leviathan with a fish hook?" — will he beg, make a covenant, or become a pet for your girls? Lay a hand on him once and "do so no more" — none is fierce enough to stir him, and the argument lands with God: "Who then is he who can stand before me? Who has first given to me, that I should repay him? Everything under the heavens is mine." The description celebrates the creature's terror: sealed rows of scales, sneezes that flash light, flame from his mouth, a heart "firm as the lower millstone," iron counted as straw, the deep boiling like a pot behind him. "On earth there is not his equal... He is king over all the sons of pride" — and this untouchable king is simply one of God's works.
**Tags:** `creation` — Leviathan, the creature no human can tame, described limb by limb by his Maker (41:1–34); `providence` — the claim the creature proves: "Who has first given to me, that I should repay him? Everything under the heavens is mine" (41:10–11); `leviathan-and-behemoth` — the named creature the whole chapter describes: "Can you draw out Leviathan with a fish hook?" — "On earth there is not his equal... He is king over all the sons of pride" (41:1–34)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings

## Job 42
**Summary:** Job answers the LORD: "I know that you can do all things, and that no purpose of yours can be restrained... I have uttered that which I didn't understand, things too wonderful for me." Hearing has become sight — "I had heard of you by the hearing of the ear, but now my eye sees you. Therefore I abhor myself, and repent in dust and ashes." Then the verdict: the LORD's wrath burns against Eliphaz and his two friends, "for you have not spoken of me the thing that is right, as my servant Job has" — they must bring seven bulls and seven rams, and Job, the man they condemned, must pray for them. "The LORD restored Job's prosperity when he prayed for his friends," giving him twice what he had: doubled flocks, seven sons and three daughters — Jemimah, Keziah, and Keren Happuch, given an inheritance among their brothers — while family and friends comfort him "concerning all the evil that the LORD had brought on him." Job lives a hundred forty more years, sees four generations, and dies "old and full of days" — restored by God's free kindness after the test, not by the payout formula that is the one thing the chapter explicitly condemns.
**Tags:** `repentance` — "I abhor myself, and repent in dust and ashes" (42:6); `prayer` — intercession as the hinge of restoration: "my servant Job shall pray for you, for I will accept him" (42:8–10); `restoration` — "The LORD restored Job's prosperity when he prayed for his friends" (42:10); `humble-exaltation` — the humbled contender is honored: Job repents in dust and ashes, and the LORD accepts him, vindicates him against his friends, and doubles his estate (42:6, 42:8–10, 42:12); `blessing` — "So the LORD blessed the latter end of Job more than his beginning" (42:12–17); `presence-of-god` — the encounter that ends the argument: "now my eye sees you" (42:5); `suffering-of-the-righteous` — the verdict that completes the question the whole book stages: the honest sufferer vindicated over his pious accusers — "you have not spoken of me the thing that is right, as my servant Job has" (42:7–8); `why-god-allows-suffering` — the book's resolution of its own question: God's answer is his presence and unrestrainable purpose, not a disclosed reason — "I know that you can do all things, and that no purpose of yours can be restrained" and "I had heard of you by the hearing of the ear, but now my eye sees you" (42:2, 5; Job is never told the heavenly backstory the reader knows — Scripture's mystery-shaped answer, the map's caveat carried)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings
**Sections:**
- **42:1–6 — Job Submits Himself to the LORD** (title: BSB) — Job confesses that no purpose of God can be restrained, that he spoke of things too wonderful for him, and — having now seen and not merely heard — repents in dust and ashes.
- **42:7–9 — The LORD Rebukes Job's Friends** (title: BSB) — Twice the LORD tells Eliphaz that the three friends have spoken of him wrongly, unlike "my servant Job"; their restoration requires sacrifice and the intercession of the man they condemned, and the LORD accepts Job.
- **42:10–17 — The LORD Blesses Job** (title: BSB) — When Job prays for his friends, the LORD restores him double: comfort from family, doubled flocks, ten children with his beautiful daughters named and given inheritances, a hundred forty more years, and a death old and full of days.

# Decisions record — final (delegated defaults; Jesse can reverse any of them)

1. **Vocabulary ruling applied; none of the nine PR #43 ids is used — on honest-presence grounds, not eligibility.** Per the project-wide ruling for the rollout threads, the taggable vocabulary is `ontology/concepts/*.yaml` on main at `b3f491d` — all 131 ids, including the nine PR #43 added (`asking-in-gods-will`, `baptism`, `benediction`, `caring-for-aging-parents`, `christ-the-cornerstone`, `doubt`, `incarnation`, `justification-by-faith`, `trinity`); a tag from those nine is permitted but must be noted here as a reversible delegated default. Two were seriously considered for Job and left off as honest-presence failures: `justification-by-faith` (label "Justified by faith"; Romans-anchored) — Job poses the court question ("how can man be just with God?", 9:2; 25:4) but never depicts the by-faith teaching the concept carries, so the tag would route "justified by faith" searches to a chapter that asks without answering; and `doubt` (anchored to "help my unbelief," Mark 9) — Job's questioning is contention with a God he never stops addressing, which `wrestling-with-god` already carries honestly. Reversible. The same ruling fixes tag ids as the yaml file BASENAMES (several `pastoral-*` files carry divergent inner ids — e.g. `pastoral-grief-and-loss.yaml` has inner id `grief-and-loss`); every tag in this document uses the basename.
2. **No tags are drawn from the three friends' condemned counsel.** The LORD's verdict — "you have not spoken of me the thing that is right, as my servant Job has" (42:7, repeated 42:8) — governs chapters 4–5, 8, 11, 15, 18, 20, 22, and 25. Considered and dropped on this ground: `the-lords-discipline` on Job 5 (5:17–18), `humble-exaltation` on Job 5 (5:11) and on Job 22 (22:29), `sin` ("reap what you sow") on Job 4 (4:8), `divine-judgment` on Jobs 15, 18, and 20, `repentance`/`prayer` on Job 11 (11:13–19) and Job 22 (22:21–27). Rationale: a tag routes real searches; sending "God's discipline" or "repentance" seekers to speeches the book's own verdict condemns — speeches misapplying true-sounding doctrine to crush an innocent man — would present the friends' retribution theology as the book's teaching. The single tag retained in those chapters (`dreams-and-visions` on Job 4) marks the presence of a vision narrative, not an endorsement of Eliphaz's use of it, and the justification attributes it. Note for reversal: the New Testament does cite one line of Eliphaz approvingly (Job 5:13 in 1 Corinthians 3:19), so a finer-grained policy is defensible; this draft takes the conservative default.
3. **Elihu is treated as a distinct voice, taggable with attribution.** The verdict of 42:7 is spoken to Eliphaz against him and "your two friends" — Elihu is neither condemned with them nor commended, and mainstream conservative treatment regards his speeches as nearer the truth and preparatory to the LORD's. Every tag whose justification rests on Elihu's words carries an "(Elihu speaking)" note — the complete list: `wisdom-from-god` and `pleasing-god-not-people` on 32; `dreams-and-visions`, `the-lords-discipline`, `repentance`, and `restoration` on 33; `providence` on 34; `the-lords-discipline` and `creation` on 36; `creation` and `providence` on 37. Reversible either way (stricter: drop Elihu tags like the friends'; looser: drop the attributions). *2026-08-25: the tag-application pass (item #16) added `mediator` and `redeemer` on 33, each carrying the same "(Elihu speaking)" attribution — the list above plus those two is now complete.* *2026-08-25, new-mint pass (item #18): `the-breath-of-life` on 32 (32:8–9) also carries the "(Elihu speaking)" attribution — the list now includes it as well.*
4. **No `pastoral-hope-in-despair` on Job 3 (or 6–7, 10, 17, 30).** The concept's pack deliberately anchors hope texts and its fixture asserts that despair narratives never rank for its crisis queries; Job 3 depicts the despair ("who long for death, but it doesn't come") without the concept's hope substance — the same shape as the Genesis-3/`resisting-the-devil` worked example. The lament theme is logged as a vocabulary gap instead — as an append of Job's refs to the shared log's existing `lament` row (Joel) — where the right routing can be designed deliberately.
5. **Job 19 carries `hope-in-god`, not `resurrection`.** The vocabulary's `resurrection` is "He is risen" — Christ's resurrection, NT-anchored — so tagging it here would be a later-revelation read-back. The classic Christian reading of 19:25–27 as resurrection hope (held by Brooks and Matthew Henry) is honored in the summary's wording ("After my skin is destroyed, then I will see God in my flesh" is quoted in full), and the redeemer-language gap is logged (`redeemer`).
6. **Subdivision: 5 of 42 chapters** (1, 2, 27, 40, 42) — only where BSB headings mark genuinely divergent themes (scene changes or speaker/verdict changes). Job 30 has two BSB headings (30:1, 30:15) but was kept whole: both halves are one continuous "but now" lament (mockery by men, then God's hand), not divergent themes. Reversible by splitting 30:1–14 / 30:15–31 with the BSB titles.
7. **`gods-protection` is NOT tagged on Job 1.** The hedge (1:10) is real in the narrative — but the chapter's action is God permitting the hedge to be breached; routing "God's protection" searches to the breach would mislead, the same shape as decision #4. Likewise `resisting-the-devil` is not tagged on Job 1–2: Job never confronts Satan and never learns of the heavenly scenes; the concept's James-4:7 substance (the believer resisting the devil) is absent even though Satan is present.
8. **`image-of-god` on Job 31 rests on 31:13–15 without the word "image."** The verses ground equal treatment of servants in shared creation by one Maker — the concept's human-dignity substance — though the "image" vocabulary is Genesis's. Judged honest, substantial presence; reversible.
