# Jeremiah — book summary

## Short

Jeremiah is the record of a long obedience under a falling kingdom: a priest's son from Anathoth, called as a boy in Josiah's days — "Before I formed you in the womb, I knew you" (1:5) — and set over nations "to uproot and to tear down, to destroy and to overthrow, to build and to plant" (1:10). For roughly four decades, through Judah's last kings, he pleads with a people who have traded "the spring of living waters" for "broken cisterns that can't hold water" (2:13), while prophets cry "Peace, peace!" when there is no peace; for saying so he is beaten, tried for his life, and left to sink in a dungeon's mire, and in his laments he argues with the God whose word is "a burning fire shut up in my bones" (20:9). The judgment he announces arrives on schedule — siege, famine, the temple burned, the people carried to Babylon for seventy years — yet at the book's summit stands its brightest promise: a new covenant, God's law written on hearts and sins remembered no more (31:31–34), sealed in advance by a field bought during the siege because "houses and fields and vineyards will yet again be bought in this land" (32:15). The closing oracles call every nation to account, Babylon last and longest; and the book's final scene — the exiled king Jehoiachin lifted from prison to eat at the king of Babylon's table — leaves judgment confirmed and hope quietly alive.

## Full

Jeremiah spans the last generation of the kingdom of Judah. The LORD's word comes to him "in the days of Josiah" from the king's thirteenth year, and keeps coming through Jehoiakim and Zedekiah "to the carrying away of Jerusalem captive" (1:2–3) — roughly forty years of preaching that fails to save the city, told by the man who had to deliver it. The opening chapter gives the whole book its shape: a prophet known and appointed before birth, a watching God who performs his word, disaster tipping out of the north, and a commission that is two-thirds demolition — "to uproot and to tear down, to destroy and to overthrow, to build and to plant" (1:10). Jeremiah is made "a fortified city, an iron pillar, and bronze walls" against kings, priests, and people; they will fight him and lose, because God is with him.

The first long movement (chapters 2–25) is the pleading. Judah has committed two evils — forsaking "the spring of living waters" and digging "broken cisterns that can't hold water" (2:13) — yet God keeps calling: return, acknowledge your iniquity, break up your fallow ground, amend your ways. In the temple gate Jeremiah tells the worshipers not to trust the chant "The LORD's temple, the LORD's temple, the LORD's temple, are these" (7:4): a house treated as "a den of robbers" will end like Shiloh. The nation's healers treat its wound "superficially, saying, 'Peace, peace!' when there is no peace" (6:14), and the diagnosis cuts to the bone — a heart "deceitful above all things" (17:9), a people no more able to do good than a leopard can change its spots. Threaded through these chapters are the prayers that earned Jeremiah the traditional nickname "the weeping prophet": he mourns with his people ("Is there no balm in Gilead?", 8:22), argues with his God ("Why does the way of the wicked prosper?", 12:1), asks whether God will fail him like "a deceitful brook" (15:18), and finds he cannot quit, because the word is "a burning fire shut up in my bones" (20:9). Sign-acts carry the message where words have worn out — the ruined belt, the potter's reworked clay, the jar smashed in the valley — and the cost keeps rising: the stocks, a death-penalty trial, plots from his own hometown. Judah's last kings are measured and sentenced in turn, false shepherds are promised a reckoning, and against them God pledges "a righteous Branch" for David, "The LORD our righteousness" (23:5–6). Chapter 25 dates the sentence: because no one listened for twenty-three years, "Nebuchadnezzar the king of Babylon, my servant" will take the land, "and these nations will serve the king of Babylon seventy years" (25:11) — and after Judah, every nation drinks the same cup.

The middle movement turns on the question of which prophets to believe. Wearing a wooden yoke, Jeremiah tells the nations and Zedekiah to submit to Babylon and live; the prophet Hananiah breaks the yoke and promises freedom within two years, and dies that same year — wooden bars traded for iron ones (28:13). To the exiles already in Babylon, Jeremiah writes the book's most surprising pastoral letter: "Build houses and dwell in them. Plant gardens and eat their fruit," seek the peace of the city that holds you captive — for the exile will run its seventy years, and God's thoughts toward his people are "thoughts of peace, and not of evil, to give you hope and a future" (29:11). Then, with Jerusalem's fall closing in, come four chapters of concentrated comfort (30–33): captivity reversed, mourning turned to joy, "I have loved you with an everlasting love" (31:3) — and the book's summit, the new-covenant promise: "I will make a new covenant with the house of Israel, and with the house of Judah... I will put my law in their inward parts, and I will write it in their heart... for I will forgive their iniquity, and I will remember their sin no more" (31:31–34) — words Christians have historically connected with Jesus' words over the cup at the Last Supper. While the siege ramps stand against the walls, God has the imprisoned prophet buy his cousin's field at Anathoth, sign and seal the deed, and store it in a clay jar — silver staked on the promise that "houses and fields and vineyards will yet again be bought in this land" (32:15).

The story of the word itself is told in chapter 36: twenty-three years of prophecy (25:3) dictated to Baruch, read aloud in the temple, and burned column by column in King Jehoiakim's brazier — then written again, "and many similar words were added to them" (36:32). The narratives that follow walk to the end: Zedekiah secretly asking for a word he will not obey, Jeremiah beaten, jailed, and lowered into the mire of a dungeon until Ebedmelech pulls him out with rags and worn-out garments, and at last, in Zedekiah's eleventh year, the breach in the wall — the king's sons slaughtered before his eyes, then those eyes put out, the city burned, the people deported. Even then the choosing does not stop: the remnant left in the land asks Jeremiah for God's way, vows obedience "whether it is good, or whether it is bad" (42:6), hears God's answer — remain in the land and be built, not plucked up — and marches to Egypt anyway, dragging the prophet along — where his last recorded sermon confronts refugees still burning incense to the queen of the sky, and one small chapter preserves God's personal kindness to weary Baruch: life itself, granted "wherever you go" (45:5).

The book then turns outward: oracles "concerning the nations" (46:1) — Egypt, Philistia, Moab, Ammon, Edom, Damascus, Kedar, Elam — each judged in turn — pride and cruelty the recurring charges — several with mercy deferred rather than denied ("I will reverse the captivity," 48:47), and the judge himself strangely grieved ("my heart sounds for Moab like flutes," 48:36). The collection crests against Babylon itself (50–51): the hammer of the whole earth is broken, for Israel's "Redeemer is strong. The LORD of Armies is his name" (50:34), and the written oracle is carried to Babylon, read aloud, tied to a stone, and sunk in the Euphrates — "Thus far are the words of Jeremiah" (51:64). A final chapter stands outside those words as history's countersignature: the fall retold, the temple stripped and burned, the deportations counted — and then, in the thirty-seventh year of the captivity, Jehoiachin released from prison to eat bread at the king's table "all the days of his life." Everything Jeremiah said came true, the uprooting and the tearing down; the building and the planting still stand promised, and a live coal of hope is carried into exile.

# The fifty-two chapters

## Jeremiah 1
**Summary:** The book opens by setting Jeremiah, a priest's son from Anathoth, under the LORD's word from the thirteenth year of Josiah until Jerusalem is carried away captive. God appoints him before birth — "Before I formed you in the womb, I knew you" (1:5) — a prophet to the nations, set "to uproot and to tear down, to destroy and to overthrow, to build and to plant" (1:10); when Jeremiah protests that he is only a child who doesn't know how to speak, the LORD touches his mouth and puts his own words there. Two visions then set the book's terms: an almond branch — "I watch over my word to perform it" (1:12) — and a boiling cauldron tipping from the north, the direction the coming disaster will break from. God makes the young prophet "a fortified city, an iron pillar, and bronze walls" against kings, princes, priests, and people: they will fight against him but not prevail, "for I am with you... to rescue you" (1:18–19).
**Tags:** `fear-not` — "Don't be afraid because of them, for I am with you to rescue you" (1:8), repeated as "Don't be dismayed at them" (1:17); `presence-of-god` — the call rests on God's own presence with his prophet: "I am with you to rescue you" (1:8), sealed again in the chapter's last words, "for I am with you... to rescue you" (1:19); `gods-protection` — Jeremiah made "a fortified city, an iron pillar, and bronze walls"; "they will not prevail against you" (1:18–19); `dreams-and-visions` — two vision-oracles: "I see a branch of an almond tree" and "I see a boiling cauldron; and it is tipping away from the north" (1:11–14); `power-of-gods-word` — the almond-branch vision's own meaning, God's direct statement about his word: "I watch over my word to perform it" (1:12)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings

## Jeremiah 2
**Summary:** God's first indictment begins by remembering the honeymoon — "the kindness of your youth, your love as a bride" (2:2) — and asking what wrong Israel's fathers ever found in him, that they "walked after worthless vanity, and have become worthless" (2:5). The charge comes to a point in a single picture: "they have forsaken me, the spring of living waters, and cut out cisterns for themselves: broken cisterns that can't hold water" (2:13) — a trade no pagan nation would make with gods that are no gods (2:11). Judah has "as many gods as you have towns" (2:28), yet in trouble will still cry to the LORD, "Arise, and save us!" (2:27). Running to Egypt and Assyria will only bring shame (2:36–37); the deeper sentence is built in: "Your own wickedness will correct you, and your backsliding will rebuke you" (2:19). Though the evidence is marked before God, the people insist "I have not sinned" — and it is for that very claim that God says, "I will judge you" (2:35).
**Tags:** `sin` — the "two evils": forsaking "the spring of living waters" for "broken cisterns that can't hold water" (2:13), with iniquity "marked before me" despite lye and soap (2:22); `divine-judgment` — "Therefore I will yet contend with you... and I will contend with your children's children" (2:9); "I will judge you" (2:35); `self-deception` — "How can you say, 'I am not defiled...'?" (2:23) and "you say, 'I have not sinned'" (2:35) against the recorded evidence; `the-lords-discipline` — correction built into the sin itself: "Your own wickedness will correct you, and your backsliding will rebuke you" (2:19), after strikes that "received no correction" (2:30); `idolatry` — the trade itself: the living God forsaken for "as many gods as you have towns" (2:28), cisterns dug for gods that are no gods (2:11–13, 27–28)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings

## Jeremiah 3
**Summary:** Starting from the law that a divorced and remarried wife cannot go back to her first husband, God presses the shock of his mercy: though Judah has "played the prostitute with many lovers," he still says "return again to me" (3:1). In a word from Josiah's days, backsliding Israel — already sent away with "a certificate of divorce" — proves "more righteous than treacherous Judah," whose return to God was "only in pretense" (3:8–11). Then comes one of the book's tenderest calls: "Return, you backsliding Israel... I will not look in anger on you, for I am merciful... I will not keep anger forever. Only acknowledge your iniquity" (3:12–13). God promises "shepherds according to my heart," a Jerusalem called "The LORD's Throne" to which the nations gather, and days when the ark of the LORD's covenant will not even be missed (3:14–18). The chapter closes with the sound of weeping on the bare heights and a modeled confession: "Truly the salvation of Israel is in the LORD our God... we have sinned against the LORD our God" (3:21–25).
**Tags:** `repentance` — "Return, you backsliding Israel... Only acknowledge your iniquity" (3:12–13), and the penitents' own words, "we have sinned against the LORD our God" (3:25); `forgiveness-of-sins` — "I will not look in anger on you, for I am merciful... I will not keep anger forever" (3:12); `restoration` — "I will take one of you from a city, and two from a family, and I will bring you to Zion," with shepherds, a renamed Jerusalem, and reunited Judah and Israel (3:14–18)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings
**Sections:**
- **3:1–5 — The Wages of the Prostitute** (title: adapted from BSB) — By the divorce law's own logic Israel's return should be impossible, yet God invites it; the withheld showers expose a people with "a prostitute's forehead" who "refused to be ashamed" (3:3).
- **3:6–10 — Judah Follows Israel's Example** (title: BSB) — Judah watched her sister Israel sent away for adultery, yet "had no fear" and repeated it, returning to God only in pretense.
- **3:11–25 — A Call to Repentance** (title: BSB) — God calls the backsliders home with promises of mercy, true shepherds, and a gathered Jerusalem, and the section ends in Israel's voiced confession of sin.

## Jeremiah 4
**Summary:** If Israel will truly return and put away its abominations, the nations themselves will bless themselves in the LORD (4:1–2); to Judah the plea sharpens: "Break up your fallow ground," and "Circumcise yourselves to the LORD, and take away the foreskins of your heart" (4:3–4). Then the alarm sounds: evil from the north, "a lion... from his thicket, and a destroyer of nations" on his way (4:6–7), while Jerusalem is urged, "wash your heart from wickedness, that you may be saved" (4:14). Jeremiah cannot hold his peace — "My anguish, my anguish! I am pained at my very heart!" (4:19) — and sees the land undone in a vision: earth "waste and void," heavens without light, mountains trembling, no man, the cities broken down before the LORD's fierce anger (4:23–26). Yet even inside the decree of desolation stands the reservation, "yet I will not make a full end" (4:27). The chapter closes with the daughter of Zion gasping like a woman bearing her first child, her lovers turned murderers (4:30–31).
**Tags:** `repentance` — "If you will return, Israel... return to me"; "Break up your fallow ground"; "wash your heart from wickedness, that you may be saved" (4:1, 3, 14); `divine-judgment` — "I will bring evil from the north, and a great destruction" (4:6), a sentence spoken, planned, and not turned back (4:12, 28), yet bounded: "I will not make a full end" (4:27); `lament` — the prophet's anguish poured out: "My anguish, my anguish! I am pained at my very heart!" (4:19–21); `hardness-of-heart` — the heart named as the problem to be cut away: "Circumcise yourselves to the LORD, and take away the foreskins of your heart" (4:3–4); `remnant` — the survivor-reservation inside the decree of desolation, in God's own words: "yet I will not make a full end" (4:27)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings
**Sections:**
- **4:1–4 — A Plea to Return** (title: BSB) — Genuine return — abominations put away, hearts circumcised — would avert the wrath that burns like unquenchable fire and would bless the nations.

## Jeremiah 5
**Summary:** God invites a search through Jerusalem's streets for a single person "who does justly, who seeks truth" — for one, "I will pardon her" (5:1) — but the poor prove ignorant of the LORD's way, and the great, who know it, "have broken the yoke, and burst the bonds" (5:4–5). The people flatly deny the LORD, saying "It is not he. Evil won't come on us" (5:12) — so God will make his words fire in Jeremiah's mouth and bring a mighty, ancient nation whose language Judah does not know (5:14–15), though "even in those days... I will not make a full end of you" (5:18). The sea keeps the boundary of sand God decreed for it, but "this people has a revolting and a rebellious heart" (5:22–23), never saying, "Let's now fear the LORD our God, who gives rain, both the former and the latter, in its season" (5:24). Among them are wicked men whose houses are full of deceit as a cage is full of birds — and so they have grown great and rich — yet they "don't plead the cause, the cause of the fatherless... and they don't defend the rights of the needy" (5:26–28). God asks, "Shouldn't I punish them for these things?" (5:9) — a question that returns at 5:29 — and the chapter ends on a horrifying partnership: prophets prophesying falsely, priests ruling by their own authority, "and my people love to have it so" (5:31).

## Jeremiah 6
**Summary:** The siege is pictured as already gathering: shepherd-invaders pitch their tents around the daughter of Zion, and it is the LORD of Armies (the WEB's rendering of the traditional "LORD of hosts") himself who orders them to "cast up a mound against Jerusalem," a city that "produces her wickedness" the way a well produces water (6:3, 6–7). From least to greatest "everyone is given to covetousness," and prophet and priest have healed the people's hurt "superficially, saying, 'Peace, peace!' when there is no peace" — and they have forgotten how to blush (6:13–15). God sets out the alternative: "ask for the old paths, 'Where is the good way?' and walk in it, and you will find rest for your souls" — and they answer, "We will not walk in it" (6:16). With the law rejected, their imported frankincense and sacrifices are unacceptable (6:19–20), and a cruel people from the north country, without mercy, rides against Zion (6:22–23). The chapter ends at the refiner's fire: Jeremiah is made "a tester of metals" among the people, the bellows blow, and the verdict comes back "rejected silver, because the LORD has rejected them" (6:27–30).
**Tags:** `divine-judgment` — the LORD directs the siege ("Cut down trees, and cast up a mound against Jerusalem," 6:6) and brings "evil on this people, even the fruit of their thoughts" (6:19), down to the final assay: "rejected silver" (6:30); `sin` — "As a well produces its waters, so she produces her wickedness" (6:7); "from their least even to their greatest, everyone is given to covetousness" (6:13); `rest-for-the-weary` — God's genuine offer, though refused: "ask for the old paths... walk in it, and you will find rest for your souls" (6:16); `false-prophets` — the people's hurt healed "superficially, saying, 'Peace, peace!' when there is no peace" by prophet and priest who have forgotten how to blush (6:13–15)

## Jeremiah 7
**Summary:** Standing in the gate of the LORD's house, Jeremiah tells the worshipers streaming in not to trust the chant "The LORD's temple, the LORD's temple, the LORD's temple, are these" (7:4): only amended ways — real justice between neighbors, no oppression of "the foreigner, the fatherless, and the widow," no walking after other gods — secure their place in the land (7:5–7). To steal, murder, commit adultery, swear falsely, and burn incense to Baal, and then stand in the house called by God's name saying "We are delivered," has made that house "a den of robbers" in their eyes; God points them to Shiloh, where his name once dwelt, destroyed for the same wickedness (7:9–14). Whole families join the idolatry — children gathering wood, fathers kindling fire, women kneading dough for "cakes to the queen of the sky" (7:18 — our WEB translation's rendering of the familiar 'queen of heaven') — and God tells Jeremiah not even to pray for this people (7:16). What he commanded when he brought them out of Egypt was never sacrifice first, but "Listen to my voice, and I will be your God, and you shall be my people" (7:22–23) — a voice they and their fathers have refused since. Because Judah has even burned its sons and daughters at Topheth — "which I didn't command, nor did it come into my mind" (7:31) — that valley will be renamed "The valley of Slaughter," and the voice of the bridegroom and the bride will cease from Jerusalem's streets (7:32–34).

## Jeremiah 18
**Summary:** Sent down to the potter's house, Jeremiah watches a vessel marred in the potter's hand be made "again another vessel, as seemed good to the potter" (18:4), and the word comes: "as the clay in the potter's hand, so are you in my hand, house of Israel" (18:6). God spells out how he deals with any nation: if a nation he has threatened turns from evil, "I will repent of the evil that I thought to do" — the WEB's wording for God relenting, turning back from an announced course (18:8) — and promised good is likewise withdrawn from a nation that turns to evil; so Judah is urged, "Everyone return from his evil way now" (18:11). They answer, "It is in vain; for we will walk after our own plans" (18:12) — a horrible thing without parallel among the nations — so God will scatter them "as with an east wind" (18:17). ## Jeremiah 25

## Jeremiah 27
**Summary:** The LORD tells Jeremiah to make a yoke — "bonds and bars" — and wear it on his own neck, then send word by the envoys gathered at Zedekiah's court to the kings of Edom, Moab, Ammon, Tyre, and Sidon (the chapter's heading dates the word to the beginning of Jehoiakim's reign, though its action addresses Zedekiah). The message rests on a sweeping claim: the LORD of Armies made the earth and everything on it "by my great power and by my outstretched arm," gives it "to whom it seems right to me," and has now given all these lands to "Nebuchadnezzar the king of Babylon, my servant" — so any nation that refuses the yoke will be punished with sword, famine, and pestilence, while the nation that submits will stay in its own land (27:5–11). Jeremiah presses the same word on Zedekiah — "Bring your necks under the yoke of the king of Babylon, and serve him and his people, and live" (27:12) — and on the priests and people, warning them all not to listen to their prophets, diviners, dreams, soothsayers, or sorcerers who say otherwise, "for they prophesy a lie to you" (27:9–10, 14–16). The chapter opens the book's great collision between true and false prophecy, which chapter 28 brings to a head.

## Jeremiah 28
**Summary:** In the fourth year of Zedekiah, the prophet Hananiah of Gibeon publicly contradicts Jeremiah in the LORD's house: "I have broken the yoke of the king of Babylon," he announces in God's name — within two full years the temple vessels, the exiled king Jeconiah, and all the captives will come home (28:2–4). Jeremiah answers "Amen! May the LORD do so" — he would love it to be true — but reminds everyone that the prophets of old prophesied war and disaster, so a prophet of peace is proven only "when the word of the prophet happens" (28:6–9). Hananiah then takes the wooden bar from off Jeremiah's neck and breaks it, and Jeremiah simply walks away — until the LORD sends him back with a harder word: "You have broken the bars of wood, but you have made in their place bars of iron," for God has put an iron yoke of service to Babylon on all these nations (28:10–14). The verdict on Hananiah is direct — "The LORD has not sent you, but you make this people trust in a lie... This year you will die, because you have spoken rebellion against the LORD" — and he dies that same year, in the seventh month (28:15–17). # Decisions record — 2026-08-23 assembly

Every delegated judgment call from the four drafting ranges plus the assembly pass, one line each, each a reversible delegated default Jesse can overturn.

**Structure and subdivision**

1. **Subdivision rate: 32 of 52 chapters** (by range: 8/13, 10/13, 7/13, 7/13) against Genesis's 14/50 — deliberate, not drift: Jeremiah's chapters are typically anthologies of separate oracles, speakers, and sign-acts (ch 22 holds five royal oracles; ch 49 five nations), where Genesis chapters mostly carry one narrative unit; every boundary sits on a BSB anchor and a real thematic break, and every chapter left whole despite an available anchor is argued below (items 2–8). Reverse by naming any chapter to merge or split.
2. **Jeremiah 2 not subdivided** — anchors at 2:14 and 2:23 exist, but all three BSB headings name facets of one continuous indictment oracle. Reverse by splitting 2:1–13 / 2:14–22 / 2:23–37.
3. **Jeremiah 5 not subdivided** — anchor at 5:14 exists; the failed search for one just person (5:1–13) flows directly into the sentence it triggers as one indictment-to-sentence unit. Reverse by splitting 5:1–13 / 5:14–31.
4. **Jeremiah 6 not subdivided** — anchor at 6:22 exists; the whole chapter is one siege-warning oracle, 6:22–30 continuing the same northern-invasion material. Reverse by splitting 6:1–21 / 6:22–30.
5. **Jeremiah 14 not subdivided** — anchor at 14:19 exists ("A Prayer for Mercy"), but the chapter is one continuous drought dialogue of plea and refusal. Reverse by splitting 14:1–18 / 14:19–22.
6. **Jeremiah 35 not subdivided** — anchor at 35:12 exists; the Rechabites' scene and the rebuke of Judah are setup and application of one object lesson. Reverse by splitting 35:1–11 / 35:12–19.
7. **Jeremiah 37 not subdivided** — anchor at 37:11 exists; one continuous narrative of the siege lull (false hope punctured, then arrest and imprisonment). Reverse by splitting 37:1–10 / 37:11–21.
8. **Jeremiah 46 (and similarly 48) not subdivided** — ch 46 has three real movements (Carchemish, the invasion of Egypt, the Jacob comfort) but the staged BSB file carries only the 46:1 anchor, and CONVENTIONS §4 forbids invented boundaries; the movements are narrated in the summary instead.
