# Revelation — book summary

## Short

Revelation is the Bible’s last book and its own title explains it: an unveiling. Jesus Christ gives John, exiled on the island of Patmos, a vision of “the things which must happen soon” — first letters of warning and promise to seven assemblies (the WEB’s word for the familiar “churches”), then a throne room where every creature worships God and the slain Lamb, who alone is worthy to open a book sealed shut with seven seals (“book” is the WEB’s word where many translations say “scroll”). Cycles of seals, trumpets, and bowls unveil God’s judgments on a rebellious world; a dragon and two beasts make war on the saints; Babylon the great falls; and Christ appears as the rider called Faithful and True. The book ends where the Bible’s story ends: a new heaven and a new earth, God dwelling with his people, every tear wiped away — and the promise Jesus repeats three times in the closing chapter: “Behold, I am coming soon!” and, at the last, “Yes, I am coming soon.”

## Full

Revelation presents itself, in its opening sentence, as “the Revelation of Jesus Christ, which God gave him to show to his servants the things which must happen soon” (1:1) — and it attaches a blessing to being read aloud: “Blessed is he who reads and those who hear the words of the prophecy, and keep the things that are written in it” (1:3). It is a prophecy meant for congregations to hear and keep, not a puzzle meant to be decoded, and it closes the same way it opens, with Jesus’ own repeated pledge — “Behold, I am coming soon!” (22:7, 12), sealed at the last with “Yes, I am coming soon.” (22:20).

The book unfolds in great movements. John, “your brother and partner with you in the oppression, Kingdom, and perseverance in Christ Jesus” (1:9), sees the risen Christ walking among seven golden lamp stands — which Christ himself explains are seven assemblies. Chapters 2–3 carry seven letters to those assemblies in Asia: honest diagnoses of abandoned love, faithfulness under persecution, tolerated false teaching, deadness, endurance, and lukewarm self-satisfaction, each carrying a promise to “him who overcomes”.

Then a door opens in heaven. Chapters 4–5 are the book’s hinge: a throne, ceaseless worship of the Creator, and a book sealed shut with seven seals, which no one in heaven or earth is worthy to open, until the Lion who is of the tribe of Judah appears as “a Lamb standing, as though it had been slain” (5:6). From the Lamb’s opening of the seals flow the book’s three judgment cycles — seven seals (6–8), seven trumpets (8–11), and seven bowls (15–16) — interrupted again and again by scenes of God sealing, sheltering, and vindicating his people. Chapters 12–14 pull the curtain back further: a dragon, identified in the text as the devil and Satan, wars against a woman, her child who is “to rule all the nations” (12:5), and “the rest of her offspring, who keep God’s commandments and hold Jesus’ testimony” (12:17), aided by a beast from the sea and a beast from the earth who enforce false worship and mark those who buy and sell.

The final movements resolve everything. Babylon the great — pictured as a wealthy, seductive, violent city drunk with the blood of the saints — falls in a single hour, and heaven answers with a fourfold “Hallelujah!” (17–19). Heaven opens and the rider called Faithful and True — named “The Word of God” and “KING OF KINGS AND LORD OF LORDS” — defeats the beast and the false prophet (19). Satan is bound a thousand years, then released, then finally thrown into the lake of fire; the dead stand before a great white throne and are judged from the books (20). And then the end for which the whole Bible has waited: “a new heaven and a new earth,” the holy city New Jerusalem coming down out of heaven, and the announcement, “Behold, God’s dwelling is with people... He will wipe away every tear from their eyes. Death will be no more” (21:1, 3–4).

Revelation states its own purpose and comfort throughout, in the songs that punctuate the visions: God reigns; the Lamb has overcome and “bought us for God with your blood out of every tribe, language, people, and nation” (5:9); the saints’ blood is not forgotten and God’s judgments are “true and righteous” (19:2); and God will dwell with his people forever. The book asks its hearers for exactly what its hard chapters keep repeating — “Here is the endurance and the faith of the saints” (13:10) — and its last word is an invitation and a prayer: “He who is thirsty, let him come... Amen! Yes, come, Lord Jesus!” (22:17, 20).

# The twenty-two chapters

## Revelation 1
**Summary:** The book announces itself: a revelation from Jesus Christ, delivered by his angel to John, with a blessing on all who read it aloud, hear it, and keep it. John, exiled on Patmos “because of God’s Word and the testimony of Jesus Christ”, greets the seven assemblies of Asia in the name of the eternal God, the seven Spirits before his throne, and Jesus Christ, who “washed us from our sins by his blood” and is coming with the clouds. Then John turns and sees the risen Christ among seven golden lamp stands — blazing eyes, a voice like many waters, a sharp two-edged sword from his mouth — and falls at his feet like a dead man. Christ lays his right hand on him with “Don’t be afraid”, declares himself the first and the last who died and is alive forever, and commands him to write; he explains the mystery himself: the seven stars are the angels of the seven assemblies, and the seven lamp stands are the assemblies.
**Tags:** `second-coming` — “Behold, he is coming with the clouds, and every eye will see him” (1:7); `deity-of-christ` — the doxology (a short burst of praise) is Christ’s — “to him be the glory and the dominion forever and ever” (1:5–6) — and he declares “I am the first and the last, and the Living one”, holding “the keys of Death and of Hades” (1:17–18); `resurrection` — “I was dead, and behold, I am alive forever and ever” (1:18); `the-cross` — “To him who loves us, and washed us from our sins by his blood” (1:5); `obedience-to-the-word` — the book’s opening blessing is for those who “hear the words of the prophecy, and keep the things that are written in it” (1:3); `dreams-and-visions` — John “in the Spirit on the Lord’s day” is shown what he must “write in a book and send to the seven assemblies” (1:10–11); `the-first-and-the-last` — the risen Jesus claims Isaiah’s divine self-title: “I am the first and the last, and the Living one. I was dead, and behold, I am alive forever and ever” (1:17–18), after the Lord God’s “I am the Alpha and the Omega” (1:8; both fixture-verified; honest scope carried from the concept map: many readers take 1:8’s speaker as the Father — the identification with Jesus rests on 1:17–18)

**Sections:**
- **1:1–3 — Prologue** (title: BSB) — The book names itself a revelation of Jesus Christ about “the things which must happen soon,” and blesses the one who reads it aloud and those who hear and keep it.
- **1:4–8 — John Greets the Seven Churches** (title: BSB) — A greeting of grace and peace from the eternal God, the seven Spirits, and Jesus Christ — faithful witness, firstborn of the dead, ruler of the kings of the earth — with a doxology to him “who loves us, and washed us from our sins by his blood”, and a promise that he is coming with the clouds.
- **1:9–20 — John’s Vision on Patmos** (title: BSB) — On Patmos, John sees one like a son of man among seven golden lamp stands and falls as dead; Christ lays his right hand on him with “Don’t be afraid”, commissions him to write, and interprets the stars and lamp stands as the angels and the assemblies.

## Revelation 2
**Summary:** The risen Christ dictates the first four of seven letters, each opening with a title drawn from John’s vision of him, each declaring “I know your works”, and each carrying a promise to “him who overcomes”. Ephesus has toil, perseverance, and doctrinal vigilance, but has left its first love and must repent; suffering, impoverished Smyrna is called rich and told, “Don’t be afraid... Be faithful to death, and I will give you the crown of life.” Pergamum holds fast to Christ’s name where “Satan’s throne is” — Antipas was killed there — but tolerates the teaching of Balaam and the Nicolaitans; Thyatira grows in love and service yet tolerates “Jezebel,” who seduces Christ’s servants into sexual immorality and idol food. The pattern is pastoral, not abstract: Christ sees each congregation exactly as it is, warns, and promises — the tree of life, exemption from the second death, hidden manna and a white stone, authority over the nations and the morning star.

Be faithful to death, and I will give you the crown of life” (2:10), and Antipas, “my witness, my faithful one, who was killed among you” (2:13); `heavenly-reward` — the overcomer promises: “the tree of life” (2:7), “the crown of life” (2:10), “the hidden manna” and “a white stone” (2:17), “authority over the nations” and “the morning star” (2:26–28); `victory-in-christ` — the fourfold refrain to “him who overcomes” (2:7, 11, 17, 26); `fear-not` — “Don’t be afraid of the things which you are about to suffer” (2:10); `false-teachers` — tolerated deceivers named and judged: the teaching of Balaam and the Nicolaitans at Pergamum, and Thyatira’s “Jezebel,” whom Christ will judge so that “all the assemblies will know that I am he who searches the minds and hearts” (2:14–15, 20–24)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings
**Sections:**
- **2:1–7 — To the Church in Ephesus** (title: BSB) — Commended for toil, perseverance, and testing false apostles, Ephesus is charged with having left its first love and called to remember, repent, and do the first works, lest its lamp stand be removed.
- **2:8–11 — To the Church in Smyrna** (title: BSB) — To the persecuted and poor (“but you are rich”), the first and the last who died and lives promises that faithfulness to death wins the crown of life, and the overcomer will not be harmed by the second death.
- **2:12–17 — To the Church in Pergamum** (title: BSB) — Faithful under pressure where “Satan’s throne is”, Pergamum nevertheless harbors the teaching of Balaam and the Nicolaitans and must repent; the overcomer receives hidden manna and a white stone with a new name.
- **2:18–29 — To the Church in Thyatira** (title: BSB) — Thyatira’s last works exceed its first, but it tolerates “Jezebel,” whom Christ will judge so that “all the assemblies will know that I am he who searches the minds and hearts”; the faithful need only hold fast until he comes.

## Revelation 3
**Summary:** The letters continue to Sardis, Philadelphia, and Laodicea. Sardis has “a reputation of being alive,” but Christ pronounces it dead and tells it to wake up, strengthen what remains, and repent — though a few there have not defiled their garments and will walk with him in white. Philadelphia, with only “a little power,” has kept Christ’s word and not denied his name; before it stands “an open door, which no one can shut”, and its overcomers will be made pillars in God’s temple, bearing God’s name and the name of the new Jerusalem. Laodicea is neither cold nor hot; wealthy in its own eyes and wretched in Christ’s, it hears the book’s tenderest and sternest words in one breath — “As many as I love, I reprove and chasten... Behold, I stand at the door and knock” — and the promises crest in the highest of all: to sit with Christ on his throne, as he overcame and sat down with his Father.

- **3:7–13 — To the Church in Philadelphia** (title: BSB) — The holder of the key of David sets an open door before a faithful assembly of little power, promises to keep it “from the hour of testing which is to come on the whole world”, and will write on the overcomer the name of God and of the new Jerusalem.
- **3:14–22 — To the Church in Laodicea** (title: BSB) — Lukewarm Laodicea, rich in its own account, is counseled to buy true gold, white garments, and eye salve from Christ, who rebukes because he loves and stands at the door knocking, ready to dine with whoever opens.

## Revelation 4
**Summary:** A door opens in heaven, and John is summoned up “in the Spirit” to be shown “the things which must happen after this” — and what he sees first is a throne, and one sitting on it with the appearance of jasper and sardius, ringed by an emerald rainbow. Around the throne sit twenty-four elders in white with golden crowns; lightnings and thunders proceed from it, seven lamps of fire — the seven Spirits of God — burn before it, and a sea like glass spreads out in front. Four living creatures — like a lion, a calf, a man’s face, a flying eagle — never stop crying “Holy, holy, holy is the Lord God, the Almighty, who was and who is and who is to come!” And whenever they do, the elders fall down, throw their crowns before the throne, and declare God worthy of glory, honor, and power, “for you created all things”. Before a single seal is opened, the book fixes its center: heaven’s business is worship.

**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings

## Revelation 5
**Summary:** In the right hand of the one on the throne John sees a book written inside and out, “sealed shut with seven seals”, and a mighty angel asks, “Who is worthy to open the book, and to break its seals?” No one in heaven, on earth, or under the earth is found, and John weeps much, until an elder tells him that the Lion who is of the tribe of Judah has overcome. But when John looks, he sees “a Lamb standing, as though it had been slain” — the book’s defining image: the conqueror is the crucified one. The Lamb takes the book, and worship breaks in waves: the living creatures and elders with harps and bowls of incense (“which are the prayers of the saints”) sing a new song — “you were killed, and bought us for God with your blood out of every tribe, language, people, and nation” — then a numberless host of angels — “ten thousands of ten thousands, and thousands of thousands” — answers “Worthy is the Lamb”, until every created thing joins in and the elders fall down and worship.

## Revelation 6
**Summary:** The Lamb begins opening the seals, and at each of the first four a living creature cries “Come and see!” — the second says simply “Come!” — and a horse rides out: a white horse whose rider goes out “conquering, and to conquer”; a red horse whose rider takes peace from the earth; a black horse whose rider carries a balance while a voice announces scarcity prices for wheat and barley; and a pale horse whose rider is named Death, with Hades following, given authority over a fourth of the earth. The fifth seal shifts the scene: under the altar are the souls of those killed for God’s Word, crying “How long, Master, the holy and true, until you judge and avenge our blood on those who dwell on the earth?” — they are given white robes and told that they should rest yet for a while, until their fellow servants and their brothers, who would also be killed even as they were, should complete their course. The sixth seal shakes creation itself — earthquake, blackened sun, falling stars, sky rolled up like a scroll — and everyone from kings to slaves hides in the rocks, begging to be hidden “from the wrath of the Lamb, for the great day of his wrath has come, and who is able to stand?”

## Revelation 7
**Summary:** Between the sixth and seventh seals, judgment pauses: four angels hold back the four winds while another angel ascends with “the seal of the living God”, crying, “Don’t harm the earth, the sea, or the trees, until we have sealed the bondservants of our God on their foreheads” (bondservants — the WEB’s word for servants bound to their master, here God’s own people). John hears the number sealed — one hundred forty-four thousand, twelve thousand from each named tribe of Israel. Then he looks and sees what no one could count: “a great multitude... out of every nation and of all tribes, peoples, and languages,” in white robes with palm branches, crying “Salvation be to our God, who sits on the throne, and to the Lamb!” An elder explains who they are — those who came out of the great suffering, whose robes were washed white in the Lamb’s blood — and the chapter closes with the book’s first great comfort: the Lamb will shepherd them to springs of life-giving waters — “And God will wipe away every tear from their eyes.”

## Revelation 8
**Summary:** The Lamb opens the seventh seal, and heaven falls silent for about half an hour — a hush before the next cycle begins. Seven angels are given seven trumpets, but first another angel stands at the altar with a golden censer — “The smoke of the incense, with the prayers of the saints, went up before God” — then the censer is filled with altar fire and thrown to the earth. The first four trumpets strike creation in thirds: hail and fire burn a third of the earth, a burning mountain turns a third of the sea to blood, the star Wormwood embitters a third of the waters, and a third of sun, moon, and stars is darkened. An eagle flying in mid heaven cries “Woe! Woe! Woe to those who dwell on the earth” because three trumpet blasts remain — and the chapter’s quiet claim is easy to miss: the judgments go out from the same altar where the saints’ prayers are heard.

## Revelation 9
**Summary:** The fifth trumpet — the first woe — opens the pit of the abyss: out of its smoke come locusts like armored war-horses, ruled by the angel of the abyss (Abaddon in Hebrew, Apollyon in Greek), permitted to torment for five months “only those people who don’t have God’s seal on their foreheads”, but not to kill; in those days “people will seek death, and will in no way find it.” The sixth trumpet frees four angels bound at the Euphrates, and an army of two hundred million horsemen kills a third of mankind by fire, smoke, and sulfur. ## Revelation 20

## Revelation 22
**Summary:** The vision closes in a garden-city: a river of water of life flows from the throne of God and of the Lamb, with the tree of life bearing twelve kinds of fruit, its leaves “for the healing of the nations.” There is no curse any more; God’s servants “will see his face,” his name on their foreheads, and they will reign forever in his light. Then the book turns to its hearers: the words are faithful and true, the prophecy is not to be sealed because “the time is at hand”, and Jesus himself speaks — “Behold, I am coming soon! My reward is with me, to repay to each man according to his work. I am the Alpha and the Omega, the First and the Last, the Beginning and the End.” Blessing rests on those who do his commandments and enter the city; the Spirit and the bride say “Come!”, and whoever is thirsty may “take the water of life freely.” A solemn warning guards the book against addition or subtraction, Jesus repeats “Yes, I am coming soon”, John answers with the Bible’s closing prayer — “Amen! 2. **No interpretive system assigned (owner’s rule, applied).** Brooks (1919) reads Revelation on a dispensational timetable — rapture before the tribulation, the woman of ch. 12 as Israel, Babylon as Rome — and Matthew Henry reads symbols ecclesially. Neither frame is adopted anywhere: summaries report only the text’s own self-interpretations (1:20; 12:9; 17:9–18) and otherwise describe the visions. Chapter 20 reports the thousand-years sequence exactly as written, with one sentence noting that its relation to the present age is answered “in several ways” by the church — signposted, not adjudicated. The beast, Babylon, and 666 are described and left unidentified, as the text leaves them. Reversible only by the owner amending his framing rule.

3. **`second-coming` on chapter 19.** The text does not use the word “coming” of the rider, but he bears Christ’s own titles from within this book — “Faithful and True” (cf. the letters’ speaker), “The Word of God”, “KING OF KINGS AND LORD OF LORDS” (19:11, 13, 16) — and the chapter presents heaven opening and Christ riding out in judgment.
