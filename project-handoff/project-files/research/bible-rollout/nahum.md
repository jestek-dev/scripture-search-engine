## Short

Nahum is a revelation about Nineveh — the announcement that the capital of the
Assyrian empire, the superpower that had crushed nations for generations, is
about to fall. About a century earlier, in Jonah's day, Nineveh had repented and
been spared; now its cruelty has returned in full, and this time there is no
reprieve. The book opens with a poem about who the LORD is — "slow to anger, and
great in power," yet one who "will by no means leave the guilty unpunished" —
and then narrates the city's destruction in some of the Bible's most vivid
battle poetry. For Judah, long under Assyria's yoke, the same verdict is good
news: the prophet's name means "comfort," and his message to the oppressed is
that their oppressor's grip is broken.

## Full

Nahum introduces itself in one line: "A revelation about Nineveh. The book of
the vision of Nahum the Elkoshite" (1:1). Nineveh was the capital of Assyria,
the empire whose armies had destroyed the northern kingdom of Israel and
squeezed Judah for tribute — a byword for conquest and cruelty across the whole
region. The entire book is God's verdict on that city. It is best read alongside
Jonah: about a century before, the generation of Nineveh that heard Jonah
repented and was spared. Nahum shows the other side of the same God. Mercy
received once is not immunity forever; the city that returned to violence now
hears its sentence.

The book opens not with Nineveh but with God. Nahum 1 is a poem about the LORD's
character: "The LORD is a jealous God and avenges... The LORD is slow to anger,
and great in power, and will by no means leave the guilty unpunished" (1:2–3).
Sea, rivers, and mountains cannot stand before him — "Who can endure the
fierceness of his anger?" (1:6). Yet the same poem holds its one bright
verse: "The LORD is good, a stronghold in the day of trouble; and he knows those
who take refuge in him" (1:7). Both lines describe one God. His patience is not
indifference, and his judgment is not temper; the wrath that terrifies Nineveh
is the very thing that shelters the people Nineveh has been crushing.

From there the vision turns cinematic. Chapter 2 watches the city fall in
present tense — chariots raging in the streets, the river gates thrown open, the
palace dissolving, the population draining away like water from a pool,
plunder without end. Chapter 3 pronounces the reason: "Woe to the bloody city!
It is all full of lies and robbery" (3:1). Nineveh had seduced and sold nations;
now God himself says, twice, "Behold, I am against you" (2:13; 3:5), and the
empire that showed no pity finds no mourners. The book ends with the whole world
applauding the news of its fall: "for who hasn't felt your endless cruelty?"
(3:19).

Nahum's name means "comfort," and that is the book's purpose. It never calls
Judah to arms; it calls Judah to trust that the Judge of the whole earth has
seen, and to celebrate: "Behold, on the mountains the feet of him who brings
good news, who publishes peace! Keep your feasts, Judah! Perform your vows, for
the wicked one will no more pass through you" (1:15). To readers who have never
been under an empire's boot, Nahum can sound harsh; to the oppressed it reads as
it was written — the promise that cruelty does not get the last word, because
the LORD is slow to anger but not blind, good to those who take refuge in him
and unwilling to leave the guilty unpunished.

## Nahum 1
**Summary:** The book announces itself as a revelation about Nineveh, seen by
Nahum the Elkoshite, and opens with a poem about God himself: the LORD is
jealous and avenging, "slow to anger, and great in power," one who "will by no
means leave the guilty unpunished" — sea, rivers, and mountains cannot stand
before his indignation. At the poem's center stands its one verse of shelter:
"The LORD is good, a stronghold in the day of trouble; and he knows those who
take refuge in him" (1:7). Then the vision turns to its two audiences. To
Nineveh, which has plotted evil against the LORD: he will make a full end, cut
off the idols, and make its grave, "for you are vile" (1:14). To Judah:
the yoke will be broken and the bonds burst, and a herald on the mountains
brings good news of peace — keep your feasts, for the wicked one will no more
pass through (1:15).
**Tags:** `divine-judgment` — the avenging-God poem itself: "The LORD takes
vengeance on his adversaries" (1:2), one who "will by no means leave the guilty
unpunished" (1:3), "Who can stand before his indignation?" (1:6) — and the
sentence passed on Nineveh: "he will make a full end" (1:8–9), "I will make your
grave, for you are vile" (1:14); `refuge-in-trouble` — "The LORD is good, a
stronghold in the day of trouble; and he knows those who take refuge in him"
(1:7) — refuge held out inside the storm of judgment; `slow-to-anger` — the
formula's own text in this book: "The LORD is slow to anger, and great in
power, and will by no means leave the guilty unpunished" (1:3) — patience and
justice stated in one breath; `vengeance` — vengeance named as the LORD's own
prerogative, not man's: "The LORD takes vengeance on his adversaries, and he
maintains wrath against his enemies" (1:2–3)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings

## Nahum 2
**Summary:** The assault on Nineveh unfolds in present-tense battle poetry: he
who dashes in pieces has come up, and the call to man the defenses — "Keep the
fortress! Watch the way!" (2:1) — is already hopeless.
Chariots flash like torches and rage through the streets, the river gates are
opened, the palace dissolves, and the city's people drain away like water from a
pool — "'Stop! Stop!' they cry, but no one looks back" (2:8). The
plunderers strip silver and gold without end, and hearts melt and knees knock. A
taunt closes the chapter in the text's own image for Nineveh, a lions' den:
where now is the den of the lions, where the lion filled his caves with prey and
no one made them afraid? "'Behold, I am against you,'
says the LORD of Armies" (2:13) — the fall is not merely military misfortune but
the LORD's own doing, and in it he "restores the excellency of Jacob" (2:2).
**Tags:** `divine-judgment` — the sack of the city is the LORD's own sentence
being carried out, sealed in his own voice: "'Behold, I am against you,' says
the LORD of Armies, 'and I will burn her chariots in the smoke... and the voice
of your messengers will no longer be heard'" (2:13); the emptying of the city
(2:8–10) carries out the sentence announced in the opening vision; `justice-and-oppression` — the oppressed-by-empire register: the lions'-den taunt
names Nineveh's predation — the den where the lion filled his caves with prey
(2:11–12) — and God's answer to it, "'Behold, I am against you,' says the LORD
of Armies" (2:13). (The 2026-08-23 note "Only one honest tag from the current
vocabulary" described the 131-id vocabulary; `justice-and-oppression` was added
2026-08-25 from the adopted display-tag vocabulary — see Decisions.)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings

## Nahum 3
**Summary:** A woe oracle gives the verdict its reasons: "Woe to the bloody
city! It is all full of lies and robbery—no end to the prey" (3:1). The
battle-noise returns — whips, wheels, charging horsemen — and corpses pile
without number, because Nineveh has been an alluring prostitute and mistress of
witchcraft "who sells nations" (3:4). The LORD of Armies declares again, "Behold,
I am against you," and promises public disgrace: the nations will see her shame,
and no one will mourn or comfort her. Was she better than No-Amon — the Egyptian
city with the sea for a wall, which still went into captivity? Nineveh's
fortresses will fall like first-ripe figs into an open mouth, her guards and
officials will flee away like locusts when the sun appears, and her wound is
fatal.
The book ends with the world applauding the news: "All who hear the report of
you clap their hands over you, for who hasn't felt your endless cruelty?" (3:19).
**Tags:** `divine-judgment` — judgment pronounced with its indictment attached:
"Woe to the bloody city!" (3:1); "'Behold, I am against you,' says the LORD of
Armies... I will show the nations your nakedness, and the kingdoms your shame"
(3:5); the fatal, unmourned end answering the city's "endless cruelty" (3:7,
3:19); `justice-and-oppression` — the woe's indictment is predatory oppression:
"Woe to the bloody city! It is all full of lies and robbery—no end to the
prey" (3:1), the mistress of witchcraft "who sells nations" (3:4), and a world
that claps because "who hasn't felt your endless cruelty?" (3:19) — God's
justice answering the oppressor, with no political adjudication. (The
2026-08-23 note "Only one honest tag from the current vocabulary" described
the 131-id vocabulary; `justice-and-oppression` was added 2026-08-25 from the
adopted display-tag vocabulary — see Decisions.)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings

## Decisions record (reversible delegated defaults — Jesse can overturn any of these)

1. **No subdivisions in any chapter.** BSB supplies only chapter-initial
   headings for Nahum (1:1, 2:1, 3:1) — no intra-chapter boundary exists in the
   sanctioned boundary source. Nahum 1 does genuinely shift (the
   character-of-God poem in vv. 2–8, then the turn to Nineveh and Judah in
   vv. 9–15, roughly where MHCC's outline splits 1–8 / 9–15), but CONVENTIONS §4
   takes boundaries "strictly from the BSB public-domain headings" plus
   structural markers the book itself provides, and Nahum 1 carries no
   toledot-type marker. Rather than invent a boundary, the shift is carried in
   the chapter summary's prose.
2. **`refuge-in-trouble` tagged on Nahum 1.** Register check performed: the
   concept's register is shelter when trouble has come (Ps 46; Ps 91:1–2), and
   the pack itself already anchors Nahum 1:7 (Torrey admission, weight 0.8), so
   the id genuinely covers this verse. Neighboring ids were checked and
   rejected: `pastoral-refuge-and-justice` is the personal abuse-crisis register
   (its lexicon is domestic-violence queries), and `gods-protection` is the
   protection-prayer register (keeping over children and travel) — neither
   honestly matches a national-judgment oracle's comfort line.
3. **`restoration` NOT tagged** despite 1:12–13 ("I will break his yoke from off
   you") and 2:2 ("the LORD restores the excellency of Jacob"). The vocabulary's
   `restoration` is the personal renewal-prayer register ("restore my soul,"
   Ps 23:3), not national restoration from an oppressor; tagging it here would
   trade on the word, not the concept. Likewise `pastoral-freedom-from-bondage`
   (addiction-recovery register) was rejected for the yoke-breaking of 1:13.
4. **`humble-exaltation` NOT tagged on Nahum 3.** The chapter humbles a proud
   city, but its stated indictment is bloodshed, lies, plunder, and predatory
   seduction of nations (3:1, 3:4, 3:19) — not a bid at self-exaltation answered
   by humbling, and the chapter teaches nothing about God exalting the humble.
   Distinct from Genesis 11, where the builders' "let's make a name for
   ourselves" is the narrative's own engine.
5. **`gods-faithfulness` NOT tagged.** 2:2 grounds the assault in the
   LORD restoring Jacob, but it is a single parenthetical verse in a chapter
   whose substance is the battle scene — below the honest-substantial-presence
   bar; the same call covers its stronger witness at 1:12–13, a two-verse
   promise of coming relief to Judah inside the judgment oracle — the pack's
   register is God's promise-keeping constancy ("god keeps his promises,"
   Lam 3:22–23), not a fresh oracle of deliverance, and chapter 1's comfort
   substance is already carried honestly by `refuge-in-trouble`. Captured as a
   motif candidate instead.
6. **Wrath/comfort framing.** The book summary and chapter summaries present
   God's wrath exactly as the text does — the same God who is "slow to anger"
   (1:3), whose judgment on the cruel is itself the comfort of the oppressed
   (1:7, 1:15, 3:19). Neither softened (no apologizing for the oracle) nor
   relished (no gloating beyond what 3:19 itself reports); the "to the
   oppressed it reads as it was written" line in the Full summary is charitable
   to the book's stated purpose of comfort to Judah, per Nahum's name.
7. **"Nahum means comfort"** is glossed once in the Short summary and once in
   the Full summary's closing paragraph — a standard, uncontroversial
   etymological note, presented as a gloss rather than an argument.
8. **No PR-#43 id is used** in this doc (none of the nine honestly fits Nahum),
   so the 131-vs-122 vocabulary question does not arise beyond the front-matter
   note.
9. **Historical identifications kept minimal.** No-Amon is glossed as "the
   Egyptian city" (its fall is the text's own argument); no dates for Nineveh's
   fall or Nahum's ministry are asserted beyond the Jonah-to-Nahum century
   link, which MHCC carries (its Nahum 1 commentary puts Jonah's preaching
   roughly a hundred years earlier) and the summary frames with "about."
   (Reviewer correction: the earlier claim that both PD sources carry the link
