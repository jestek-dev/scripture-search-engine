# 1 Kings — book summary

## Short

1 Kings carries Israel from the golden noon of Solomon to the darkest house it ever crowned. It opens with David dying and Solomon asking God for an understanding heart — and receiving wisdom, and with it a temple where the LORD's glory descends in cloud. But the same Solomon who prays "heaven and the heaven of heavens can't contain you" ends with his heart turned after other gods, and the kingdom tears in two under his son. From there the book runs on parallel tracks: Judah's kings measured one by one against David, Israel's dynasties rising and falling under the sins of Jeroboam, until Ahab and Jezebel enthrone Baal — and God answers with Elijah, fire on Carmel, a still small voice at Horeb, and a word that no king's armor can turn aside. The book's engine is the word of the LORD: what it promises stands, and what it warns against falls, "according to the LORD's word" again and again.

## Full

1 Kings begins where 2 Samuel ends — with David old and cold in his bed and the succession unsettled — and it never stops being a book about what a king does with the word of the LORD. Its first movement (chapters 1–11) belongs to Solomon. Anointed amid his brother's coup, charged by his dying father to "keep the instruction of the LORD your God," Solomon asks God at Gibeon not for long life or riches but for "an understanding heart to judge your people" — and the request pleases the Lord so much that wisdom comes with riches and honor besides. The famous judgment between two mothers, the proverbs and songs, the visiting queen of Sheba left breathless: all of it displays a gift the text keeps calling God's, not Solomon's.

At the center of the Solomon story stands the temple. Seven years of quarried stone and carved cedar and gold end with the ark carried into the inner sanctuary and the cloud of the LORD's glory filling the house — and with Solomon's dedication prayer holding the mystery honestly in both hands: "will God in very deed dwell on the earth? Behold, heaven and the heaven of heavens can't contain you; how much less this house that I have built!" The prayer he builds on that confession is the book's theological heart — case after case of sinning, straying, defeated, exiled people turning back toward this house, with one refrain: "hear in heaven, your dwelling place; and when you hear, forgive."

Then the turn. The narrator has been letting warning notes sound quietly — the Egyptian marriage alliance, the multiplied horses and gold — and in chapter 11 the account is called due: Solomon's many foreign wives "turned away his heart after other gods; and his heart was not perfect with the LORD his God, as the heart of David his father was." The LORD, who had appeared to him twice, tears the kingdom — yet not in Solomon's days, and not entirely, "for David my servant's sake": one tribe stays with the house of David, "that David my servant may have a lamp always before me in Jerusalem." That lamp — judgment executed, promise kept — burns through the rest of the book.

The second movement (chapters 12–16) is the tearing and its aftermath. Rehoboam takes the young men's counsel over the old men's, answers his overworked people with threats, and ten tribes walk away — "a thing brought about from the LORD, that he might establish his word." Jeroboam, handed the north on God's own terms, immediately secures it his own way: two golden calves, new shrines, new priests, a feast in a month "devised of his own heart." That sin becomes the north's inheritance; the narrator measures every Israelite king by it, and dynasty after dynasty falls — Jeroboam's and Baasha's under prophetic words the text checks off as fulfilled, Zimri's inside seven days, with Omri's house rising to outdo them all. In Judah the pattern is different but not clean: Rehoboam's apostasy, Abijam's divided heart, and then Asa, who "did that which was right in the LORD's eyes, as David his father did."

The third movement (chapters 17–22) belongs to Ahab, who "did that which was evil in the LORD's sight above all that were before him," to Jezebel his queen, and to Elijah the Tishbite, who walks in from Gilead announcing that there will be no rain except at his word. Through drought, ravens, a widow's unfailing jar of meal, and a dead boy restored to life, the LORD keeps his prophet alive to stage the great confrontation on Mount Carmel: "The God who answers by fire, let him be God." Fire falls; the people cry, "The LORD, he is God!"; and the next chapter finds Elijah under a juniper tree asking to die — met not with rebuke but with food, sleep, a still small voice, a recommissioning, and the assurance of seven thousand in Israel who never bowed to Baal. The book closes its case against Ahab in the vineyard of Naboth, judicially murdered so the king could take his inheritance, and at Ramoth Gilead, where about four hundred prophets say what the king wants to hear and one — Micaiah — says what the LORD says. A bow drawn at random finds the gap in the disguised king's armor, and the dogs lick his blood "according to the LORD's word which he spoke."

1 Kings ends mid-story — Elijah's remaining commissions unfinished, Ahaziah serving Baal in Samaria — and leans into 2 Kings. But its verdict is already on record: not one word of the LORD's good promise has failed, and not one of his warnings has fallen to the ground.

# The twenty-two chapters

## 1 Kings 1
**Summary:** King David is old and cannot keep warm, and the succession hangs open — so Adonijah, his handsome son born after Absalom, "exalted himself, saying, 'I will be king,'" gathering chariots, fifty runners, and the backing of Joab and Abiathar the priest, while pointedly not inviting Nathan, Benaiah, or Solomon to his feast beside En Rogel. Nathan and Bathsheba move first: they remind David of his oath that Solomon would reign, and the old king keeps it that day — "as I swore to you by the LORD, the God of Israel... I will most certainly do this today." Solomon rides David's own mule to Gihon, is anointed by Zadok and Nathan to the trumpet blast and the people's roar, and the city rings so loudly that the sound reaches Adonijah's feast just as the eating ends — and at Jonathan's report the guests scatter. The would-be king ends the day clinging to the horns of the altar, and Solomon grants him a conditional pardon: "If he shows himself a worthy man, not a hair of his shall fall to the earth; but if wickedness is found in him, he shall die."

## 1 Kings 2
**Summary:** Dying, David charges Solomon with the words the whole book will keep grading kings by: "be strong therefore, and show yourself a man; and keep the instruction of the LORD your God, to walk in his ways... according to that which is written in the law of Moses" — so that the LORD's promise of a man on Israel's throne may stand. He also leaves unfinished business: Joab's two peacetime murders, kindness owed to Barzillai's sons, and Shimei's old curse. After David dies and is buried in his city, the new reign is secured in blood and banishment: Adonijah's request for Abishag is read as a fresh grab at the throne and costs him his life; Abiathar is expelled from the priesthood, "that he might fulfill the LORD's word which he spoke concerning the house of Eli in Shiloh"; Joab is struck down at the very altar he clings to, his victims' blood returned on his own head; and Shimei, confined to Jerusalem on oath, breaks bounds after three years and dies for it. The chapter closes: "The kingdom was established in the hand of Solomon."
**Tags:** `obedience-to-the-word` — David's charge makes written Scripture the condition of the throne: "keep the instruction of the LORD your God... according to that which is written in the law of Moses, that you may prosper," with the promise standing "If your children are careful of their way" (2:3–4); `divine-judgment` — long-deferred accounts settled under God's word: Abiathar's expulsion fulfills the sentence on Eli's house (2:27), the word over Joab — "The LORD will return his blood on his own head" — for Abner and Amasa (2:31–33), and Shimei's wickedness comes back on him (2:44); `davidic-covenant` — the promise quoted at the succession as the dynasty's ground: the throne stands "If your children are careful of their way" — the word the LORD spoke concerning David's house (2:2–4); `death-and-burial` — the formula that will govern both books of Kings begins: David "slept with his fathers, and was buried" in David's city, forty years a king (2:10–12); `leadership` — the royal charge as leadership's charter: "be strong therefore, and show yourself a man; and keep the instruction of the LORD your God" — the terms on which a throne is handed over (2:1–4)

## 1 Kings 3
**Summary:** Solomon allies with Egypt by marrying Pharaoh's daughter, and the narrator notes the era's compromise — "the people sacrificed in the high places, because there was not yet a house built for the LORD's name" — while affirming that "Solomon loved the LORD." At the great high place of Gibeon, God appears to him in a dream by night and says, "Ask for what I should give you"; Solomon, confessing "I am just a little child. I don't know how to go out or come in," asks for "an understanding heart to judge your people, that I may discern between good and evil." The request pleases the Lord: because Solomon did not ask for long life, riches, or his enemies' lives, God gives him an unequaled "wise and understanding heart" — and adds riches and honor he never asked for, with lengthened days held out on the one condition of walking in God's ways as David did. The gift goes straight to work: two prostitutes contest one living baby, Solomon calls for a sword, and the true mother's yearning gives her away — "All Israel... saw that the wisdom of God was in him to do justice."
**Tags:** `wisdom-from-god` — the Bible's signature story of wisdom asked for and given: "Give your servant therefore an understanding heart" (3:9), "I have given you a wise and understanding heart" (3:12), and the verdict that "the wisdom of God was in him to do justice" (3:28); `asking-in-gods-will` — a request granted because it was the right one: "Because you have asked this thing, and have not asked for yourself long life, nor have you asked for riches for yourself... behold, I have done according to your word" — "This request pleased the Lord" (3:10–12; PR #43 id, recorded in the Decisions record); `dreams-and-visions` — the whole exchange comes as night revelation: "the LORD appeared to Solomon in a dream by night," and "Solomon awoke; and behold, it was a dream" (3:5, 15); `leadership` — the leader's confessed inadequacy at the throne: "I am just a little child. I don't know how to go out or come in," asking "an understanding heart to judge your people" (3:7–9)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings

## 1 Kings 4
**Summary:** The kingdom at its height is shown in lists and pictures: Solomon's roster of princes and priests, and twelve officers who each supply the royal table for one month a year. The portrait widens — "Judah and Israel were numerous as the sand which is by the sea in multitude, eating and drinking and making merry," ruled in peace from the River — the WEB's name for the Euphrates — to Egypt's border, "every man under his vine and under his fig tree." The daily provisions and the forty thousand stalls of horses measure the court's scale. Above it all the narrator sets the reason for the fame: "God gave Solomon abundant wisdom, understanding, and breadth of mind like the sand that is on the seashore" — three thousand proverbs, a thousand and five songs, and speech that ranges over trees, animals, birds, creeping things, and fish — so that people of all nations, sent by all kings of the earth, come to hear him.

## 1 Kings 5
**Summary:** Hiram king of Tyre, who had always loved David, sends envoys to the newly anointed Solomon, and Solomon answers with his building plans: David could not build a house for the LORD's name because of the wars around him — "But now the LORD my God has given me rest on every side. There is no enemy and no evil occurrence" — so the son will build the house the LORD promised David his son would build. Hiram rejoices — "Blessed is the LORD today, who has given to David a wise son to rule over this great people" — and the two kings strike a working treaty: Lebanon cedar and cypress floated down by sea in exchange for wheat and pure oil, year by year. Solomon raises a rotating levy of thirty thousand Israelites for Lebanon, plus seventy thousand burden-bearers and eighty thousand stone cutters, and the foundation stones are cut: "large stones, costly stones... with worked stone." The narrator's summary keeps the credit where the chapter puts it: "The LORD gave Solomon wisdom, as he promised him. There was peace between Hiram and Solomon."
**Tags:** `gods-faithfulness` — the project rests on promises being kept: rest given as spoken ("the LORD my God has given me rest on every side," 5:4), the temple-builder son foretold to David now at work (5:5), and "The LORD gave Solomon wisdom, as he promised him" (5:12); `harmony-with-others` — international peace made and kept: Hiram's long friendship with David (5:1), his blessing over Solomon (5:7), and "There was peace between Hiram and Solomon, and the two of them made a treaty together" (5:12)

## 1 Kings 6
**Summary:** In the four hundred eightieth year after the exodus, in the fourth year of his reign, Solomon begins to build the LORD's house — sixty cubits long, twenty wide, thirty high, with side rooms around it and stone finished at the quarry so that "no hammer or ax or any tool of iron was heard in the house while it was under construction." Mid-build, the LORD's word comes with the condition that outranks the architecture: "Concerning this house which you are building, if you will walk in my statutes... then I will establish my word with you... I will dwell among the children of Israel, and will not forsake my people Israel." The interior is lined with carved cedar and overlaid with pure gold; in the inner sanctuary — the most holy place prepared for the ark — stand two olive-wood cherubim ten cubits high, wings touching wall to wall. Begun in the month Ziv and finished in the month Bul, the house takes seven years.
**Tags:** `presence-of-god` — the building's whole point, stated by God himself: "I will dwell among the children of Israel, and will not forsake my people Israel" (6:13), with the inner sanctuary prepared "to set the ark of the LORD's covenant there" (6:19); `obedience-to-the-word` — the oracle inside the building log makes obedience, not masonry, the condition of God's dwelling: "if you will walk in my statutes, and execute my ordinances, and keep all my commandments... then I will establish my word with you" (6:12)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings

## 1 Kings 7
**Summary:** Solomon spends thirteen years on his own palace complex — the House of the Forest of Lebanon with its cedar pillar rows, the hall of pillars, the porch of the throne "where he was to judge," and a house for Pharaoh's daughter — all in costly sawed stone. Then the account returns to the LORD's house and to another Hiram, a bronze worker from Tyre, son of a widow of Naphtali, "filled with wisdom and understanding and skill to work all works in bronze." His castings furnish the temple courts: the two great pillars named Jachin and Boaz, the molten sea on twelve oxen, ten ornate wheeled stands with their basins, and pots, shovels, and basins beyond weighing — "The weight of the bronze could not be determined." Solomon adds the gold furnishings — altar, show bread table, ten lamp stands, and vessels — and the chapter closes with the work finished and David's dedicated silver, gold, and vessels brought into the treasuries of the LORD's house.

## 1 Kings 8
**Summary:** Solomon assembles all Israel at the feast in the seventh month, and the priests carry the ark of the LORD's covenant — holding nothing "except the two stone tablets which Moses put there at Horeb" — into the inner sanctuary beneath the cherubim's wings; when they come out, the cloud fills the house, "for the LORD's glory filled the LORD's house," and the priests cannot stand to minister. Solomon blesses the assembly, recounting the promise kept from David's heart to this finished house, and then prays the book's greatest prayer, opening with its own check on every temple theology: "But will God in very deed dwell on the earth? Case by case — oaths, defeat, drought, famine, the foreigner from a far country, armies at war, and captives who "repent in the land where they are carried captive" (for "there is no man who doesn't sin") — the prayer asks one thing: "hear in heaven, your dwelling place; and when you hear, forgive." Rising from his knees, Solomon blesses the assembly — "There has not failed one word of all his good promise" — and prays that God would incline Israel's hearts to himself, "that all the peoples of the earth may know that the LORD himself is God. There is no one else." Fourteen days of feasting, with peace offerings counted in the tens of thousands, end with the people sent home "joyful and glad in their hearts."

## 1 Kings 9
**Summary:** With the building done, the LORD appears to Solomon a second time, as at Gibeon, and answers the dedication prayer directly: "I have made this house holy... to put my name there forever; and my eyes and my heart shall be there perpetually." The promise comes with the fork in the road stated to Solomon personally — walk before me as David did and the throne is established forever; but if he or his children "turn away" and "go and serve other gods," Israel will be cut off from the land, and this house — cast out of God's sight, "Though this house is so high" — will leave everyone who passes by astonished and hissing, asking why, with the answer ready: "Because they abandoned the LORD their God." ## 1 Kings 10
**Summary:** The queen of Sheba, hearing "of the fame of Solomon concerning the LORD's name," arrives with a great caravan to test him with hard questions — and Solomon answers everything. When she has seen the wisdom, the house, the table, and the ascent to the LORD's house, "there was no more spirit in her": the report was true, and "not even half was told me." Her verdict gives the glory past Solomon to his God — "Blessed is the LORD your God, who delighted in you... Because the LORD loved Israel forever, therefore he made you king, to do justice and righteousness." The chapter then piles up the era's gold: six hundred sixty-six talents a year, shields of beaten gold, an ivory throne with twelve lions, silver "as common as stones in Jerusalem," fleets bringing ivory, apes, and peacocks — and closes with horses and chariots imported from Egypt. The narrator reports the splendor without a word of blame; readers who remember Deuteronomy's charter for kings — not multiplying horses from Egypt, wives, silver and gold — have long heard warning notes under the catalog, a reading the very next chapter's turn bears out.

## 1 Kings 20
**Summary:** Ben Hadad of Syria, with thirty-two allied kings, besieges Samaria and dictates terms; Ahab concedes his silver, gold, wives, and children — but when the demand grows to a general looting of the palace, the elders stiffen him: "Don't listen, and don't consent." To the Syrian's boast Ahab returns the book's best one-liner — "Don't let him who puts on his armor brag like he who takes it off" — and an unnamed prophet brings the LORD's staggering word to this Baal-serving king: "Have you seen all this great multitude? Behold, I will deliver it into your hand today. Then you will know that I am the LORD" — and two hundred thirty-two young officers lead the sortie while the drunken king of Syria flees on horseback. The next year Syria returns to fight in the plains, on the theory that "Their god is a god of the hills" — and precisely because of that slander, a man of God announces, the LORD delivers the vast army again: a hundred thousand fall in a day and a wall falls on twenty-seven thousand more at Aphek. Ben Hadad's servants gamble on Israel's "merciful kings," and Ahab — "Is he still alive? He is my brother." — restores him with a covenant and lets him go. The verdict arrives in costume: a prophet, wounded at his own request (a companion who refused to strike him was killed by a lion for disobeying "the LORD's voice"), poses as a negligent guard and traps the king in his own ruling; unmasked, he delivers the sentence: "Because you have let go out of your hand the man whom I had devoted to destruction" — the ban, the devoted-to-destruction judgment — "therefore your life will take the place of his life, and your people take the place of his people" — and Ahab goes home "sullen and angry."
**Tags:** `gods-protection` — two impossible deliverances, each given so that "you will know that I am the LORD" (20:13; "you shall know" at 20:28) — the second expressly to answer the slander that the LORD is "a god of the hills... not a god of the valleys" (20:23, 28–30); `divine-judgment` — the acted parable and its sentence on the king who released what God had devoted to destruction: "your life will take the place of his life" (20:39–42), with the lion's judgment on the man who disobeyed the LORD's voice standing starkly beside it (20:35–36); `deliverance` — the plain rescue register with its stated purpose clause, twice: "Behold, I will deliver it into your hand today. Then you will know that I am the LORD" — the second time expressly answering the god-of-the-hills slander (20:13, 28)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings
**Sections:**
- **20:1–12 — Ben-hadad Attacks Samaria** (title: BSB) — The siege and the escalating demands: Ahab bends, then — braced by the elders — refuses, and the drunken king orders the attack.
- **20:13–34 — Ahab Defeats Ben-hadad** (title: BSB) — Twice the LORD hands Syria's armies to Israel so that Israel will know he is the LORD — at Samaria and, answering the "god of the hills" slander, at Aphek — and Ahab crowns the victory by covenanting with the man God had doomed.
- **20:35–43 — A Prophet Reproves Ahab** (title: BSB) — A wounded prophet's disguise draws the king into passing his own sentence: life for life, people for people, for releasing the man devoted to destruction.

## 1 Kings 21
**Summary:** Ahab wants Naboth's vineyard beside the palace in Jezreel for "a garden of herbs", and offers a better vineyard or its price; Naboth refuses on the LORD's own ground: "May the LORD forbid me, that I should give the inheritance of my fathers to you!" The king goes home "sullen and angry," lies down on his bed, turns away his face, and will eat no bread — and Jezebel takes over: "Do you now govern the kingdom of Israel?" Writing letters in Ahab's name under his seal, she orders Naboth's own city elders to proclaim a fast, seat him in honor, and have "two men, wicked fellows" testify that he "cursed God and the king" — and Naboth is stoned to death on the state's manufactured evidence, whereupon Jezebel tells Ahab to go take possession, and he goes. In the stolen vineyard the LORD's word through Elijah finds him: "Have you killed and also taken possession?... In the place where dogs licked the blood of Naboth, dogs will lick your blood, even yours" — and the greeting between old enemies ("Have you found me, my enemy?" — "I have found you, because you have sold yourself to do that which is evil in the LORD's sight") widens into the sentence already passed on Jeroboam's and Baasha's houses, with a word for Jezebel too: "The dogs will eat Jezebel by the rampart of Jezreel." The narrator pauses to file the verdict — "there was no one like Ahab, who sold himself to do that which was evil in the LORD's sight, whom Jezebel his wife stirred up" — and then records the surprise: Ahab tears his clothes, fasts in sackcloth, and goes about despondently, and the LORD tells Elijah, "Because he humbles himself before me, I will not bring the evil in his days; but I will bring the evil on his house in his son's day."
**Tags:** `sin` — coveting, false witness, judicial murder, and theft in one royal act, summed by the text itself: "you have sold yourself to do that which is evil in the LORD's sight" (21:20, 25–26); `honesty` — the machinery of the crime is manufactured testimony: forged letters under the king's seal (21:8) and "two men, wicked fellows" (21:10) swearing "Naboth cursed God and the king" (21:13; instructed at 21:10 as "You cursed God and the king!"); `divine-judgment` — the sentence in the stolen vineyard: dogs will lick Ahab's blood where they licked Naboth's, his house will go the way of Jeroboam's and Baasha's, and Jezebel's end is named (21:19, 21–24); `repentance` — reported in God's own words, without a verdict on the heart: Ahab fasts in sackcloth, and the LORD says, "Because he humbles himself before me, I will not bring the evil in his days" (21:27–29); `covetousness` — the Old Testament's defining narrative of coveting: the king who wants his neighbor's vineyard for "a garden of herbs," refused, goes home "sullen and angry," turns away his face, and will eat no bread — desire ripening into theft and murder (21:1–4, 15–16); `fasting` — both edges in one chapter: a fast proclaimed as cover for judicial murder (21:9, 12), and a fast of real self-humbling that God himself acknowledges — "See how Ahab humbles himself before me?" (21:27–29); `justice-and-oppression` — a legal process operated as a weapon: forged letters under the king's seal, a proclaimed fast, "two men, wicked fellows" swearing the charge, and a citizen stoned so the state can seize his inheritance — answered by the LORD's word at the vineyard gate: "Have you killed and also taken possession?" (21:8–16, 19). (The chapter's national-scale injustice was routed to the shared justice-and-oppression tag-gap row per the project-wide ruling, not to any `pastoral-*` tag; since Jesse's 2026-08-25 adoption of that concept as a display tag, the chapter carries it directly — the pastoral-register bar stands unchanged.)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings

## 1 Kings 22
**Summary:** After three years' peace, Ahab enlists visiting Jehoshaphat of Judah to retake Ramoth Gilead, and Jehoshaphat agrees — but first: "Please inquire first for the LORD's word." Ahab's prophets — about four hundred — oblige in unison — "Go up; for the Lord will deliver it into the hand of the king" — complete with Zedekiah's iron horns; but Jehoshaphat senses the echo chamber and asks for a prophet of the LORD, and Ahab admits there is one more: "Micaiah the son of Imlah; but I hate him, for he does not prophesy good concerning me, but evil." Pressed by the messenger to match the majority, Micaiah answers, "As the LORD lives, what the LORD says to me, that I will speak" — and after a sarcastic echo of the court line, he delivers two visions: Israel "scattered on the mountains, as sheep that have no shepherd," and the throne room of heaven, where the LORD asks, "Who will entice Ahab, that he may go up and fall at Ramoth Gilead?" and a spirit volunteers: "I will go out and will be a lying spirit in the mouth of all his prophets." Micaiah's conclusion states it without softening: "Now therefore, behold, the LORD has put a lying spirit in the mouth of all these your prophets; and the LORD has spoken evil concerning you" — a hard scene readers have long weighed carefully, and worth noticing on the text's own terms: Ahab hears the entire mechanism explained truthfully before he chooses to ride — and Micaiah, struck on the cheek by Zedekiah and jailed on affliction rations, stakes everything on the outcome: "If you return at all in peace, the LORD has not spoken by me." Ahab goes up disguised while Jehoshaphat wears his robes; Syria's captains hunt only the king of Israel, and "A certain man drew his bow at random, and struck the king of Israel between the joints of the armor" — he bleeds out propped in his chariot until evening, and the dogs lick his blood at the pool of Samaria, "according to the LORD's word which he spoke" — and two brief regnal accounts close the book: Jehoshaphat's twenty-five good years in Judah (right in the LORD's eyes, though the high places stay) and Ahaziah son of Ahab, who serves Baal like his father and mother before him.
**Tags:** `pleasing-god-not-people` — Micaiah under maximum pressure to join the four hundred: "Please let your word be like the word of one of them" — "As the LORD lives, what the LORD says to me, that I will speak" (22:13–14), at the price of a slap and a prison cell (22:24–27); `dreams-and-visions` — Micaiah's two reported visions: the shepherdless sheep on the mountains (22:17) and the throne room — "I saw the LORD sitting on his throne, and all the army of heaven standing by him" (22:19–23); `divine-judgment` — the word finds the disguised king through a bow drawn at random: Ahab dies at evening, and the dogs lick his blood "according to the LORD's word which he spoke" (22:34–35, 38); `false-prophets` — the classic how-do-I-know-a-true-prophet scene: about four hundred court prophets in unison — "Go up; for the Lord will deliver it into the hand of the king" — against one, with the mechanism explained to Ahab truthfully before he rides: "the LORD has put a lying spirit in the mouth of all these your prophets" (22:5–28)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings
**Sections:**
- **22:1–28 — Ahab, the False Prophets, and Micaiah** (title: adapted from BSB) — Four hundred voices say go; one hated prophet, refusing to please the court, reports the vision of the lying spirit and stakes his word on Ahab's death.
- **22:29–40 — Ahab's Defeat and Death** (title: BSB) — Disguise fails where the word has spoken: a random arrow finds the armor's joint, the king bleeds out at evening, and dogs lick his blood as foretold.
- **22:41–50 — Jehoshaphat Reigns in Judah** (title: BSB) — A good reign in Asa's pattern — right in the LORD's eyes, high places excepted — with peace toward Israel and a wrecked gold fleet at Ezion Geber.
- **22:51–53 — Ahaziah Reigns in Israel** (title: BSB) — Ahab's son walks in the way of his father and mother and of Jeroboam, serving Baal, and 1 Kings ends with the LORD provoked still.

1. **Vocabulary per the project-wide rollout ruling: all 131 ids at commit b3f491d are usable, including the nine PR #43 ids.** Two of the nine ARE used in this book, each a reversible delegated default: (a) **`asking-in-gods-will` on chapter 3** — the pack exists to route ask-and-receive queries to the canon's honest teaching about asking within God's will, and 1 Kings 3:10–12 is that teaching in narrative form: the request itself pleases the Lord, and is granted *because* it was not self-serving ("Because you have asked this thing, and have not asked for yourself long life..."). The summary adds no ask-formula framing; the unasked riches are reported with the text's own condition (3:14) attached. Reversible if Jesse prefers the pack confined to its NT anchors. (b) **`benediction` on chapter 8** — the pack is a locator for scripture benedictions a worship leader reaches for, and 8:54–61 is a complete spoken benediction over a whole assembly (the BSB's own heading is "Solomon's Benediction"); 8:57–58 could serve that pack as an anchor candidate. Reversible likewise. The other seven PR #43 ids were considered and none is used — nearest calls: `trinity`/`incarnation`/`christ-the-cornerstone` have no honest OT-narrative presence here (no later-revelation read-backs), and `doubt` on chapter 19 was rejected because Elijah's crisis is despair, not the mercy-for-the-doubter register. *2026-08-25: both uses — (a) `asking-in-gods-will` on ch. 3 and (b) `benediction` on ch. 8 — ratified by Jesse 2026-08-25 — no longer reversible-pending-review.*
2. **Provenance split recorded.** Chapters 16, 18, and 19 verified against the pinned-snapshot fixture (`pipeline/fixtures/web-subset.json` — all 101 witnessed 1 Kings verses byte-identical to the current edition); chapters 1–15, 17, and 20–22 verified against the current ebible.org engwebp edition (sha256 b6f55cc7…, fetched 2026-08-23), because the pinned zip (sha256 3458ca34…) is no longer obtainable upstream. **Divine-name check (per the coordinator's ruling):** the extracted corpus renders "the LORD" throughout 1 Kings — 178 verse lines, zero "Yahweh" — consistent with the coordinator's report; no discrepancy to record. Two quoted spans contain lowercase "the Lord" as the corpus's own reading (3:10 "This request pleased the Lord"; 22:6 "for the Lord will deliver it into the hand of the king") and are quoted byte-faithfully.
3. **Pastoral-register ruling applied.** Both `pastoral-*` uses pass the personal-crisis register test: **chapter 17** (`pastoral-prayer-for-healing`) is one widow's household — a sick child, a mother's grief, a prophet's desperate prayer answered (17:17–24); the summary follows the pack's own guardrail by reporting the answer as this family's story, never as a scheduling of cures. **Chapter 19** (`pastoral-hope-in-despair`) is the pack's register exactly — a man alone under a tree asking God for death (19:4), met with food, sleep, presence, and recommissioning; the chapter is arguably the canon's central narrative for that pack, and "Elijah under the juniper tree" is flagged as a lexicon-extension candidate. Conversely, **chapter 21** (Naboth) is national-scale injustice — the crown fabricating a capital case — and per the project-wide ruling carries no `pastoral-*` tag; its justice themes are routed to the landed `justice-and-oppression` row (see tag-gap item 3). **Chapter 9:16** (Pharaoh burning Gezer's Canaanites) and the war chapters were likewise kept clear of pastoral ids.

Chapter 4 carries no `blessing` tag (the chapter never uses blessing language; tagging prosperity description as `blessing` risked exactly the formula-reading the guardrail forbids) and chapter 10's wealth catalog is likewise untagged; both decisions reversible.
5. **The Deuteronomy-17 undertone signposted, not asserted.** Chapter 10 closes on horses multiplied from Egypt, and chapter 11 opens on many wives; the summary marks the echo of Deuteronomy's charter for kings as what "readers... have long heard" — a standard conservative reading explicitly flagged as a reading, supported by the text's own sequencing (the chapter-11 turn), never stated as the narrator's own comment.

Reversible by swapping either back in for `covenant` or `benediction`.
7. **Chapter 13 told without smoothing, and without an obedience tag.** The summary reports what the text reports — the lie ("He lied to him," 13:18), the true word arriving through the liar (13:20–22), the lion that kills but does not eat (13:24–28) — and adds one sentence naming the chapter's refusal to be domesticated. `obedience-to-the-word` is NOT tagged: the chapter depicts the failure mode (a genuine command abandoned on the strength of a claimed counter-revelation), and per the worked-example rule a failure-mode chapter does not get the concept; the teaching substance is logged as motif 12 instead. Same rule kept `obedience-to-the-word` off chapter 20 (the struck-prophet scene, 20:35–36). Chapter 12 DOES carry the tag — Judah's army actually obeys the word and stands down (12:24), a depicted success, not a failure.
8. **Chapter 22's lying-spirit vision quoted exactly and signposted, not adjudicated.** Micaiah's words are given verbatim ("the LORD has put a lying spirit in the mouth of all these your prophets," 22:23) with one textual observation — Ahab hears the whole mechanism explained truthfully before choosing to ride — and the note that readers "have long weighed" the scene. No theory of divine deception is asserted or denied; the summary lets the chapter's own structure (true word delivered alongside the enticement's exposure) do its work.

The fixture's own note (this chapter is a pastoral-harm gate for "prophets cutting themselves") supported keeping the description exact and unornamented.
