## Short

Zephaniah is the day of the LORD compressed into three chapters. Speaking in the days of King Josiah, the prophet announces a judgment that opens at the scale of creation — "I will utterly sweep away everything from the surface of the earth" — and lands with precision on Judah and Jerusalem, where people mix worship of the LORD with Baal, the stars, and the pagan god Malcam. One door stands open: "Seek the LORD, all you humble of the land… It may be that you will be hidden in the day of the LORD's anger." After judgment circles the surrounding nations and wheels back onto Jerusalem itself, the book ends in a changed key: God purifies a humble remnant, takes his place among them as King, and rejoices over his own people with singing.

## Full

The book introduces its prophet more fully than most of the Twelve: Zephaniah, the son of Cushi, with a family line traced back four generations to Hezekiah — usually understood to be King Hezekiah, which would make the prophet royalty, though the text does not say so outright. He speaks "in the days of Josiah, the son of Amon, king of Judah," the era of Judah's last great reform, and his message suggests how much corruption the reform still had to reach. The opening announcement is as wide as the flood: "I will utterly sweep away everything from the surface of the earth, says the LORD" — man and animal, birds of the sky, fish of the sea.

From that whole-earth horizon the word narrows onto Judah and Jerusalem. The LORD's hand stretches out against "the remnant of Baal," the idolatrous priests, the rooftop worshipers of the army of the sky (the sun, moon, and stars treated as gods), and people who swear by the LORD and by Malcam in the same breath. This is the book that gives Scripture some of its starkest "day of the LORD" language: a day the LORD has prepared like a sacrifice, "a day of wrath, a day of distress and anguish… a day of darkness and gloom," hunting down even the complacent who sit "settled on their dregs" — undisturbed, like wine left on its sediment — telling themselves, "The LORD will not do good, neither will he do evil." Neither silver nor gold will be able to deliver anyone from it.

At the book's hinge stands its one open door: "Seek the LORD, all you humble of the land, who have kept his ordinances. Seek righteousness. Seek humility. It may be that you will be hidden in the day of the LORD's anger." Then the judgment already pronounced on Judah rings the compass — Philistia to the west, Moab and Ammon to the east, Cush to the south, Assyria to the north, where carefree Nineveh, the city that said "I am, and there is no one besides me," becomes a desolation for animals. The circle closes on Jerusalem, "the oppressing city," whose princes are roaring lions, whose judges are evening wolves, whose prophets are treacherous and whose priests have "done violence to the law" — while "The LORD, within her, is righteous," bringing his justice to light every morning.

Then the book turns. Beyond the day when God gathers the nations to pour out his indignation, he promises to "purify the lips of the peoples, that they may all call on the LORD's name," to remove the proud from his holy mountain, and to leave "an afflicted and poor people" — a remnant who take refuge in the LORD's name and speak no lies. The ending is a song sung from both directions. Zion is told: "Sing, daughter of Zion!… Be glad and rejoice with all your heart," for "The King of Israel, the LORD, is among you." And the LORD sings back: "The LORD, your God, is among you, a mighty one who will save. He will rejoice over you with joy. He will calm you in his love. He will rejoice over you with singing." The book that began by sweeping everything from the earth closes with God gathering the outcast, turning shame into praise, and restoring his people's fortunes before their eyes.

## Zephaniah 1
**Summary:** The LORD's word comes to Zephaniah, whose line is traced back four generations to Hezekiah, in the days of Josiah, king of Judah. The opening announcement is total: "I will utterly sweep away everything from the surface of the earth" — man and animal, birds and fish — and then the LORD's hand stretches out against Judah and Jerusalem in particular: against "the remnant of Baal," the idolatrous priests, those who worship the army of the sky — the stars taken for gods — on their housetops, and those who swear by the LORD and by the pagan god Malcam in the same breath. The day of the LORD is at hand, pictured as a sacrifice the LORD himself has prepared, and it falls on the princes and the king's sons, on those clothed in foreign clothing, on violent and deceitful servants, and on those loaded with silver. The LORD will search Jerusalem with lamps for the complacent who sit "settled on their dregs" — at ease like wine never poured off its sediment — saying, "The LORD will not do good, neither will he do evil." The great day is near: "a day of wrath, a day of distress and anguish," and neither their silver nor their gold will be able to deliver them.
**Tags:** `divine-judgment` — the sweeping judgment announced from the first oracle, "I will utterly sweep away everything from the surface of the earth" (1:2–6), the LORD's "I will punish" falling on princes, the violent, and the complacent (1:8–13), and the announcement "The great day of the LORD is near… a day of wrath, a day of distress and anguish" (1:14–15) from which no silver or gold can deliver (1:18); `day-of-the-lord` — one of the theme's core chapters: the day pictured as a sacrifice the LORD has prepared (1:7–8), and "The great day of the LORD is near… a day of wrath, a day of distress and anguish" (1:14–15); `idolatry` — the hand stretched out against "the remnant of Baal," the idolatrous priests, the housetop worshipers of the army of the sky, and those who swear by the LORD and by Malcam in one breath (1:4–6); `complacency` — the row's classic text: the LORD searching Jerusalem with lamps for those "settled on their dregs" who say, "The LORD will not do good, neither will he do evil" (1:12). (The 2026-08-23 note "Only one honest tag from the current vocabulary" described the 131-id vocabulary; the three tags following `divine-judgment` were added 2026-08-25 from the adopted display-tag vocabulary — see Decisions D15.)

## Zephaniah 2
**Summary:** Before the day arrives, the prophet issues the book's one summons: gather together, "you nation that has no shame," and "Seek the LORD, all you humble of the land… Seek righteousness. Seek humility. It may be that you will be hidden in the day of the LORD's anger." Then the judgment widens to the nations around Judah, moving around the compass: the Philistine coast in the west will be emptied into pastureland held for "the remnant of the house of Judah"; Moab and Ammon in the east, who taunted the LORD's people, will end like Sodom and Gomorrah, plundered by the remnant they insulted; Cush to the south falls to the sword; and Assyria to the north, with its carefree capital Nineveh — the "joyous city" that said in her heart, "I am, and there is no one besides me" — becomes a desolation where herds lie down. In the middle of the oracles stands a striking promise: the LORD "will famish all the gods of the land" — starve them out — and then "Men will worship him, everyone from his place, even all the shores of the nations."
**Tags:** `repentance` — the summons to turn back to God before judgment falls: "Seek the LORD, all you humble of the land… Seek righteousness. Seek humility," spoken "before the fierce anger of the LORD comes on you" (2:1–3); `divine-judgment` — the LORD's judgment ringing the nations: "The LORD's word is against you, Canaan, the land of the Philistines" (2:5), "Moab will be as Sodom, and the children of Ammon as Gomorrah" (2:9), and Nineveh made "a desolation, as dry as the wilderness" (2:13–15); `day-of-the-lord` — the summons is framed by the approaching day: seek the LORD "before the fierce anger of the LORD comes on you," for "It may be that you will be hidden in the day of the LORD's anger" (2:2–3); `remnant` — each oracle's hope-note: the Philistine coast held in the end for "the remnant of the house of Judah" (2:7), and Moab and Ammon plundered by the remnant they insulted (2:9)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings
**Sections:**
- **2:1–3 — A Call to Repentance** (title: BSB) — The shameless nation is told to gather before the day of the LORD's anger arrives; the humble of the land are urged to seek the LORD, righteousness, and humility, with the hope that they may be hidden.
- **2:4–7 — Judgment on the Philistines** (title: BSB) — The Philistine coast — Gaza, Ashkelon, Ashdod, Ekron — will be emptied and turned to shepherds' pasture, held in the end for the remnant of the house of Judah, whom the LORD will visit and restore.
- **2:8–11 — Judgment on Moab and Ammon** (title: BSB) — For reproaching the LORD's people and magnifying themselves against their border, Moab and Ammon will end like Sodom and Gomorrah; the LORD will famish all the gods of the land, and the nations' shores will worship him.
- **2:12–15 — Judgment on Cush and Assyria** (title: BSB) — The sword reaches south to Cush and north to Assyria: carefree Nineveh, which said "I am, and there is no one besides me," becomes a desolation where animals lie down and passersby hiss.

## Zephaniah 3
**Summary:** The woe wheels back onto Jerusalem itself — "rebellious and polluted, the oppressing city" — which did not obey the voice, receive correction, trust in the LORD, or draw near to her God; her princes are roaring lions, her judges evening wolves, her prophets arrogant and treacherous, her priests doers of "violence to the law," while "The LORD, within her, is righteous," bringing his justice to light every morning. Since Judah refused to learn even from the nations God had already cut off, the LORD says "wait for me" for the day he gathers all kingdoms to pour out his fierce anger on the earth. Then judgment turns to healing: God will "purify the lips of the peoples, that they may all call on the LORD's name, to serve him shoulder to shoulder," remove the proudly exulting from his holy mountain, and leave "an afflicted and poor people" — a remnant who take refuge in the LORD's name, do no iniquity, and speak no lies. The ending is a song from both directions: Zion is told to "Sing… Be glad and rejoice with all your heart," for her King, the LORD, is among her, and the LORD himself — "a mighty one who will save" — "will rejoice over you with joy… He will rejoice over you with singing." He will deal with all who afflicted his people, save the lame, gather the driven away, and give praise and honor to those whose shame has been in all the earth, restoring their fortunes before their eyes.
**Tags:** `divine-judgment` — woe on the oppressing city whose leaders devour and whose priests "have done violence to the law" (3:1–4), and the day the LORD gathers the kingdoms "to pour on them my indignation, even all my fierce anger" (3:8); `joy-in-the-lord` — Zion's commanded joy in her God: "Sing, daughter of Zion!… Be glad and rejoice with all your heart," because "The King of Israel, the LORD, is among you" (3:14–15) — answered by the LORD's own rejoicing over his people (3:17); `gods-love` — "He will calm you in his love. He will rejoice over you with singing" — God's love for his people expressed as his own delight and song over them (3:17); `humble-exaltation` — the "proudly exulting ones" are removed and "an afflicted and poor people" are left (3:11–12), and God gives "praise and honor" to those "whose shame has been in all the earth" (3:19–20); `restoration` — "I will save those who are lame and gather those who were driven away… when I restore your fortunes before your eyes" (3:19–20); `nations-and-peoples` — the peoples of the whole earth purified for worship: "I will purify the lips of the peoples, that they may all call on the LORD's name, to serve him shoulder to shoulder" (3:9), with worshipers coming from "beyond the rivers of Cush" (3:10); `remnant` — a whole section leaves "an afflicted and poor people" — a remnant who take refuge in the LORD's name, do no iniquity, and speak no lies (3:12–13); `gods-delight-in-his-people` — the theme's landing text, in the WEB's own wording: the LORD among his people, "a mighty one who will save," who "will rejoice over you with joy," "will calm you in his love," and "will rejoice over you with singing" (3:14–17)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings
**Sections:**
- **3:1–5 — Judgment on Jerusalem** (title: BSB) — Woe falls on the oppressing city: princes, judges, prophets, and priests have each corrupted their office, while the righteous LORD within her brings his justice to light every morning and the unjust know no shame.
- **3:6–8 — Purification of the Nations** (title: BSB) — Judah refused to learn from the nations God had already cut off, so the LORD says "wait for me": he will gather the nations and assemble the kingdoms to pour out his indignation and fierce anger on the earth.
- **3:9–13 — A Faithful Remnant** (title: BSB) — God promises to purify the lips of the peoples so that all may call on the LORD's name, to remove the proud from his holy mountain, and to leave an afflicted and poor remnant who take refuge in his name and speak no lies.
- **3:14–20 — Israel's Restoration** (title: BSB) — Zion is called to sing: her King, the LORD, is among her, a mighty one who will save, rejoicing over her with singing; he will gather the scattered, turn their shame to praise and honor, and restore their fortunes before their eyes.

## Doctrinal notes (per `docs/DOCTRINAL-BASIS.md` and CONVENTIONS §6)

- Wrath and delight are both presented exactly as the text gives them: the "day
  of wrath" language of chapter 1 is not softened, and the rejoicing of chapter
  3 is not detached from the judgment it follows.
- Zephaniah 3:17 is a beloved comfort verse; its display copy is anchored
  word-for-word to the staged WEB text: "The LORD, your God, is among you, a
  mighty one who will save. He will rejoice over you with joy. He will calm you
  in his love. He will rejoice over you with singing." Note the direction of the
  verse: it is **God** who rejoices over his people; the people's rejoicing is
  commanded separately in 3:14. The doc keeps those two directions distinct.
- 2:3's "It may be that you will be hidden" is kept with the text's own
  contingency — seeking the LORD is never presented as a formula that
  guarantees shelter (no prosperity-style framing).
- The identification of Hezekiah (1:1) with King Hezekiah is signposted as a
  common reading, not stated as narrative fact.
