# Proverbs — book summary (draft)

## Short

Proverbs is the Bible's book of practical wisdom — hundreds of short, sharpened sayings about money, speech, work, friendship, marriage, parenting, anger, and the two paths every choice sets a person on. Its motto stands at the front door: "The fear of the LORD is the beginning of knowledge" (1:7) — skill for living starts with reverence for the God who made the world. The opening chapters are a father's urgent speeches to his son, with Wisdom herself personified as a woman calling in the streets; the long middle collects Solomon's proverbs along with two short "sayings of the wise" collections; and the book closes with the words of Agur, the counsel King Lemuel's mother taught him, and an alphabet poem in praise of a worthy woman. Its sayings tell how life generally goes under God's ordered world — they are wisdom to live by, not guarantees to cash in.

## Full

Proverbs gathers Israel's wisdom teaching into one book, and it names its own aim in the opening lines: "to know wisdom and instruction… to give prudence to the simple, knowledge and discretion to the young man" (1:2, 4). Wisdom here is not cleverness but skill for living rightly in God's world, and the book grounds it in a single foundation, stated at the beginning and again at the hinge of chapter 9: "The fear of the LORD is the beginning of knowledge" (1:7); "The fear of the LORD is the beginning of wisdom" (9:10).

The book is built from marked collections, and it labels its own seams. Chapters 1–9 are extended speeches — "the proverbs of Solomon, the son of David, king of Israel" (1:1) — in which a father pleads with "my son" to choose the path of wisdom over the path of the wicked and of the adulteress, and Wisdom, personified as a woman, calls aloud in the public squares (1:20–33), recounts her presence with the LORD at creation (8:22–31), and sets her feast against the rival feast of Folly (9). At 10:1 the form changes: "The proverbs of Solomon" introduces the long middle collections of two-line sayings, most of them self-contained, deliberately mixed in topic — righteousness and wickedness, diligence and laziness, wealth and poverty, the tongue, the king, the family. At 22:17 the book turns to "the words of the wise" — "thirty excellent things of counsel and knowledge" (22:20) — followed at 24:23 by a short appendix ("These also are sayings of the wise"). Chapter 25 opens a further Solomon collection with a note of its own history: "These also are proverbs of Solomon, which the men of Hezekiah king of Judah copied out" (25:1). Two final voices close the book: "the words of Agur the son of Jakeh" (30:1), a humble outsider whose numbered sayings marvel at what he does not understand, and "the words of King Lemuel — the revelation which his mother taught him" (31:1), a queen mother's charge about drink, women, and justice for the voiceless, capped by the famous alphabet acrostic in praise of the worthy woman who fears the LORD (31:10–31).

Two ways of reading Proverbs go wrong, and the book itself guards against both. Its act–consequence sayings — the diligent grow rich, the righteous are delivered, the sluggard goes hungry — describe how God's ordered world generally works; they are generalizations for shaping character, not promises to claim, and Proverbs itself knows the exceptions ("Better is a little with righteousness, than great revenues with injustice," 16:8; "Better is the poor who walks in his integrity," 28:6). And its wisdom is never merely secular technique: the LORD is named throughout as the one who weighs hearts, directs steps, hates false balances, hears the prayer of the upright, and pleads the cause of the poor. In Proverbs, the practical is theological all the way down — how a person handles money, words, anger, and neighbors reveals whether they fear the LORD.

# The thirty-one chapters

## Proverbs 1
**Summary:** The book opens by stating its purpose — "to know wisdom and instruction," to give prudence to the simple and discretion to the young — and lays down its motto: "The fear of the LORD is the beginning of knowledge, but the foolish despise wisdom and instruction" (1:7). A father then warns his son against the first rival voice, sinners who entice him to join their ambush for easy plunder; their greed is a trap that springs on themselves, for "these lay in wait for their own blood" (1:18). The second voice answers: Wisdom, personified as a woman, "calls aloud in the street" (1:20), warning that those who refuse her will call on her in calamity and not be answered, while "whoever listens to me will dwell securely" (1:33).
**Tags:** `wisdom-from-god` — the book's motto: "The fear of the LORD is the beginning of knowledge" (1:7), and Wisdom's own public call, "Turn at my reproof" (1:20–23); `sin` — sin's self-destroying pull, from "if sinners entice you, don't consent" to "these lay in wait for their own blood" (1:10–19); `fear-of-the-lord` — the motto's own phrase, in its piety register: "The fear of the LORD is the beginning of knowledge" (1:7); `temptation` — Scripture's plainest anti-temptation imperative: "if sinners entice you, don't consent," against the whole enticement scene (1:10–19)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings
**Sections:**
- **1:1–7 — The Beginning of Knowledge** (title: BSB) — The title and purpose statement: proverbs to make the simple prudent and the wise wiser, founded on the fear of the LORD as the beginning of knowledge.
- **1:8–19 — The Enticement of Sin** (title: BSB) — The father's first warning: when sinners entice you to violent, easy gain, don't consent — their path sheds blood and ends by taking the life of its owners.
- **1:20–33 — Wisdom Calls Aloud** (title: BSB) — Wisdom cries out in the streets and city gates: turn at her reproof, for those who hate knowledge will eat the fruit of their own way, but whoever listens will dwell securely, without fear of harm.

## Proverbs 2
**Summary:** The father tells his son what seeking wisdom takes and what it yields. If you call out for discernment and "search for her as for hidden treasures" (2:4), "then you will understand the fear of the LORD, and find the knowledge of God" (2:5) — "for the LORD gives wisdom. Out of his mouth comes knowledge and understanding" (2:6). God himself guards the seeker: "He is a shield to those who walk in integrity" (2:7). Wisdom's protection is then made concrete against two dangers that will dominate the coming chapters — the men who "rejoice to do evil" (2:14) and the strange woman "who flatters with her words" and whose "house leads down to death" (2:16, 18) — so that the son walks instead in the way of good men.
**Tags:** `wisdom-from-god` — wisdom sought like treasure and given by God: "the LORD gives wisdom. Out of his mouth comes knowledge and understanding" (2:4–6); `gods-protection` — "He is a shield to those who walk in integrity… and preserve the way of his saints" (2:7–8); `knowing-god` — seeking wisdom's stated goal: "then you will understand the fear of the LORD, and find the knowledge of God" (2:5)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings

## Proverbs 3
**Summary:** The chapter holds the book's best-loved counsel: "Trust in the LORD with all your heart, and don't lean on your own understanding. In all your ways acknowledge him, and he will make your paths straight" (3:5–6). Around it cluster the marks of a life bound to God — kindness and truth written "on the tablet of your heart" (3:3), honoring the LORD with the first fruits of all your increase, and receiving hard providences as love: "whom the LORD loves, he corrects, even as a father reproves the son in whom he delights" (3:11–12). A poem then prices wisdom above silver, gold, and rubies — "she is a tree of life to those who lay hold of her" (3:18), the same wisdom by which "the LORD founded the earth" (3:19) — and the chapter closes with neighbor-ethics: don't withhold good, devise evil, or envy the violent, for God "mocks the mockers, but he gives grace to the humble" (3:34).

By understanding, he established the heavens" (3:19–20); `humble-exaltation` — "Surely he mocks the mockers, but he gives grace to the humble" (3:34); `design-in-creation` — creation executed by wisdom, understanding, and knowledge: "By wisdom the LORD founded the earth. By understanding, he established the heavens. By his knowledge, the depths were broken up, and the skies drop down the dew." (3:19–20, fixture-verified; both-tags beside `creation` on the same verses — orderedness located in the Maker's mind vs the act of making)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings
**Sections:**
- **3:1–12 — Trust in the LORD with All Your Heart** (title: BSB) — Keep the teaching, bind kindness and truth to your heart, trust the LORD rather than your own understanding, honor him with your first fruits, and don't despise his fatherly discipline.
- **3:13–35 — The Blessings of Wisdom** (title: BSB) — Wisdom outvalues silver, gold, and rubies — a tree of life, the very wisdom by which the LORD founded the earth — and it works outward into concrete good faith with neighbors, for the LORD's friendship is with the upright.

## Proverbs 4
**Summary:** The father reaches back a generation: "For I was a son to my father… He taught me" (4:3–4), and passes on the family charge — "Wisdom is supreme. Get wisdom. Yes, though it costs all your possessions, get understanding" (4:7). He sets the two paths side by side: avoid the way of the wicked, who "don't sleep unless they do evil" (4:16), for "the path of the righteous is like the dawning light that shines more and more until the perfect day" (4:18), while the way of the wicked is darkness. The closing charge gathers the whole body into wisdom's keeping — eyes straight ahead, feet on a level path, and at the center the charge the chapter is remembered for: "Keep your heart with all diligence, for out of it is the wellspring of life" (4:23).
**Tags:** `parenting` — wisdom handed down parent to child across three generations: "For I was a son to my father… He taught me" (4:1–4). (Only one honest tag from the current vocabulary: the chapter's get-wisdom charge is father-voiced, with no divine reference anywhere in the chapter, so the wisdom-from-god concept — wisdom as God's gift — is not honestly present; see Decisions record #13.)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings

## Proverbs 5
**Summary:** The first full-length warning against adultery. The adulteress's appeal is real — "the lips of an adulteress drip honey" (5:3) — but her end is wormwood and a two-edged sword: "Her feet go down to death. Her steps lead straight to Sheol" (5:5). The counsel is distance, not confidence: "Remove your way far from her. Don't come near the door of her house" (5:8), lest strength, wealth, and honor drain away to strangers and the end be groaning: "How I have hated instruction" (5:12). Against the stolen spring the father sets the blessed one — "rejoice in the wife of your youth… be captivated always with her love" (5:18–19) — and grounds it all in God's sight: "the ways of man are before the LORD's eyes" (5:21), and the wicked man's own sin becomes the cord that holds him.
**Tags:** `pastoral-sexual-purity` — the strategy of distance: "Remove your way far from her. Don't come near the door of her house" (5:3–14; see Decisions record #5); `godly-marriage` — the God-given alternative: "Rejoice in the wife of your youth… Be captivated always with her love" (5:15–19); `sin` — sin as a self-tightening snare: "The evil deeds of the wicked ensnare him. The cords of his sin hold him firmly" (5:21–23)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings

## Proverbs 6
**Summary:** Four warnings in quick succession. First, if you've pledged yourself as collateral for another's debt, humble yourself and get free "like a gazelle from the hand of the hunter" (6:5). Second, the sluggard: "Go to the ant, you sluggard. Consider her ways, and be wise" (6:6) — the ant needs no overseer, yet "a little sleep, a little slumber" ends with poverty arriving "as a robber" (6:10–11). Third, the worthless man who winks, signals, and "always sows discord" — capped by the numbered list of "six things which the LORD hates; yes, seven which are an abomination to him" (6:16), from arrogant eyes and a lying tongue to sowing discord among brothers. The chapter then returns to adultery with harder arithmetic: "Can a man scoop fire into his lap, and his clothes not be burned?" (6:27) — the adulterer "destroys his own soul" (6:32), and a husband's jealous fury will accept no ransom.
**Tags:** `work-and-diligence` — the ant with no chief who "provides her bread in the summer," against the sluggard's ruinous "little sleep, a little slumber" (6:6–11); `honesty` — twice in the LORD's hate-list: "a lying tongue" and "a false witness who utters lies" (6:16–19); `pastoral-sexual-purity` — adultery as fire in the lap: "Whoever touches her will not be unpunished" (6:23–35; see Decisions record #5); `money-and-possessions` — the debt-pledge warning: free yourself from rash collateral "like a gazelle from the hand of the hunter" (6:1–5)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings
**Sections:**
- **6:1–19 — Warnings against Foolishness** (title: BSB) — Free yourself from rash debt-pledges, learn diligence from the ant, and recognize the discord-sowing worthless man — then the seven things the LORD hates, discord-sowing chief among them.
- **6:20–35 — Warnings against Adultery** (title: BSB) — The parents' commandment is a lamp against the wayward wife: adultery burns like carried fire, costs more than a thief's sevenfold restitution, and faces a jealous husband who will accept no ransom.

## Proverbs 7
**Summary:** The father tells a story he watched from his window: a young man "void of understanding" (7:7) drifts at twilight toward the corner of the strange woman, who meets him dressed like a prostitute, "loud and defiant" (7:11). Her seduction is a masterclass — religious respectability ("Today I have paid my vows," 7:14), a perfumed bed, and a safely absent husband — and "with persuasive words, she led him astray" (7:21). He follows "as an ox goes to the slaughter" (7:22), not knowing it will cost his life. The moral is spoken over the young man's shoulder to every reader: "she has thrown down many wounded… Her house is the way to Sheol, going down to the rooms of death" (7:26–27). The defense, again, is to bind wisdom close — "Guard my teaching as the apple of your eye" (7:2).

## Proverbs 8
**Summary:** Against the adulteress's whispered corner-invitations, Wisdom cries out in the open — at the crossroads, beside the gates — to everyone: "I call to you men!… You simple, understand prudence!" (8:4–5). She commends her words as true, right, and worth more than silver, gold, or rubies; she claims the public world of rulers ("By me kings reign," 8:15) and promises herself to seekers: "I love those who love me. Those who seek me diligently will find me" (8:17). The poem then reaches back before the world: "The LORD possessed me in the beginning of his work" (8:22) — before depths, mountains, or fields, Wisdom was there, "the craftsman by his side… always rejoicing before him" (8:30), delighting in the sons of men. So the choice is life-or-death: "whoever finds me finds life, and will obtain favor from the LORD… all those who hate me love death" (8:35–36).
**Tags:** `wisdom-from-god` — Wisdom's own self-offer: "Receive my instruction rather than silver… Those who seek me diligently will find me" (8:10–11, 8:17); `creation` — Wisdom present at the founding of the world: "When he established the heavens, I was there… then I was the craftsman by his side" (8:22–31); `fear-of-the-lord` — Wisdom's own definition: "The fear of the LORD is to hate evil" (8:13)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings
*Note:* the speaker here is wisdom personified as a woman — the same figure as 1:20–33 and 9:1–6, a poetic personification of the wisdom the LORD used in creating and governing the world. Christian readers from the New Testament era onward have heard in this poem a foreshadowing of Christ, whom Paul calls "the wisdom of God" (1 Corinthians 1:24; Brooks and Matthew Henry both read the poem that way); that is a later, signposted reading — the chapter itself presents Lady Wisdom, not a second divine person, and the summary above describes only what the text says.

## Proverbs 9
**Summary:** The nine opening chapters end with two rival invitations. Wisdom has built her house, prepared her feast, and sent out her maidens with an open call: "Whoever is simple, let him turn in here!… Leave your simple ways, and live" (9:4–6). Between the feasts sits the book's hinge, restated from chapter 1: "The fear of the LORD is the beginning of wisdom. The knowledge of the Holy One is understanding" (9:10) — with the shrewd aside that a scoffer repays correction with hate, while a wise man loves you for it. Then the counter-hostess: Folly, "loud, undisciplined" (9:13), sits at her door calling the same simple passersby with her own menu — "Stolen water is sweet. Food eaten in secret is pleasant" (9:17) — "but he doesn't know that the departed spirits are there, that her guests are in the depths of Sheol" (9:18).

## Proverbs 10
**Summary:** "The proverbs of Solomon" (10:1) opens the book's long middle: from here through chapter 22 the form is the freestanding two-line saying, and the chapters are deliberately miscellaneous — this one ranges over sons wise and foolish, honest and lazy hands, love and hatred, and above all the two mouths. The chapter's steadiest contrast is righteous and wicked speech: "The mouth of the righteous is a spring of life" (10:11), "the lips of the righteous feed many" (10:21), while "he who utters a slander is a fool" (10:18) and "in the multitude of words there is no lack of disobedience" (10:19). Behind the maxims stands the LORD — he "will not allow the soul of the righteous to go hungry" (10:3), and "the LORD's blessing brings wealth, and he adds no trouble to it" (10:22) — sayings about how his ordered world generally runs, not formulas for extracting favors.
**Tags:** `taming-the-tongue` — the chapter's densest thread: "he who restrains his lips does wisely," the mouth as spring of life or fountain of violence (10:11, 10:18–21, 10:31–32); `work-and-diligence` — "He becomes poor who works with a lazy hand, but the hand of the diligent brings wealth" (10:4–5, 10:26); `gods-provision` — "The LORD will not allow the soul of the righteous to go hungry" (10:3); `blessing` — "The LORD's blessing brings wealth, and he adds no trouble to it" (10:22, 10:6–7; a generalization about blessing's source, not a formula — see Decisions record #4); `integrity` — "He who walks blamelessly walks surely" (10:9)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings

## Proverbs 11
**Summary:** A chapter of mixed sayings whose recurring notes are honest dealing, humility, and the strange economics of generosity. It opens at the market stall: "A false balance is an abomination to the LORD, but accurate weights are his delight" (11:1). "When pride comes, then comes shame, but with humility comes wisdom" (11:2); gossip betrays confidences while the trustworthy keep them; "in the multitude of counselors there is victory" (11:14). Riches cannot deliver in the day of wrath, and whoever trusts in them will fall — yet giving multiplies: "There is one who scatters, and increases yet more… The liberal soul shall be made fat. He who waters shall be watered also himself" (11:24–25). A city rises or falls with the character of its people, and "the fruit of the righteous is a tree of life. He who is wise wins souls" (11:30).
**Tags:** `honesty` — "A false balance is an abomination to the LORD, but accurate weights are his delight" (11:1, 11:3); `generosity` — the scatterer who increases: "The liberal soul shall be made fat. He who waters shall be watered also himself" (11:24–26); `humble-exaltation` — "When pride comes, then comes shame, but with humility comes wisdom" (11:2); `mercy` — the human-mercy side: "The merciful man does good to his own soul" (11:17); `money-and-possessions` — riches' limits: they cannot deliver in the day of wrath, and "He who trusts in his riches will fall" (11:4, 11:28); `sowing-and-reaping` — "one who sows righteousness reaps a sure reward" — a generalization about God's ordered world, not a formula (11:18)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings

## Proverbs 12
**Summary:** Mixed sayings, with the tongue and the plow doing the most work. Truth outlasts lies — "Truth's lips will be established forever, but a lying tongue is only momentary" (12:19), and "lying lips are an abomination to the LORD, but those who do the truth are his delight" (12:22). Words can wound or mend: "There is one who speaks rashly like the piercing of a sword, but the tongue of the wise heals" (12:18), and "anxiety in a man's heart weighs it down, but a kind word makes it glad" (12:25). Steady work beats chasing fantasies — "He who tills his land shall have plenty of bread" (12:11); "the hands of the diligent ones shall rule" (12:24) — and the chapter's kindness reaches even the barnyard: "A righteous man respects the life of his animal" (12:10).
**Tags:** `honesty` — "Lying lips are an abomination to the LORD, but those who do the truth are his delight" (12:17, 12:19, 12:22); `taming-the-tongue` — rash words that pierce, wise words that heal, kind words that lift a weighed-down heart (12:18, 12:25); `work-and-diligence` — "He who tills his land shall have plenty of bread, but he who chases fantasies is void of understanding" (12:11, 12:24, 12:27)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings

## Proverbs 13
**Summary:** Mixed sayings on teachability, money, companions, and the home. The wise son listens; the scoffer won't hear rebuke; and desire without diligence starves — "the soul of the sluggard desires, and has nothing, but the desire of the diligent shall be fully satisfied" (13:4). Wealth gained dishonestly dwindles away, and "hope deferred makes the heart sick, but when longing is fulfilled, it is a tree of life" (13:11–12). Company shapes character: "One who walks with wise men grows wise, but a companion of fools suffers harm" (13:20). And love disciplines: "One who spares the rod hates his son, but one who loves him is careful to discipline him" (13:24) — with a longer horizon in view, since "a good man leaves an inheritance to his children's children" (13:22).
**Tags:** `parenting` — "One who spares the rod hates his son, but one who loves him is careful to discipline him" (13:1, 13:24); `work-and-diligence` — the sluggard's empty desire against the diligent fully satisfied (13:4, 13:11); `friendship` — "One who walks with wise men grows wise, but a companion of fools suffers harm" (13:20); `money-and-possessions` — the honest-earning register: "Wealth gained dishonestly dwindles away" (13:11)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings

## Proverbs 14
**Summary:** Mixed sayings, many about seeing straight. Self-assessment fails without wisdom — "There is a way which seems right to a man, but in the end it leads to death" (14:12) — while "a simple man believes everything, but the prudent man carefully considers his ways" (14:15). Temper tells: "He who is slow to anger has great understanding, but he who has a quick temper displays folly" (14:29), and "envy rots the bones" while "the life of the body is a heart at peace" (14:30). ## Proverbs 15
**Summary:** Mixed sayings in which words and worship keep close company. The opening line is the book's most quoted counsel on conflict: "A gentle answer turns away wrath, but a harsh word stirs up anger" (15:1) — a gentle tongue is "a tree of life" (15:4). Nothing is hidden from God: "The LORD's eyes are everywhere, keeping watch on the evil and the good" (15:3), and he weighs worship by the worshiper — "the sacrifice made by the wicked is an abomination to the LORD, but the prayer of the upright is his delight" (15:8); "he hears the prayer of the righteous" (15:29). A run of "better" sayings prices contentment — "Better is little, with the fear of the LORD, than great treasure with trouble. ## Proverbs 16
**Summary:** Mixed sayings, gathered unusually tightly around the LORD's sovereignty over human plans. "The plans of the heart belong to man, but the answer of the tongue is from the LORD" (16:1); "a man's heart plans his course, but the LORD directs his steps" (16:9); even randomness answers to him — "the lot is cast into the lap, but its every decision is from the LORD" (16:33). So the counsel is surrender: "Commit your deeds to the LORD, and your plans shall succeed" (16:3) — with success defined by his weighing of motives, not by self-assessment, since "all the ways of a man are clean in his own eyes" (16:2). A royal cluster measures kings by righteousness; the most famous line warns the self-sufficient — "Pride goes before destruction, and an arrogant spirit before a fall" (16:18) — and strength is redefined: "one who is slow to anger is better than the mighty; one who rules his spirit, than he who takes a city" (16:32).

## Proverbs 17
**Summary:** Mixed sayings, with strife and friendship as the loudest notes. Peace outranks plenty — "Better is a dry morsel with quietness, than a house full of feasting with strife" (17:1) — and quarrels are best stopped before they start: "The beginning of strife is like breaching a dam, therefore stop contention before quarreling breaks out" (17:14). Love keeps short accounts: "He who covers an offense promotes love; but he who repeats a matter separates best friends" (17:9), and the book's finest line on loyalty lands here: "A friend loves at all times; and a brother is born for adversity" (17:17). Above the human scene, "the refining pot is for silver, and the furnace for gold, but the LORD tests the hearts" (17:3), and he calls both corrupt verdicts by name: justifying the wicked and condemning the righteous "are an abomination to the LORD" (17:15). Body and soul are one: "A cheerful heart makes good medicine, but a crushed spirit dries up the bones" (17:22).

## Proverbs 18
**Summary:** Mixed sayings clustered around words, conflict, and where safety really lies. ## Proverbs 19
**Summary:** Mixed sayings on poverty, honesty, and teachability. ## Proverbs 20

## Proverbs 21
**Summary:** Mixed sayings under one open claim of sovereignty: "The king's heart is in the LORD's hand like the watercourses. He turns it wherever he desires" (21:1) — and what holds for kings holds for everyone, since "the LORD weighs the hearts" (21:2). Religion cannot substitute for righteousness: "To do righteousness and justice is more acceptable to the LORD than sacrifice" (21:3), and "the sacrifice of the wicked is an abomination" (21:27). The chapter watches money and mercy trade places — "whoever stops his ears at the cry of the poor, he will also cry out, but shall not be heard" (21:13), while "the righteous give and don't withhold" (21:26) — and it ends where it began, with human strength relativized: "There is no wisdom nor understanding nor counsel against the LORD. ## Proverbs 22
**Summary:** The Solomon collection closes and a new one opens mid-chapter. The closing sayings prize reputation and humility — "A good name is more desirable than great riches" (22:1); "the result of humility and the fear of the LORD is wealth, honor, and life" (22:4, a generalization about where honor comes from, not a bargain) — level rich and poor before one Maker (22:2), and give the book's most quoted parenting line: "Train up a child in the way he should go, and when he is old he will not depart from it" (22:6). At 22:17 a new voice begins: "Turn your ear, and listen to the words of the wise" — "thirty excellent things of counsel and knowledge" (22:20) written "so that your trust may be in the LORD" (22:19). Its first sayings defend the powerless: "Don't exploit the poor because he is poor… for the LORD will plead their case" (22:22–23), avoid the hot-tempered friend and the debt-pledge that costs your bed, and honor the ancient boundary stone.

- **22:17–29 — Thirty Sayings of the Wise** (title: BSB) — A new collection opens: thirty excellent sayings to ground your trust in the LORD, beginning with justice for the poor at the gate, distance from the hot-tempered, refusal of rash debt-pledges, and the skilled worker who will serve kings.

## Proverbs 23
**Summary:** The thirty sayings continue in longer strokes: restrain appetite at a ruler's table; "don't weary yourself to be rich," for wealth "certainly sprouts wings like an eagle" (23:4–5); don't eat a stingy man's bread, move the ancient boundary, or encroach on the fatherless — "for their Defender is strong" (23:11). Discipline is love with a future — "don't withhold correction from a child… punish him with the rod, and save his soul from Sheol" (23:13–14) — and the parents' joy hangs on the son's heart: "My son, give me your heart" (23:26). "Don't let your heart envy sinners, but rather fear the LORD all day long. Indeed surely there is a future hope" (23:17–18). Honor is owed at both ends of life — "listen to your father who gave you life, and don't despise your mother when she is old" (23:22) — and the chapter closes with Scripture's most vivid drunkard portrait: woe, sorrow, bloodshot eyes, the sea-sick stagger, and the craving that wakes up asking for more, for wine "bites like a snake, and poisons like a viper" (23:32).

## Proverbs 24
**Summary:** The thirty sayings close, and a short appendix follows. Don't envy evil men or fret over evildoers, "for there will be no reward to the evil man. The lamp of the wicked will be snuffed out" (24:19–20) — and don't gloat either: "Don't rejoice when your enemy falls" (24:17). Wisdom builds the house and wins the war, "for by wise guidance you wage your war, and victory is in many advisors" (24:6). Moral courage is pressed — "if you falter in the time of trouble, your strength is small" (24:10); "rescue those who are being led away to death," for ignorance is no excuse before "he who weighs the hearts" (24:11–12) — and the righteous man's resilience gets the section's most famous line: he "falls seven times and rises up again" (24:16). The appendix ("These also are sayings of the wise," 24:23) forbids partiality in judgment and private revenge — "don't say, 'I will do to him as he has done to me'" (24:29) — and ends in the sluggard's vineyard, all thorns and broken wall, where the sage relearns that "a little sleep, a little slumber" ends in poverty like an armed man (24:33–34).

## Proverbs 25
**Summary:** "These also are proverbs of Solomon, which the men of Hezekiah king of Judah copied out" (25:1) — a collection preserved two centuries after Solomon, and it opens at court: "It is the glory of God to conceal a thing, but the glory of kings is to search out a matter" (25:2). Humility beats self-promotion — "it is better that it be said to you, 'Come up here,' than that you should be put lower" (25:7) — and words are weighed like jewelry: "a word fitly spoken is like apples of gold in settings of silver" (25:11), a wise reprover "an earring of gold" to a listening ear (25:12), while "a soft tongue breaks the bone" (25:15). The collection's moral peak is enemy-kindness: "If your enemy is hungry, give him food to eat… for you will heap coals of fire on his head, and the LORD will reward you" (25:21–22). It closes on the unguarded self: "Like a city that is broken down and without walls is a man whose spirit is without restraint" (25:28).

## Proverbs 26
**Summary:** A rogues' gallery in proverbs: the fool, the sluggard, the meddler, the gossip, and the flatterer. Honor no fool — it fits him "like snow in summer" (26:1) — and answer him carefully: the paired sayings "don't answer a fool according to his folly" and "answer a fool according to his folly" (26:4–5) demand discernment about which moment is which. "As a dog that returns to his vomit, so is a fool who repeats his folly" (26:11) — yet one case is worse: "Do you see a man wise in his own eyes? There is more hope for a fool than for him" (26:12). The sluggard turns on his bed "as the door turns on its hinges" (26:14). Quarrels die without fuel — "without gossip, a quarrel dies down" (26:20) — but the whisperer's words "go down into the innermost parts" (26:22), and hidden malice self-destructs: "Whoever digs a pit shall fall into it" (26:27); "a flattering mouth works ruin" (26:28).

## Proverbs 27
**Summary:** Mixed sayings on time, praise, and the friends who shape us. Tomorrow is not yours to promise — "Don't boast about tomorrow; for you don't know what a day may bring" (27:1) — and praise is not yours to give yourself: "Let another man praise you, and not your own mouth" (27:2); indeed "man is refined by his praise" the way a furnace refines gold (27:21). The chapter is the book's richest on friendship: "The wounds of a friend are faithful, although the kisses of an enemy are profuse" (27:6); "Iron sharpens iron; so a man sharpens his friend's countenance" (27:17); and a near neighbor beats a distant brother in the day of disaster (27:10). It ends with a small pastoral economics lesson: "Know well the state of your flocks" (27:23), "for riches are not forever" (27:24) — steady, attentive husbandry, not hoarded wealth, feeds a household.
**Tags:** `friendship` — faithful wounds, iron sharpening iron, the counsel of a friend like perfume and incense (27:5–6, 27:9–10, 27:17); `humble-exaltation` — praise left to other mouths, honor not self-sought, the crucible of being praised (27:2, 27:21); `work-and-diligence` — "Know well the state of your flocks," for riches are not forever (27:23–27); `receiving-correction` — "Better is open rebuke than hidden love," the friend's faithful wounds welcomed (27:5–6). ## Proverbs 28
**Summary:** Mixed sayings with a public edge: many lines weigh rulers, law, and the moral nerve of a society. "The wicked flee when no one pursues; but the righteous are as bold as a lion" (28:1). Law-keeping and God-seeking go together — "those who seek the LORD understand it fully" (28:5) — and religion divorced from obedience curdles: "He who turns away his ear from hearing the law, even his prayer is an abomination" (28:9). The chapter carries the book's clearest gospel-shaped saying: "He who conceals his sins doesn't prosper, but whoever confesses and renounces them finds mercy" (28:13), beside its warning twin, "blessed is the man who always fears; but one who hardens his heart falls into trouble" (28:14). Money sayings cut both ways — exploitive interest gathers wealth for someone kinder (28:8), "one who gives to the poor has no lack" (28:27) — and the true ledger of trust reads: "One who trusts in himself is a fool," but "one who trusts in the LORD will prosper" (28:25–26).
**Tags:** `repentance` — "whoever confesses and renounces them finds mercy" — concealment against confession (28:13); `trust-in-god` — trust in the LORD against trust in self and in riches (28:25–26, 28:11); `generosity` — "One who gives to the poor has no lack; but one who closes his eyes will have many curses" (28:8, 28:27); `hardness-of-heart` — the warning twin of the confession saying: "one who hardens his heart falls into trouble" (28:14); `justice-and-oppression` — "As a roaring lion or a charging bear, so is a wicked ruler over helpless people" (28:15–16); `money-and-possessions` — the get-rich-quick register: "one who is eager to be rich will not go unpunished," and the stingy man who "doesn't know that poverty waits for him" (28:20, 28:22); `unanswered-prayer` — prayer voided by refusing God's word: "He who turns away his ear from hearing the law, even his prayer is an abomination" (28:9)

## Proverbs 29
**Summary:** The Hezekiah collection closes with sayings about stubbornness, authority, and where safety lies. The opening is a hinge-warning for the whole book: "He who is often rebuked and stiffens his neck will be destroyed suddenly, with no remedy" (29:1). Rulers set a moral weather — "when the righteous thrive, the people rejoice; but when the wicked rule, the people groan" (29:2); "the king who fairly judges the poor, his throne shall be established forever" (29:14) — and "the righteous care about justice for the poor" (29:7). The home needs the same steady hand: "The rod of correction gives wisdom, but a child left to himself causes shame to his mother" (29:15); "correct your son, and he will give you peace" (29:17). Anger management is wisdom's test — "a fool vents all of his anger, but a wise man brings himself under control" (29:11) — and the chapter's summit contrasts two fears: "The fear of man proves to be a snare, but whoever puts his trust in the LORD is kept safe" (29:25), while "where there is no revelation, the people cast off restraint; but one who keeps the law is blessed" (29:18).
**Tags:** `parenting` — the rod of correction against the child left to himself; "Correct your son, and he will give you peace" (29:15, 29:17); `self-control` — the fool who vents everything, the man hasty in words, the wrathful man abounding in sin (29:11, 29:20, 29:22); `pleasing-god-not-people` — "The fear of man proves to be a snare, but whoever puts his trust in the LORD is kept safe" (29:25–26); `humble-exaltation` — "A man's pride brings him low, but one of lowly spirit gains honor" (29:23); `obedience-to-the-word` — "Where there is no revelation, the people cast off restraint; but one who keeps the law is blessed" (29:18); `justice-and-oppression` — "the righteous care about justice for the poor," and "the king who fairly judges the poor, his throne shall be established forever" (29:7, 29:14); `receiving-correction` — the hinge-warning: "He who is often rebuked and stiffens his neck will be destroyed suddenly, with no remedy" (29:1)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings

## Proverbs 30
**Summary:** "The words of Agur the son of Jakeh, the revelation" (30:1) — a new, humbler voice: "Surely I am the most ignorant man" (30:2). His opening riddle stretches toward a God beyond human grasp — "Who has ascended up into heaven, and descended? Who has gathered the wind in his fists?… What is his name, and what is his son's name, if you know?" (30:4) — and his creed answers with the sufficiency of what God has said: "Every word of God is flawless. He is a shield to those who take refuge in him. Don't you add to his words" (30:5–6). His famous two-petition prayer asks for truthfulness and for enough: "Give me neither poverty nor riches… lest I be full, deny you, and say, 'Who is the LORD?' or lest I be poor, and steal" (30:8–9). The rest is numbered observation — the never-satisfied leech and Sheol, four wonders "too amazing for me" ending with "the way of a man with a maiden" (30:18–19), four small-but-wise creatures led by the food-storing ants, four stately walkers — closing with counsel to clap a hand over a proud or scheming mouth, "for… the forcing of wrath produces strife" (30:32–33).
**Tags:** `contentment` — Agur's prayer: "Give me neither poverty nor riches. Feed me with the food that is needful for me" (30:7–9); `refuge-in-trouble` — "He is a shield to those who take refuge in him" (30:5); `humble-exaltation` — the sage who owns his ignorance, and the closing "put your hand over your mouth" if you have exalted yourself (30:2–3, 30:32); `slander-and-false-accusation` — "Don't slander a servant to his master" (30:10); `the-name-of-god` — the motive clause of Agur's prayer: to steal would be to "dishonor the name of my God" (30:9); `trustworthiness-of-scripture` — Agur's creed: "Every word of God is flawless," sufficient as given — "Don't you add to his words" (30:5–6)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings
*Note:* Agur's question "what is his son's name, if you know?" (30:4) presses the limits of human knowledge of God; Christian readers have long heard an anticipation of the Son revealed in the New Testament (so Brooks). That is a signposted later reading — in context the question belongs to Agur's confession that God is beyond his grasp.

## Proverbs 31
**Summary:** The book closes with "the words of King Lemuel — the revelation which his mother taught him" (31:1): a queen mother's charge to a king. Kings must not trade their strength for women or their clarity for wine — "it is not for kings to drink wine… lest they drink, and forget the law, and pervert the justice due to anyone who is afflicted" (31:4–5) — because royal power exists for the voiceless: "Open your mouth for the mute… judge righteously, and serve justice to the poor and needy" (31:8–9). Then an alphabet poem (each verse beginning with a successive Hebrew letter) answers the question "Who can find a worthy woman?" (31:10 — the WEB's phrase for the familiar "virtuous" or "excellent" wife). She is trusted by her husband, tireless in wool, flax, field, and vineyard, generous — "she opens her arms to the poor" (31:20) — dignified and unafraid of the future: "Strength and dignity are her clothing. She laughs at the time to come" (31:25); "she opens her mouth with wisdom" (31:26). Her secret is the book's motto brought home: "Charm is deceitful, and beauty is vain; but a woman who fears the LORD, she shall be praised" (31:30).
**Tags:** `godly-marriage` — a wife whose husband's heart "trusts in her," who "does him good, and not harm, all the days of her life," and who is praised by husband and children (31:10–12, 31:28–29); `work-and-diligence` — she "works eagerly with her hands," rises while it is night, plants a vineyard, and "doesn't eat the bread of idleness" (31:13–19, 31:27); `generosity` — "She opens her arms to the poor; yes, she extends her hands to the needy" (31:20); `drunkenness` — the queen mother's charge: "it is not for kings to drink wine… lest they drink, and forget the law, and pervert the justice due to anyone who is afflicted" (31:4–5); `fear-of-the-lord` — the acrostic's climax and the book's motto brought home: "a woman who fears the LORD, she shall be praised" (31:30); `justice-and-oppression` — "Open your mouth for the mute… judge righteously, and serve justice to the poor and needy" (31:8–9); `leadership` — how a king must rule: clear-headed, for "it is not for kings to drink wine," and a voice for the voiceless, opening his mouth for the mute (31:4–9)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings
**Sections:**
- **31:1–9 — The Sayings for King Lemuel** (title: BSB) — A mother's charge to her royal son: guard your strength, keep wine from clouding a king's justice, and open your mouth for the mute, the desolate, and the poor.
- **31:10–31 — The Virtues of a Noble Woman** (title: BSB) — The acrostic portrait of the worthy woman: trusted, industrious, enterprising, generous, wise of speech, clothed in strength and dignity — praised in the gates because she fears the LORD.
