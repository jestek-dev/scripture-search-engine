# Song of Solomon — book summary (draft)

## Short

Song of Solomon is the Bible's book of love poetry. In it a woman and a man delight in each other openly — praising each other's beauty in extravagant garden-and-vineyard imagery, aching in each other's absence, resting in mutual belonging: "My beloved is mine, and I am his." The poems move through courtship, a royal wedding day, and married delight, twice shadowed by night scenes of losing and seeking. Three times the woman charges the daughters of Jerusalem "that you not stir up, nor awaken love, until it so desires," and the book crests in her final charge: "Set me as a seal on your heart... for love is strong as death... Many waters can't quench love." The Song celebrates romantic love as a good and mighty gift; readers of both testaments have also long heard in it a picture of God's covenant love for his people — a later interpretive tradition, not a claim the text makes for itself.

## Full

The book announces itself as "The Song of songs, which is Solomon's" — Hebrew's way of saying *the best of songs* — and then gives eight chapters of love poetry with almost no narration. Two voices carry it: a woman (the WEB labels her the Beloved) and a man (labeled the Lover), with a chorus of Friends and, once, the woman's Brothers. The woman speaks first, last, and most. There is no plot in the strict sense, but there is movement: desire and courtship in the opening chapters, a wedding at the center, and a seasoned, settled love by the end — interrupted twice by dreamlike nights in which the beloved is lost, sought through the city streets, and found (or not found, and grieved).

The poetry works by comparison, and the comparisons are drawn from a whole countryside: doves, gazelles, vineyards in blossom, henna and myrrh and every spice, Lebanon's cedars, David's tower, the pools in Heshbon. The man's praise-poems move down the woman's form feature by feature; hers answers in kind — "white and ruddy. The best among ten thousand... he is altogether lovely." The frankest passages keep this indirection: the bride is "a locked up garden; a locked up spring, a sealed fountain," and consummation is her invitation — "Let my beloved come into his garden" — and his acceptance — "I have come into my garden, my sister, my bride" — sealed by the Friends' blessing, "Eat, friends! Drink, yes, drink abundantly, beloved." The Song is sensuous without ever being crude, and exclusive without ever being grim: one man, one woman, each other's, by free delight.

Two refrains give the book its spine. "My beloved is mine, and I am his" (2:16) returns as "I am my beloved's, and my beloved is mine" (6:3) and ripens into "I am my beloved's. His desire is toward me" (7:10). And three times the woman puts the daughters of Jerusalem under oath "that you not stir up, nor awaken love, until it so desires" (2:7; 3:5; 8:4) — love this strong is not to be forced or hurried. Both refrains flow into the summit at 8:6–7: love is "strong as death," its flashes are fire, "Many waters can't quench love," and all the wealth of a house offered for it "would be utterly scorned." In that saying our WEB translation reads the blaze as "a very flame of the LORD" — the only mention of God in the whole book, and a rendering some translations give instead as simply a mighty flame; the Song otherwise never names, addresses, or quotes God. It teaches by celebration, not command.

What, then, is this book doing in the Bible? On its face it is a witness that romantic and physical love between a woman and a man is God's good gift — worth songs, worth patience, worth guarding, stronger than death and beyond price. Readers have long heard more: Jewish tradition read the Song as the love between God and Israel, and Christian tradition (including both public-domain commentaries consulted for this draft) as the love between Christ and the church — readings encouraged by Scripture's own habit of casting covenant love as marriage (Hosea; Ephesians 5). Those are venerable interpretive traditions, and this document signposts them as such; the summaries below stay with what the text itself sings.

# The eight chapters

## Song of Solomon 1
**Summary:** The book announces itself — "The Song of songs, which is Solomon's" — and the woman's voice opens the poetry with desire: "Let him kiss me with the kisses of his mouth; for your love is better than wine" (our WEB translation labels the speakers — the woman "Beloved," the man "Lover," a chorus "Friends"). Before the daughters of Jerusalem she is self-conscious and unbowed — "I am dark, but lovely," sun-scorched from keeping the vineyards her angry brothers ("my mother's sons") assigned her, while "I haven't kept my own vineyard." She asks where the one her soul loves grazes his flock; he answers her, "most beautiful among women," to follow the tracks of the sheep to the shepherds' tents. The rest of the chapter settles into answering praise — he compares her "to a steed in Pharaoh's chariots" with jeweled cheeks and neck, she calls him "a sachet of myrrh" and "a cluster of henna blossoms," he tells her "Behold, you are beautiful, my love... Your eyes are like doves" — until the green outdoors becomes their house: "our couch is verdant," cedar beams, fir rafters.
**Tags:** `romantic-love-and-intimacy` — the woman's voice opens the book's celebration of love: "Let him kiss me with the kisses of his mouth; for your love is better than wine" (1:2), answered through the chapter in the lovers' mutual praise. ## Song of Solomon 2
**Summary:** The woman names herself modestly — "I am a rose of Sharon, a lily of the valleys" — and the man tops the figure: beside her, the other daughters are thorns. Under his shadow she tastes sweet fruit — "He brought me to the banquet hall. His banner over me is love" — and she is so "faint with love" she asks to be strengthened with raisins and apples. Then comes the book's refrain, laid for the first time on the daughters of Jerusalem: "that you not stir up, nor awaken love, until it so desires" — love this strong must not be forced or hurried. The rest is springtime: her beloved comes "leaping on the mountains," calling her out — "Rise up, my love, my beautiful one, and come away," for "the winter is past. The rain is over and gone" — with a playful charge to catch "the little foxes that plunder the vineyards" while the vines are in blossom. The chapter rests on the book's belonging-refrain: "My beloved is mine, and I am his."
**Tags:** `romantic-love-and-intimacy` — the chapter carries both registers the adopted concept serves: delight ("His banner over me is love," 2:4, and the belonging-refrain "My beloved is mine, and I am his," 2:16) and love's right timing ("that you not stir up, nor awaken love, until it so desires," 2:7 — the awaken-love refrain formerly logged as a separate vocabulary gap and folded into this concept's row at adoption). ## Song of Solomon 3
**Summary:** Two scenes share the chapter. In the first, by night on her bed the woman seeks "him whom my soul loves" and doesn't find him; she rises to circle the city's streets and squares, asks the watchmen, and scarcely past them finds him — "I held him, and would not let him go" — until she has brought him to her mother's house; the refrain about not awakening love follows. The second scene is a wedding: "Who is this who comes up from the wilderness like pillars of smoke," perfumed with myrrh and frankincense? It is Solomon's carriage — sixty of Israel's mighty men around it, swords ready "because of fear in the night," its pillars silver, its bottom gold, its seat purple, "the middle of it being paved with love." The daughters of Zion are called out to see King Solomon crowned by his mother "in the day of his weddings, in the day of the gladness of his heart."

## Song of Solomon 4
**Summary:** The man's longest praise-poem moves feature by feature down his bride — dove's eyes behind her veil, hair "as a flock of goats" descending Gilead, teeth like a newly shorn flock, lips "like scarlet thread," a neck like David's tower hung with shields, breasts like twin fawns — and then sums her up whole: "You are all beautiful, my love. There is no spot in you." For the first time he calls her "my sister, my bride": "You have ravished my heart... with one of your eyes," and her love is better than wine. The imagery then gathers into an enclosed place kept for one person — "My sister, my bride, is a locked up garden; a locked up spring, a sealed fountain" — yet inside, an orchard of pomegranates, henna, spikenard, saffron, cinnamon, and every best spice, "a well of living waters." The chapter closes with her answer, freely given: let the winds blow on my garden — "Let my beloved come into his garden, and taste his precious fruits."
**Tags:** `godly-marriage` — the bridal chapter: "my sister, my bride" runs through it (4:8–12), and the "locked up garden; a locked up spring, a sealed fountain" (4:12) is the book's own picture of a love kept exclusively for one, given freely at her invitation (4:16); `romantic-love-and-intimacy` — the man's longest praise-poem moves down his bride ("You are all beautiful, my love. There is no spot in you"), and the garden imagery crests in her freely given invitation, "Let my beloved come into his garden, and taste his precious fruits" (4:1–16) — intimacy celebrated in the text's own non-graphic figures. ## Song of Solomon 5
**Summary:** The wedding poetry completes: "I have come into my garden, my sister, my bride," the man answers — myrrh and spice, honeycomb and honey, wine and milk — and the Friends bless the union: "Eat, friends! Drink, yes, drink abundantly, beloved." Then the mood turns. Half-asleep, the woman hears her beloved knocking — "Open to me, my sister, my love, my dove, my undefiled" — and hesitates over her shed robe and washed feet; by the time she rises, hands dripping myrrh on the lock's handles, "my beloved left, and had gone away," and her calling gets no answer. This night-search fares worse than the first: the watchmen beat and bruise her and take away her cloak, and she can only charge the daughters of Jerusalem to tell him "that I am faint with love." Their challenge — "How is your beloved better than another beloved?" — draws out the book's one head-to-toe praise of the man: "white and ruddy. The best among ten thousand," a head of purest gold, eyes like doves by the brooks, legs like marble pillars — "yes, he is altogether lovely. This is my beloved, and this is my friend, daughters of Jerusalem."
**Tags:** `godly-marriage` — the wedding sequence completes here: the bridal address "I have come into my garden, my sister, my bride" and the Friends' blessing over the union, "Eat, friends! Drink, yes, drink abundantly, beloved" (5:1). A borderline call — the marriage marker is one verse, and the chapter's remaining fifteen are the loss-and-search and praise poems — recorded at Decisions record #4; `romantic-love-and-intimacy` — the consummation, "I have come into my garden, my sister, my bride" (5:1), the ache of the night-loss ("that I am faint with love"), and the woman's answering head-to-toe praise of the man: "white and ruddy. The best among ten thousand... yes, he is altogether lovely" (5:10–16). ## Song of Solomon 6
**Summary:** Where has your beloved gone? the Friends ask, offering to seek him with her — but this loss is not like the last: she knows where he is ("My beloved has gone down to his garden") and rests in the bond: "I am my beloved's, and my beloved is mine." The man answers with praise that reaches past prettiness for grandeur — "You are beautiful, my love, as Tirzah, lovely as Jerusalem, awesome as an army with banners" — her eyes so overcoming that he begs her to turn them away. Sixty queens, eighty concubines, virgins without number, and still: "My dove, my perfect one, is unique," her mother's favorite, called blessed by the daughters and praised by the queens themselves — "Who is she who looks out as the morning, beautiful as the moon, clear as the sun?" A trip down to the nut tree grove ends in a line even translators find dark — "Without realizing it, my desire set me with my royal people's chariots" — and the chapter closes with the call "Return, return, Shulammite!" (the only place the book names her) and the man's retort about gazing at her "as at the dance of Mahanaim."

## Song of Solomon 7
**Summary:** The admiration continues, now from the feet up: "How beautiful are your feet in sandals, prince's daughter!" Her thighs are like jewels from a craftsman's hands, her waist "like a heap of wheat, set about with lilies," her neck an ivory tower, her eyes the pools in Heshbon, her head like Carmel — until the praise overwhelms the praiser: "The king is held captive in its tresses." Her stature is a palm tree he means to climb, her mouth "like the best wine, that goes down smoothly for my beloved." Then the woman answers with the book's belonging-refrain in its ripest form — "I am my beloved's. His desire is toward me" — and now she leads: "Come, my beloved! Let's go out into the field," to lodge in the villages and rise early for the vineyards — "There I will give you my love" — where mandrakes give fragrance and she has stored up precious fruits, new and old, for him.
**Tags:** `romantic-love-and-intimacy` — the belonging-refrain in its ripest form, "I am my beloved's. His desire is toward me" (7:10), and the woman's own invitation, "Come, my beloved! Let's go out into the field" and "There I will give you my love" (7:11–12), closing a chapter of mutual admiration. ## Song of Solomon 8
**Summary:** The woman wishes love could be freer still: were he "like my brother," she could kiss him when she found him outside and no one would despise her, and bring him into her mother's house; the embrace-refrain and the adjuration both sound a final time — "that you not stir up, nor awaken love, until it so desires." The Friends see the pair returning — "Who is this who comes up from the wilderness, leaning on her beloved?" — and then comes the book's summit, her charge to him: "Set me as a seal on your heart, as a seal on your arm; for love is strong as death," its flashes fire — in our WEB translation "a very flame of the LORD," the book's only mention of God, where some translations read simply a mighty flame — and "Many waters can't quench love, neither can floods drown it"; offer all the wealth of a house for love, and the offer "would be utterly scorned." Her Brothers' old worry over their "little sister" — is she a wall to crown or a door to board up? — is answered by the grown woman herself: "I am a wall... then I was in his eyes like one who found peace." Let Solomon keep the thousand shekels of silver from his leased vineyard at Baal Hamon — "My own vineyard is before me": she has given herself, and no one bought her. The book ends still mid-love: he asks to hear her voice, and she calls, "Come away, my beloved! Be like a gazelle or a young stag on the mountains of spices!"
**Tags:** `godly-marriage` — the covenant-love summit, a staple of wedding readings: "Set me as a seal on your heart, as a seal on your arm; for love is strong as death," unquenchable and beyond purchase (8:6–7), answered by "then I was in his eyes like one who found peace" (8:10); `romantic-love-and-intimacy` — the book's summit belongs to this concept's row as well: "Set me as a seal on your heart, as a seal on your arm; for love is strong as death" and "Many waters can't quench love, neither can floods drown it" (8:6–7), with the awaken-love adjuration's final sounding at 8:4 — the refrain formerly logged as a separate gap and folded into this concept's row at adoption.
