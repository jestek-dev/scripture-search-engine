# Ruth — book summary (draft)

## Short

Ruth is a small story with a wide reach. Set "in the days when the judges judged," it follows Naomi, an Israelite widow who buries her husband and both sons in Moab, and the Moabite daughter-in-law who refuses to leave her: "your people will be my people, and your God my God." Back in Bethlehem and destitute, Ruth gleans leftover grain in Boaz's field — he is a wealthy relative of Naomi's dead husband — and his kindness grows from extra barley to marriage as he takes up the role the WEB calls the "near kinsman" — the relative with the right to buy back a dead man's land and stand up for his family. Naomi, who came home "empty," ends the book with a grandson in her lap — and the closing genealogy lands the surprise: that child, Obed, is "the father of Jesse, the father of David." A story of famine, grief, loyalty, and ordinary fieldwork turns out to carry the line of Israel's greatest king.

## Full

Ruth opens in the era of the judges, and it reads like relief after Judges' violence: no wars, no idols smashed, no miracles on stage — just a famine, three funerals, two harvests, a marriage, and a birth. The book's scale is deliberately small. Its four chapters trace one family's road from emptiness to fullness, and its theology mostly travels in the mouths of its characters, who keep asking the LORD to bless, repay, and provide — prayers the story then quietly answers, usually through people doing kind and lawful things for each other.

Chapter 1 empties Naomi out. Famine sends her family from Bethlehem to Moab; her husband Elimelech dies there, her two sons marry Moabite women and then die too, and she starts home with nothing, urging her daughters-in-law to go back and remarry. Orpah goes; Ruth clings, binding herself by oath to Naomi, Naomi's people, and Naomi's God — "where you go, I will go... Your people will be my people, and your God my God." Naomi reaches Bethlehem so bitter she renames herself: "Call me Mara, for the Almighty has dealt very bitterly with me... I went out full, and the LORD has brought me home again empty." The book takes her complaint seriously — and then spends three chapters answering it.

The middle chapters run on two engines: hidden providence and human kindness. Ruth goes out to glean — gathering what harvesters leave behind, the poor's share in Israel — and the narrator notes, with a straight face, that "she happened to come" to the field of Boaz, of all people: a wealthy, godly relative of Elimelech. Boaz's generosity exceeds every requirement — protection, water, a place at the meal, grain pulled from the bundles on purpose — and he names the reality underneath it, praying Ruth a full reward "from the LORD, the God of Israel, under whose wings you have come to take refuge." Naomi recognizes the name at once: Boaz is "one of our near kinsmen," the relative with the right to redeem — buy back — a dead man's land and care for those he left. On that right she builds a bold, honorable plan: Ruth, at the threshing floor by night, asks Boaz to spread the corner of his garment over her — a request for marriage under the kinsman's duty. Boaz, calling her "a worthy woman," pledges himself, but tells the truth even when it risks his hopes: a nearer kinsman has the prior right.

Chapter 4 settles everything in daylight, at the town gate, before elders and witnesses. The nearer kinsman wants the land until it comes with Ruth and the duty "to raise up the name of the dead on his inheritance," and he steps aside; Boaz buys, marries, and "the LORD enabled her to conceive" — one of the only actions the narrator attributes directly to God in the whole book. The women of Bethlehem speak over Naomi's grandson words that reverse chapter 1 point by point — the LORD has not left you without a near kinsman; this child is "a restorer of life"; the daughter-in-law who loves you "is better to you than seven sons" — and Naomi takes the child and lays him in her bosom. Then the genealogy: Obed is the father of Jesse, the father of David. The book never preaches its point; it simply shows a Moabite widow's loyalty, a landowner's costly kindness, and a God who fills empty hands — and then reveals that this unnoticed faithfulness was carrying Israel's royal future the whole time.

# The four chapters

## Ruth 1
**Summary:** In the days when the judges judged, famine drives Elimelech of Bethlehem to the country of Moab with his wife Naomi and their two sons — and there Naomi loses everything: Elimelech dies, the sons marry Moabite women, Orpah and Ruth, and after about ten years both sons die too. Hearing that "the LORD had visited his people in giving them bread," Naomi sets out for home and urges her daughters-in-law back to their mothers' houses with a blessing, reasoning that she has no more sons to give them. Orpah tearfully turns back, but Ruth stays, binding herself by oath: "where you go, I will go; and where you stay, I will stay. Your people will be my people, and your God my God" — only death will part them. The two women reach Bethlehem at the beginning of barley harvest, where Naomi tells the stirred-up town to call her not Naomi but Mara — a name meaning bitter — "for the Almighty has dealt very bitterly with me... I went out full, and the LORD has brought me home again empty."
**Tags:** `pastoral-grief-and-loss` — Naomi buries husband and both sons: "the woman was bereaved of her two children and of her husband," and she carries the loss God-ward — "the Almighty has dealt very bitterly with me" (1:3–5, 13, 20–21); `loving-others` — Ruth's costly love holds when everything says go home: "Don't urge me to leave you... Where you die, I will die" (1:14, 16–17), a kindness Naomi has already named — "as you have dealt with the dead and with me" (1:8); `faith` — a Moabite stakes her future on Israel's God, sealed with an oath in his name: "Your people will be my people, and your God my God... May the LORD do so to me, and more also" (1:16–17); `gods-provision` — the turn home rests on God's giving: "the LORD had visited his people in giving them bread" (1:6); `lament` — Naomi's complaint is voiced straight to God and honored by the book rather than rebuked: "the LORD's hand has gone out against me," and the Mara speech — "the Almighty has dealt very bitterly with me... I went out full, and the LORD has brought me home again empty" (1:13, 20–21); `oaths-and-vows` — Ruth's loyalty is sealed by a formal oath in the LORD's name: "May the LORD do so to me, and more also, if anything but death parts you and me" (1:16–17)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings

## Ruth 2
**Summary:** Left to feed a household of two, Ruth asks leave to glean — gathering the grain harvesters leave behind, the poor's provision in Israel — and "she happened to come to the portion of the field belonging to Boaz," a "mighty man of wealth" from the family of Naomi's dead husband. Boaz notices the foreigner, and his kindness runs far past custom: stay close to my maidens, drink what the young men draw, come eat at meal time — and, quietly, to his workers: "Let her glean even among the sheaves, and don't reproach her," and pull grain from the bundles on purpose for her. He tells Ruth why: everything she did for her mother-in-law has been told to him, and he prays her wages in full "from the LORD, the God of Israel, under whose wings you have come to take refuge." Ruth works from morning until evening and beats out about an ephah of barley — a strikingly large load for a day of gleaning — and when Naomi hears whose field it was, her bitterness gives way to blessing — "May he be blessed by the LORD, who has not abandoned his kindness to the living and to the dead" — and to recognition: "The man is a close relative to us, one of our near kinsmen" — the WEB's term for the relative Israel's law entitled to buy back ("redeem") a dead man's land and stand up for the family he left. Ruth stays with Boaz's maidens through barley and wheat harvest, living with her mother-in-law.
**Tags:** `gods-provision` — the hungry widow "ate, was satisfied, and left some of it," and carries home "about an ephah of barley" plus her saved leftovers (2:14, 17–18); `providence` — the narrator's pointed coincidence: "she happened to come to the portion of the field belonging to Boaz, who was of the family of Elimelech" — the one field where everything the book needs can unfold (2:1, 3); `generosity` — Boaz gives beyond every custom: a seat at the meal, standing orders to "pull out some for her from the bundles, and leave it," and no reproach for gleaning among the sheaves (2:14–16); `refuge-in-trouble` — Boaz names where Ruth has actually fled for shelter: "the LORD, the God of Israel, under whose wings you have come to take refuge" (2:12); `hospitality` — a self-described foreigner ("Why have I found favor... since I am a foreigner?") is welcomed, protected from the young men, watered, and fed at the table (2:8–10, 14); `work-and-diligence` — she "has continued even from the morning until now," gleans until evening, and beats out what she gathered (2:7, 17); `justice-and-oppression` — the provision-care side of this concept: Israel's gleaning law — the poor's share — working as intended and exceeded, as Ruth asks leave to glean and Boaz orders "Let her glean even among the sheaves, and don't reproach her" (2:2–3, 7–9, 15–16); `sojourners-and-strangers` — the foreigner welcomed into Israel: Ruth names her own status — "Why have I found favor... since I am a foreigner?" — and the Moabitess stays on among Boaz's maidens through both harvests (2:6, 10, 21)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings

## Ruth 3
**Summary:** Naomi turns matchmaker on the strength of the kinsman right: seeking "rest" for Ruth — a secure home of her own — she sends her, washed and dressed, to the threshing floor where Boaz winnows barley, telling her to note where he lies down, uncover his feet, and wait for his word. At midnight the startled Boaz finds a woman at his feet, and Ruth asks for what the kinsman's duty allows: "spread the corner of your garment over your servant; for you are a near kinsman" — a formal appeal for marriage and protection, which Boaz receives as an honor. He blesses her for showing "more kindness in the latter end than at the beginning" — she has sought her dead husband's family's good instead of following young men — calls her what "all the city of my people knows," a "worthy woman," and then volunteers the truth that puts his own hopes at risk: "there is a kinsman nearer than I," who must get first refusal; if that man will not act, "then I will do the duty of a kinsman for you, as the LORD lives." Before dawn he guards her reputation, loads her mantle with six measures of barley — "Don't go empty to your mother-in-law" — and Naomi counsels patience: "the man will not rest until he has settled this today."
**Tags:** `loving-others` — Ruth's loyalty is the chapter's hinge, named by Boaz himself: "You have shown more kindness in the latter end than at the beginning, because you didn't follow young men, whether poor or rich" (3:10); `honesty` — Boaz tells the whole truth at his own expense: "Now it is true that I am a near kinsman. However, there is a kinsman nearer than I," and he submits his hopes to that man's prior right (3:12–13); `kindness` — the book's central virtue named by name, from Boaz's own mouth: "You have shown more kindness in the latter end than at the beginning, because you didn't follow young men, whether poor or rich" (3:10); `kinsman-redeemer` — Ruth's formal appeal to the institution itself: "spread the corner of your garment over your servant; for you are a near kinsman," met by Boaz's careful ordering of the nearer kinsman's prior right (3:9, 12–13); `oaths-and-vows` — Boaz binds his pledge with an oath: "then I will do the duty of a kinsman for you, as the LORD lives" (3:13)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings

## Ruth 4
**Summary:** Boaz settles the matter in daylight at the city gate, seating ten elders as witnesses. He offers the nearer kinsman first right to redeem Elimelech's land, and the man agrees — until Boaz adds that the purchase comes with "Ruth the Moabitess, the wife of the dead," along with the duty "to raise up the name of the dead on his inheritance"; unwilling to endanger his own inheritance, the man cedes his right and confirms it by the old custom of handing over his sandal. Before all the witnesses Boaz buys what was Elimelech's and his sons' and takes Ruth as his wife, and the people bless her into the company of Israel's founding mothers: "May the LORD make the woman who has come into your house like Rachel and like Leah, which both built the house of Israel." "The LORD enabled her to conceive," and a son is born; the women of Bethlehem answer Naomi's old bitterness point for point — "Blessed be the LORD, who has not left you today without a near kinsman"; he will be "a restorer of life"; the daughter-in-law who loves you "is better to you than seven sons" — and Naomi takes the child and lays him in her bosom, becoming his nurse. The women name the boy Obed — and the closing genealogy runs ten names from Perez to its quiet thunderclap: Obed "is the father of Jesse, the father of David."
**Tags:** `restoration` — the emptied woman is refilled: a redeemer at the gate, a child in her arms, "a restorer of life and sustain you in your old age" (4:13–17); `gods-faithfulness` — the women bless "the LORD, who has not left you today without a near kinsman" — his kindness has outlasted famine, the years in Moab, and three graves (4:14); `blessing` — blessing spoken and then granted: the gate's prayer "May the LORD make the woman... like Rachel and like Leah" and the house "like the house of Perez" is answered in a marriage and a son (4:11–13); `providence` — the chapter's ordinary events — a gate, a marriage, a birth — turn out to carry Israel's royal line: "the LORD enabled her to conceive," and the child is grandfather to David (4:13, 17, 21–22); `care-for-widows` — the widow redeemed and sustained: the purchase carries "the wife of the dead" with the duty to her husband's name, and the women bless "the LORD, who has not left you today without a near kinsman" — the child will be "a restorer of life and sustain you in your old age" (4:5, 10, 14–15); `kinsman-redeemer` — the institution enacted in full at the gate: land redeemed, the sandal handed over, and the duty "to raise up the name of the dead on his inheritance" taken up by Boaz before ten elders (4:1–10, 14)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings
**Sections:**
- **4:1–12 — Boaz Redeems Ruth** (title: BSB) — At the gate, before ten elders, the nearer kinsman declines to redeem once Ruth and the duty to the dead come with the land; the sandal changes hands, and Boaz buys the inheritance and claims Ruth as his wife before the witnesses, under the people's blessing.
- **4:13–17 — Boaz Marries Ruth** (title: BSB) — The LORD gives conception; a son is born, and the women of Bethlehem bless the LORD and name the child Obed — "A son is born to Naomi" — while Naomi takes him and lays him in her bosom, a "restorer of life."
- **4:18–22 — The Line of David** (title: BSB) — A ten-name genealogy from Perez through Boaz and Obed to Jesse and David, opening with the book's own structural marker: "this is the history of the generations of Perez."

# Decisions record (delegated defaults — each reversible on Jesse's word)

1. **Vocabulary per the project-wide rollout ruling: all 131 ids at commit b3f491d are usable, including the nine PR #43 ids** (`asking-in-gods-will`, `baptism`, `benediction`, `caring-for-aging-parents`, `christ-the-cornerstone`, `doubt`, `incarnation`, `justification-by-faith`, `trinity`), with any use of those nine to be recorded here as a reversible delegated default. All nine were considered for Ruth and none is used. The two nearest calls: `benediction` on the book's blessing speech (2:4; 4:11–12) was dropped — these are greetings and a community's marriage blessing, not the liturgical benediction text that concept's searchers want; `caring-for-aging-parents` on Ruth's support of Naomi (2:11, 18; 4:15) was dropped — the chapters' substance is loyal kindness between in-laws, not the aging-parent care register that concept's searchers need (only 4:15's "sustain you in your old age" brushes it); tagging it would stretch both texts. Tag ids are YAML file basenames throughout; one `pastoral-*` concept is tagged in Ruth (`pastoral-grief-and-loss`, whose inner id differs — basename used per the ruling).
2. **Chapter 1 NOT subdivided despite three Berean headings** (1:1 Naomi Becomes a Widow; 1:6 Ruth's Loyalty to Naomi; 1:19 The Return to Bethlehem). The three units are successive phases of a single homecoming narrative — loss, decision on the road, arrival — not divergent themes; subdividing would be navigation, which §4 does not permit (precedent: Genesis 3 kept whole across temptation, judgment, and expulsion; Joshua 19 kept whole across seven headings). Reversible: the BSB boundaries are recorded here and would partition cleanly (1:1–5, 1:6–18, 1:19–22).
3. **Chapter 4 subdivided into all three Berean units** (4:1–12; 4:13–17; 4:18–22). The chapter contains no creedal statement about God's deeds; if this reads as stretching the concept, drop it and the chapter keeps three tags. Reversible.
5. **`honesty` on chapter 3.** Boaz's unprompted disclosure against his own interest — "there is a kinsman nearer than I" (3:12–13) — is a positive depiction of costly truth-telling. Considered instead for chapter 4 (open dealing before witnesses), but the concept's register is truth-telling rather than commercial transparency, and 3:12 is the explicit truth-telling moment. Reversible; without it chapter 3 is a single-tag chapter.
