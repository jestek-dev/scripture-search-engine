# Mark — book summary (draft)

## Short

Mark is the shortest of the four Gospels and the fastest moving — one of its favorite words is “immediately,” and it hurries from scene to scene with almost no pause. Its opening line announces the whole book: “The beginning of the Good News of Jesus Christ, the Son of God.” The first half piles up works of power in Galilee — demons expelled, the sick healed, a storm stilled, thousands fed — while everyone asks who this man can be. At the book’s hinge Peter answers, “You are the Christ,” and from there Jesus walks the road to Jerusalem, telling his disciples three times that the Son of Man must suffer, die, and rise, and that following him means taking up a cross — for he “came not to be served but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom for many.” At the cross it is a Roman centurion who finally says out loud what the first verse claimed: “Truly this man was the Son of God!” — and at the empty tomb the message rings out, “He has risen!”

## Full

Mark tells the story of Jesus at a run. Its first verse is its title — “The beginning of the Good News of Jesus Christ, the Son of God” (1:1; “Good News” is our WEB translation’s word for the familiar “gospel”) — and instead of birth stories it opens with John baptizing in the wilderness, Jesus baptized and declared “my beloved Son” by a voice from the sky, forty days of temptation compressed into two verses, and the announcement that sets the theme: “The time is fulfilled, and God’s Kingdom is at hand! Repent, and believe in the Good News” (1:15). Mark prefers deeds to long discourses, and its narrative habit of “immediately” — again and again — gives the book its breathless pace.

The first half (chapters 1–8) is a gathering storm of power and a rising question. Jesus teaches with an authority that astonishes, commands unclean spirits, heals a leper with a touch, forgives sins, stills a storm with “Peace! Be still!”, delivers a man possessed by a legion of demons, raises a dead girl, and feeds five thousand and then four thousand from a few loaves. Strangely, he keeps ordering demons and the healed to keep quiet about him — yet the news spreads anyway. The people ask, “Who then is this, that even the wind and the sea obey him?” (4:41); his hometown takes offense at the carpenter; the religious leaders decide early that he must be destroyed; and even the disciples, in the boat with him, don’t yet understand — “their hearts were hardened” (6:52).

The turning point comes at Caesarea Philippi. To Jesus’ question “But who do you say that I am?” Peter answers, “You are the Christ” (8:29) — and immediately Jesus begins to teach “that the Son of Man must suffer many things, and be rejected by the elders, the chief priests, and the scribes, and be killed, and after three days rise again” (8:31). Three times on the road to Jerusalem he says it, and three times the disciples answer with fear, misunderstanding, or arguments about greatness — each time drawing the same kind of correction: whoever wants to be first must be last of all and servant of all, for “the Son of Man also came not to be served but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom for many” (10:45). Following this King means denying yourself and taking up your cross (8:34).

The final week takes up more than a third of the book. Jesus enters Jerusalem to shouts of “Hosanna!”, clears the temple, silences every challenge, and foretells the temple’s destruction and the Son of Man’s coming in clouds. Then the story slows almost to the hour: the anointing at Bethany, the Passover meal — “This is my blood of the new covenant, which is poured out for many” (14:24) — Gethsemane’s “Abba, Father… not what I desire, but what you desire” (14:36), betrayal with a kiss, a night trial where Jesus finally answers plainly, “I am” (14:62), Peter’s tears, and the cross, where darkness covers the land and Jesus dies with a loud cry. The temple veil tears from top to bottom, and the centurion facing him says, “Truly this man was the Son of God!” (15:39). The women who come to the tomb hear “He has risen! He is not here” and flee trembling, saying “nothing to anyone; for they were afraid” (16:6, 8); in the closing verses our WEB text carries, the risen Lord appears, rebukes the disciples’ unbelief, sends them to “preach the Good News to the whole creation” (16:15), and is received up into heaven — and they go out and preach everywhere, “the Lord working with them” (16:20).

# The sixteen chapters

## Mark 1
**Summary:** Mark opens with its title line — “The beginning of the Good News of Jesus Christ, the Son of God” — and moves fast: John baptizes in the wilderness, preaching “the baptism of repentance for forgiveness of sins” and announcing one mightier than himself; Jesus is baptized in the Jordan, the Spirit descends like a dove, and a voice from the sky says, “You are my beloved Son, in whom I am well pleased.” After forty days in the wilderness tempted by Satan, Jesus comes into Galilee preaching, “The time is fulfilled, and God’s Kingdom is at hand! Repent, and believe in the Good News,” and calls four fishermen who immediately leave their nets. A single Sabbath in Capernaum shows his authority: he teaches “as having authority, and not as the scribes,” expels an unclean spirit that calls him “the Holy One of God,” heals Simon’s fever-stricken mother-in-law, and by sunset the whole city is at the door. Before dawn he slips away to a deserted place to pray, then tours Galilee’s synagogues; a leper begging “If you want to, you can make me clean” is touched and cleansed — and though ordered to silence, spreads the news until Jesus can no longer openly enter a city.

Be made clean,” and a whole city’s sick brought to him at evening (1:40–42, 32–34); `prayer` — “Early in the morning, while it was still dark, he rose up and went out, and departed into a deserted place, and prayed there” (1:35); `kingdom-of-heaven` — the announcement that sets the theme: “The time is fulfilled, and God’s Kingdom is at hand! Repent, and believe in the Good News” (1:14–15); `discipleship` — the first call to follow: “Come after me, and I will make you into fishers for men,” and the four leave nets, boat, and father immediately (1:16–20); `deliverance-from-demons` — the Capernaum synagogue exorcism — an unclean spirit silenced and cast out — and demons expelled through the evening and across Galilee (1:21–28, 32–34, 39)

**Sections:**
- **1:1–11 — The Mission of John the Baptist** (title: BSB) — John appears in the wilderness baptizing and preaching repentance, announces the mightier one coming after him, and baptizes Jesus — on whom the Spirit descends as the voice from the sky declares him the beloved Son.
- **1:12–15 — The Temptation and Preaching of Jesus** (title: BSB) — The Spirit drives Jesus into the wilderness, where Satan tempts him forty days among the wild animals while angels serve him; after John’s arrest, Jesus comes into Galilee announcing that God’s Kingdom is at hand.
- **1:16–20 — The First Disciples** (title: BSB) — By the sea of Galilee, Simon, Andrew, James, and John leave nets, boat, and father immediately at Jesus’ call: “Come after me, and I will make you into fishers for men.”
- **1:21–34 — Jesus Expels an Unclean Spirit and Heals at Peter’s House** (title: adapted from BSB) — One Capernaum Sabbath: authoritative teaching in the synagogue, an unclean spirit silenced and cast out, Simon’s mother-in-law raised from her fever, and the whole city gathered at the door as Jesus heals many at evening.
- **1:35–39 — Jesus Prays and Preaches** (title: BSB) — Long before daylight Jesus withdraws to pray; when Simon finds him, he presses on to the next towns — “because I came out for this reason” — preaching and casting out demons throughout Galilee.
- **1:40–45 — The Leper’s Prayer** (title: BSB) — Moved with compassion, Jesus touches and cleanses a kneeling leper, sends him to the priest with strict orders to tell no one — but the man proclaims it everywhere, and the crowds swell.

## Mark 2
**Summary:** Back in Capernaum, the crowd is so thick that four people carrying a paralyzed man break open the roof to lower him down — and Jesus, “seeing their faith,” says, “Son, your sins are forgiven you.” When the scribes silently call that blasphemy, he heals the man to show “that the Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins.” He then calls Levi from the tax office and eats with tax collectors and sinners, answering the offended scribes and Pharisees, “Those who are healthy have no need for a physician, but those who are sick. I came not to call the righteous, but sinners to repentance.” Questions follow: his disciples don’t fast because the bridegroom is still with them — and new wine needs fresh wineskins — and when they pluck grain on the Sabbath, Jesus appeals to David and declares, “The Sabbath was made for man, not man for the Sabbath. Therefore the Son of Man is lord even of the Sabbath.”
**Tags:** `forgiveness-of-sins` — “Son, your sins are forgiven you,” defended against the charge of blasphemy by the healing that shows “the Son of Man has authority on earth to forgive sins” (2:5–12); `repentance` — “I came not to call the righteous, but sinners to repentance,” spoken at a table full of tax collectors and sinners (2:15–17); `faith` — four people dig through a roof to reach Jesus, and it is “seeing their faith” that he speaks (2:3–5); `sabbath-rest` — “The Sabbath was made for man, not man for the Sabbath. Therefore the Son of Man is lord even of the Sabbath” (2:23–28)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings
**Sections:**
- **2:1–12 — Jesus Heals a Paralytic** (title: BSB) — Lowered through a broken-open roof, a paralyzed man hears his sins forgiven; Jesus answers the scribes’ silent charge of blasphemy by telling the man to rise, and everyone glorifies God.
- **2:13–17 — Jesus Calls Levi** (title: BSB) — Levi leaves the tax office at a word, and Jesus dines with tax collectors and sinners: the sick need a physician, and he came to call sinners.
- **2:18–22 — Questions about Fasting** (title: BSB) — Why don’t his disciples fast? Because the bridegroom is still with them — and the new thing Jesus brings won’t patch onto old cloth or pour into old wineskins.
- **2:23–28 — The Lord of the Sabbath** (title: BSB) — Challenged over grain plucked on the Sabbath, Jesus cites David eating the show bread and declares the Sabbath made for man, with the Son of Man lord even of the Sabbath.

## Mark 3
**Summary:** In the synagogue Jesus asks whether the Sabbath is for doing good or harm, saving life or killing — and when his watchers stay silent, he looks around “with anger, being grieved at the hardening of their hearts” and restores a man’s withered hand; the Pharisees immediately conspire with the Herodians to destroy him. Great multitudes press in from every region, and the unclean spirits fall down crying, “You are the Son of God!” — but he sternly warns them not to make him known. On the mountain he appoints twelve “that they might be with him” and be sent out to preach and cast out demons. Meanwhile his friends say, “He is insane,” and go out to seize him, and scribes from Jerusalem charge that he casts out demons by Beelzebul; Jesus answers that a divided kingdom cannot stand and no one plunders a strong man’s house without first binding him, then warns that all sins can be forgiven, “but whoever may blaspheme against the Holy Spirit never has forgiveness.” When his mother and brothers call for him, he looks at those seated around him: “whoever does the will of God is my brother, my sister, and mother.”
**Tags:** `sabbath-rest` — the Sabbath question put in Jesus’ own words — “Is it lawful on the Sabbath day to do good or to do harm? To save a life or to kill?” — and answered by a healing (3:1–6); `obedience-to-the-word` — the family of Jesus redefined: “whoever does the will of God is my brother, my sister, and mother” (3:31–35); `blasphemy-against-the-spirit` — the warning stated as the text states it, with the narrator’s reason: all sins can be forgiven, “but whoever may blaspheme against the Holy Spirit never has forgiveness” — “because they said, ‘He has an unclean spirit.’” (3:28–30); `deliverance-from-demons` — unclean spirits falling down before him crying, “You are the Son of God!”, and the Beelzebul charge answered with the divided kingdom and the bound strong man (3:11–12, 22–27)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings
**Sections:**
- **3:1–6 — Jesus Heals on the Sabbath** (title: BSB) — Watched by accusers, Jesus grieves at hard hearts, restores a withered hand on the Sabbath, and the Pharisees and Herodians begin plotting his destruction.
- **3:7–12 — Jesus Heals the Multitudes** (title: BSB) — Crowds from Galilee to Idumaea press in to touch him; unclean spirits cry out that he is the Son of God, and he sternly silences them.
- **3:13–19 — The Twelve Apostles** (title: BSB) — On the mountain Jesus appoints twelve — named from Simon Peter to Judas Iscariot, “who also betrayed him” — to be with him, to preach, and to have authority over demons.

- **3:31–35 — Jesus’ Mother and Brothers** (title: BSB) — With his mother and brothers standing outside, Jesus looks at those around him and names as his true family whoever does the will of God.

## Mark 4
**Summary:** Teaching a huge crowd from a boat, Jesus tells the parable of the farmer whose seed falls by the road, on rocky ground, among thorns, and into good ground — and privately tells the twelve and those around them that “the mystery of God’s Kingdom” is given to them, explaining that the seed is the word: Satan snatches it from some hearers, others fall away under persecution, in others “the cares of this age, and the deceitfulness of riches” choke it, but good-soil hearers accept it and bear fruit thirty, sixty, and a hundred times over. More pictures follow: a lamp belongs on a stand, the measure you use will be measured to you, God’s Kingdom grows like seed sprouting secretly night and day, and like a mustard seed — smallest of seeds, becoming greater than all the herbs. That evening, crossing the sea, a violent windstorm swamps the boat while Jesus sleeps; wakened with “Teacher, don’t you care that we are dying?” he rebukes the wind — “Peace! Be still!” — and asks, “Why are you so afraid? How is it that you have no faith?” Filled with awe, they ask, “Who then is this, that even the wind and the sea obey him?”
**Tags:** `obedience-to-the-word` — the parable’s whole point is how the word is heard: good soil is “those who hear the word, accept it, and bear fruit” (4:3–20); `faith` — the storm exposes its absence — “Why are you so afraid? How is it that you have no faith?” (4:35–41); `fear-not` — panicked disciples in a sinking boat meet the great calm and Jesus’ question about their fear (4:37–40); `kingdom-of-heaven` — “the mystery of God’s Kingdom” given to those around him, and the Kingdom pictured as seed sprouting secretly night and day and as the mustard seed rising from smallest to greatest (4:11, 26–32)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings
**Sections:**
- **4:1–20 — The Parable of the Sower** (title: BSB) — From a boat Jesus tells of seed on four soils; privately he gives the twelve and those around them the mystery of God’s Kingdom and explains that the farmer sows the word, received or lost according to the hearer’s heart.
- **4:21–34 — The Lamp, the Growing Seed, and the Mustard Seed** (title: adapted from BSB) — A lamp is made for a stand and hidden things for light; God’s Kingdom grows of itself like seed a farmer can’t explain, and like a mustard seed it rises from smallest to greatest — parables suited to what his hearers can bear.
- **4:35–41 — Jesus Calms the Storm** (title: BSB) — Asleep in the stern of a swamped boat, Jesus is wakened, stills wind and sea with a word, questions his disciples’ fear and faith, and leaves them asking who this is whom wind and sea obey.

## Mark 5
**Summary:** Across the sea in the country of the Gadarenes, a man driven among the tombs by an unclean spirit — untamable, crying out night and day and cutting himself with stones — runs to Jesus, who commands the spirit out; its name is “Legion, for we are many,” and at their begging Jesus sends the demons into a herd of about two thousand pigs, which rushes down the bank and drowns. The frightened townspeople find the man “sitting, clothed, and in his right mind” and beg Jesus to leave; the healed man begs to come along, but Jesus sends him home instead: “tell them what great things the Lord has done for you and how he had mercy on you” — and he proclaims it through Decapolis. Back across the sea, Jairus, a synagogue ruler, pleads for his dying daughter; on the way a woman who has bled for twelve years touches Jesus’ clothes and is instantly healed, and he tells her, “Daughter, your faith has made you well. Go in peace, and be cured of your disease.” When word comes that the girl has died, Jesus tells Jairus, “Don’t be afraid, only believe,” puts out the wailing mourners, takes the child’s hand — “Talitha cumi!… Girl, I tell you, get up!” — and the twelve-year-old rises and walks, to everyone’s amazement.
**Tags:** `pastoral-freedom-from-bondage` — the man no chains could bind, cutting himself among the tombs, is delivered and found “sitting, clothed, and in his right mind” (5:1–15); `faith` — “Daughter, your faith has made you well,” and to Jairus at the worst news, “Don’t be afraid, only believe” (5:34, 36); `pastoral-prayer-for-healing` — Jairus falls at Jesus’ feet begging him to lay hands on his dying daughter, and the suffering woman reaches through the crowd for his clothes (5:22–23, 27–28); `fear-not` — “Don’t be afraid, only believe” meets a father hearing that his daughter is dead (5:35–36); `sharing-your-faith` — the healed man is sent home to tell “what great things the Lord has done for you,” and he proclaims it in Decapolis (5:19–20); `deliverance-from-demons` — the deliverance narrative itself: the spirit named “Legion, for we are many” commanded out and sent into the pigs, and the man found “sitting, clothed, and in his right mind” (5:1–20)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings
**Sections:**
- **5:1–20 — The Demons and the Pigs** (title: BSB) — The man with the legion of unclean spirits is freed; the demons enter a herd of pigs that drowns in the sea, the unnerved region begs Jesus to go, and the restored man becomes Decapolis’s first witness.
- **5:21–43 — The Healing Touch of Jesus** (title: BSB) — Two healings interlock: a bleeding woman’s secret touch of faith is met with public blessing, and Jairus’s daughter — dead before Jesus arrives — is raised by the hand with “Talitha cumi!”

## Mark 6
**Summary:** In his own country Jesus astonishes the synagogue, but the hometown that knows “the carpenter, the son of Mary” takes offense; he answers, “A prophet is not without honor, except in his own country,” and he “could do no mighty work there” beyond healing a few sick people, marveling at their unbelief. He sends the twelve out two by two with authority over unclean spirits, traveling light; they preach repentance, cast out demons, and anoint the sick with oil. Their success reaches King Herod, who is haunted by the thought that John — whom he beheaded — has risen; Mark backtracks to tell how Herodias, through her daughter’s dance and Herod’s rash oath, obtained John’s head on a platter. When the apostles return, Jesus draws them away to rest, but the crowds outrun the boat; he has compassion on them, “because they were like sheep without a shepherd,” teaches them, and feeds five thousand men from five loaves and two fish, with twelve baskets left over. That night he comes to the straining rowers walking on the sea — “Cheer up! It is I! Don’t be afraid.” — and the wind ceases, though “they hadn’t understood about the loaves, but their hearts were hardened”; at Gennesaret, all who touch the fringe of his garment are made well.

## Mark 7
**Summary:** Pharisees and scribes from Jerusalem fault Jesus’ disciples for eating with unwashed hands, and he turns Isaiah on them — “This people honors me with their lips, but their heart is far from me” — charging that they “set aside the commandment of God, and hold tightly to the tradition of men,” even letting a gift declared “Corban” — glossed in the WEB text itself as “given to God” — cancel a person’s duty to support father and mother. To the crowd he declares that nothing going into a person defiles them; it is what comes out — “evil thoughts, adulteries, sexual sins, murders, thefts” and the rest — that proceeds from the heart and defiles. In the borders of Tyre and Sidon a Greek woman, a Syrophoenician, begs him to free her demonized daughter; to his hard saying about the children’s bread she answers, “Yes, Lord. Yet even the dogs under the table eat the children’s crumbs,” and for that saying her daughter is freed. Returning through Decapolis, Jesus takes a deaf man with a speech impediment aside, sighs toward heaven — “Ephphatha!… Be opened!” — and the astonished crowd says, “He has done all things well. He makes even the deaf hear and the mute speak!”

## Mark 8
**Summary:** With a great crowd three days without food, Jesus says, “I have compassion on the multitude,” and feeds about four thousand from seven loaves and a few small fish, with seven baskets left over. The Pharisees demand a sign from heaven; he sighs deeply and refuses — then, in the boat, warns against “the yeast of the Pharisees and the yeast of Herod,” while the disciples, fretting over bread, draw his exasperated questions: after twelve baskets and seven, “Don’t you understand yet?” At Bethsaida a blind man is healed in two stages — first “I see men, but I see them like walking trees,” then everything clearly. Then comes the book’s hinge: at Caesarea Philippi Peter answers “But who do you say that I am?” with “You are the Christ,” and Jesus at once begins teaching that the Son of Man “must suffer many things… and be killed, and after three days rise again”; when Peter rebukes him, he answers, “Get behind me, Satan! For you have in mind not the things of God, but the things of men.” To the crowd he sets the terms of following: deny yourself, take up your cross — “For whoever wants to save his life will lose it; and whoever will lose his life for my sake and the sake of the Good News will save it.”
**Tags:** `gods-provision` — seven loaves feed four thousand, and Jesus points back to both feedings — twelve baskets, then seven — as the answer to the disciples’ bread anxiety (8:1–9, 19–21); `suffering-for-christ` — the call to the crowd: take up your cross and lose your life “for my sake and the sake of the Good News,” unashamed of Jesus and his words (8:34–38); `surrender-to-god` — “let him deny himself” — the profit-and-exchange questions weigh the whole world against a life handed over to him (8:34–37); `discipleship` — the terms of following set for the multitude: self-denial, a cross, and “For whoever wants to save his life will lose it; and whoever will lose his life for my sake and the sake of the Good News will save it” (8:34–38)
