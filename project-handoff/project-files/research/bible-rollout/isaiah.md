# Isaiah — book summary

## Short

Isaiah is the vision of "Isaiah the son of Amoz" concerning Judah and Jerusalem, spanning the reigns of four kings while two empires rise and fall. It opens in a courtroom — the children God raised have rebelled — and from there moves between searing judgment and radiant hope: a purged and faithful Zion, a child born to reign on David's throne, oracles that bring down every proud nation, a feast on the mountain where God swallows up death forever. At the book's hinge, Hezekiah's Jerusalem survives Assyria only to learn that everything will one day be carried to Babylon — and to that future captivity the book speaks its defining word: "Comfort, comfort my people" (40:1). God alone is God and the idols are nothing; his unnamed servant bears the people's iniquity; and the book ends in new heavens and a new earth, where all flesh comes to worship. Its favorite name for God carries its whole message: the Holy One of Israel — the God whose holiness judges sin and whose arm saves those who wait for him.

## Full

Isaiah is one of the longest books in the Bible, and the prophet the New Testament quotes most often. Its sixty-six chapters hold together as one sustained vision (1:1) with one geographic center — Jerusalem, called Zion — seen first under the shadow of Assyria, then addressed across the coming Babylonian exile, and finally transfigured into the city of a renewed creation.

The first movement (chapters 1–12) sets the terms. Judah's worship is elaborate and its heart corrupt, so the LORD refuses the sacrifices of blood-stained hands while holding open an astonishing invitation: "Though your sins are as scarlet, they shall be as white as snow" (1:18). In the year King Uzziah dies, Isaiah sees the Lord enthroned, hears the seraphim cry "Holy, holy, holy," and answers the commission — "Here I am. Send me!" (6:8). When Assyria's shadow falls across the land, the issue becomes trust: King Ahaz will not believe, and out of that failure come the book's great promises of a child called Immanuel, a son given to reign on David's throne forever, and a shoot from the stock of Jesse on whom the LORD's Spirit rests. The movement ends in a song of salvation (12:1–6). Chapters 13–27 then widen the lens: a series of "burdens" summons Babylon, Moab, Damascus, Egypt, Tyre, and the rest of the nations before God's judgment seat, and swells into a vision of the whole earth judged — and of a feast on Mount Zion where the LORD wipes away tears and swallows up death forever (25:6–9).

Chapters 28–35 bring the argument home in a cycle of woes: Judah's leaders scheme for Egyptian cavalry against Assyria, and the prophet answers with the book's central alternative — "You will be saved in returning and rest. Your strength will be in quietness and in confidence" (30:15) — while promising a king who will reign in righteousness and a desert that blossoms. The hinge of the book is history (chapters 36–39): Sennacherib's army stands at Jerusalem's wall, Hezekiah spreads the blasphemous letter before the LORD, and the city is delivered in a night — yet the movement closes with Hezekiah showing his treasures to envoys from Babylon and hearing that everything, sons included, will be carried there. Assyria's threat is answered; Babylon's is announced.

To that unborn generation of exiles, chapters 40–55 speak comfort. Jerusalem's warfare is accomplished; the God before whom nations are a drop in a bucket carries his people from the womb to gray hairs; the idols are summoned to court and found to be nothing — "Besides me, there is no savior" (43:11). God names Cyrus, a foreign king, as the shepherd of his purpose, and promises a new exodus home. Woven through these chapters are the four poems of the LORD's servant, cresting in the fourth: "he was pierced for our transgressions… the LORD has laid on him the iniquity of us all" (53:5–6). Christians have historically read the book's child, king, and suffering servant as portraits of Jesus — a reading the New Testament takes up — and the chapter entries below describe each passage on its own terms and mark that reading where it is noted. The movement ends with an open door: "Come, everyone who thirsts, to the waters!" (55:1).

The final movement (chapters 56–66) turns to life after return: a house of prayer for all peoples, a fast that breaks yokes and feeds the hungry, a Redeemer who comes to Zion, a city renamed because "the LORD delights in you" (62:4). Judgment and hope intensify together — the wine press of God's wrath, and long prayers of confession answered by the promise "behold, I create new heavens and a new earth" (65:17). The book closes holding both ends in view: all flesh coming to worship before the LORD, and the fate of the transgressors — the unquenched fire that makes the final choice unmistakable. From first chapter to last, Isaiah insists that the Holy One of Israel is both the judge no nation escapes and the only Savior — and that the humble and contrite who tremble at his word will inherit the city he makes new.

# The sixty-six chapters

## Isaiah 1
**Summary:** The book opens with Isaiah's vision concerning Judah and Jerusalem across four kings' reigns, and with the LORD's charge against his own children: "I have nourished and brought up children and they have rebelled against me" (1:2). The nation is a body sick from the sole of the foot to the head, its land already desolate; only "a very small remnant" left by the LORD of Armies — the WEB's name for the one familiar Bible English calls "the LORD of hosts" — keeps it from Sodom's fate (1:9). God refuses the people's sacrifices, feasts, and even prayers while their hands are "full of blood" (1:15), demanding instead that they wash, cease evil, and take up the cause of the fatherless and the widow. Yet the invitation stands open: "Though your sins are as scarlet, they shall be as white as snow" (1:18). The chapter closes lamenting the faithful city turned prostitute — silver become dross, princes companions of thieves — and promising a purging judgment that restores: "Zion shall be redeemed with justice" (1:27), while those who forsake the LORD burn like tinder.
**Tags:** `sin` — the opening indictment of "Ah sinful nation, a people loaded with iniquity" who have forsaken the LORD and despised the Holy One of Israel (1:4, 21–23); `repentance` — "Wash yourselves. Make yourself clean. … Cease to do evil. Learn to do well" (1:16–17), with "Come now, and let's reason together" (1:18); `forgiveness-of-sins` — "Though your sins are as scarlet, they shall be as white as snow. Though they are red like crimson, they shall be as wool" (1:18); `divine-judgment` — "I will turn my hand on you, thoroughly purge away your dross" (1:25), and the destruction of transgressors together (1:28–31); `empty-worship` — God refuses the people's sacrifices, feasts, and even prayers while their hands are "full of blood" (1:11–17) — religion kept up over an unwashed life; `justice-and-oppression` — the demanded turn is justice for the fatherless and the widow (1:17), against princes turned "companions of thieves" (1:21–23), until "Zion shall be redeemed with justice" (1:27); `remnant` — "a very small remnant" left by the LORD of Armies is all that keeps the nation from Sodom's fate (1:9)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings
**Sections:**
- **1:1–9 — Judah's Rebellion** (title: BSB) — The LORD calls heaven and earth to hear his case: the children he raised have rebelled, the ox knows its owner but Israel does not know, and the beaten, desolate nation survives only as a very small remnant.
- **1:10–20 — Meaningless Offerings** (title: BSB) — God rejects sacrifices, feasts, and prayers offered by blood-stained hands, calling instead for cleansing, justice for the oppressed, and reasoning together — scarlet sins made white as snow, with life or the sword set before the willing and the rebellious.
- **1:21–31 — The Corruption of Zion** (title: BSB) — The faithful city has become a prostitute, her silver dross and her princes companions of thieves; the Mighty One of Israel will purge the dross, restore judges as at the first, and redeem Zion with justice while transgressors are consumed.

## Isaiah 2
**Summary:** Isaiah sees what will happen "in the latter days": the mountain of the LORD's house raised above all hills, with all nations streaming to it to be taught God's ways, for "the law shall go out of Zion" (2:2–3). God himself will judge between the nations, and they "shall beat their swords into plowshares" and learn war no more (2:4). Then the oracle turns on the present: Judah's land is full of silver, horses, and idols — "They worship the work of their own hands" (2:8) — and a day of the LORD is coming against everything proud and lifted up, cedar and mountain, tower and ship. Men will flee into caves "from before the terror of the LORD, and from the glory of his majesty" (2:19), flinging their silver and gold idols to the moles and bats, and the refrain lands twice: "the LORD alone will be exalted in that day" (2:11; echoed in 2:17). The chapter ends with the lesson drawn: "Stop trusting in man, whose breath is in his nostrils; for of what account is he?" (2:22).
**Tags:** `nations-and-peoples` — "all nations shall flow" to the mountain of the LORD's house, and "Many peoples shall go" up to be taught his ways (2:2–4); `humble-exaltation` — "the arrogance of men will be bowed down, and the LORD alone will be exalted in that day" (2:11, 17), the whole oracle leveling human loftiness; `divine-judgment` — "there will be a day of the LORD of Armies for all that is proud and arrogant" (2:12), driving men into the caves of the rocks (2:19–21); `day-of-the-lord` — the named day itself, the register this oracle gives the prophets: "there will be a day of the LORD of Armies," and the refrain of what that day does — "the LORD alone will be exalted in that day" (2:11–21); `idolatry` — a land full of idols: "They worship the work of their own hands" (2:8), until the idols of silver and gold are flung to the moles and bats (2:18–20); `peace-among-nations` — the latter-days vision: God judges between the nations and they "shall beat their swords into plowshares" and learn war no more (2:2–4)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings
**Sections:**
- **2:1–4 — The Mountain of the House of the LORD** (title: BSB) — In the latter days the LORD's mountain is raised above all others; the nations stream to it for instruction, God judges between them, and weapons of war are re-forged into farm tools.
- **2:5–22 — The Day of Reckoning** (title: BSB) — Against a land full of treasure, horses, and idols comes the day of the LORD: every proud and lofty thing brought low, idols abandoned to moles and bats, men hiding in the rocks — and the call to stop trusting in mere breathing man.

## Isaiah 3
**Summary:** The Lord strips Jerusalem and Judah of every support — bread and water, soldier and judge, prophet and elder — until boys become their princes and the people oppress one another. The cause is not hidden: "their tongue and their doings are against the LORD" (3:8), and "They parade their sin like Sodom" (3:9). The LORD stands up in court against the elders and princes who have eaten up the vineyard: "you crush my people, and grind the face of the poor" (3:15) — while assuring the righteous "that it will be well with them" and the wicked, "Disaster is upon them" (3:10–11). The chapter closes on the arrogant daughters of Zion, itemizing their finery from anklets to shawls, all of it exchanged for rope, baldness, sackcloth, and branding, as their men fall by the sword and the emptied city sits mourning on the ground.
**Tags:** `divine-judgment` — "The LORD stands up to contend, and stands to judge the peoples" (3:13), stripping Jerusalem of supply and support (3:1–3); `sin` — "They parade their sin like Sodom. They don't hide it" (3:9), tongue and doings against the LORD (3:8); `humble-exaltation` — "Because the daughters of Zion are arrogant," walking with outstretched necks, the Lord brings them low (3:16–17, 24–26); `justice-and-oppression` — the LORD's courtroom charge against the elders and princes who have eaten up the vineyard: "you crush my people, and grind the face of the poor" (3:13–15)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings

## Isaiah 4
**Summary:** The slaughter just foretold leaves seven women taking hold of one man, asking only to bear his name: "Take away our reproach" (4:1). Then the oracle brightens: "In that day, the LORD's branch will be beautiful and glorious" (4:2), and everyone left in Zion "shall be called holy" (4:3), once the Lord has washed away the city's filth "by the spirit of justice and by the spirit of burning" (4:4). Over the purged mountain God will create the old wilderness signs — a cloud and smoke by day, the shining of a flaming fire by night — with a canopy over all the glory. The chapter ends in shelter: a pavilion for shade from the heat, "for a refuge and for a shelter from storm and from rain" (4:6).
**Tags:** `restoration` — after devastation, "the LORD's branch will be beautiful and glorious," and Jerusalem is washed and made new (4:2–4); `holiness` — "he who is left in Zion and he who remains in Jerusalem shall be called holy" (4:3); `gods-protection` — a canopy over Zion and "a pavilion for a shade in the daytime from the heat, and for a refuge and for a shelter from storm and from rain" (4:5–6); `remnant` — the tiny chapter is about the survivors themselves: "he who is left in Zion and he who remains in Jerusalem" after the purge (4:2–4) — the book's remnant theme in its survivor form, distinct from the calling the `holiness` tag carries
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings

## Isaiah 5
**Summary:** Isaiah sings a song for his "well beloved" about his vineyard: dug, cleared of stones, planted with the choicest vine, given a tower and a wine press — yet it yielded wild grapes, so its owner will tear out its hedge, let it be trampled, and command the clouds to withhold rain. The parable is then decoded: "the vineyard of the LORD of Armies is the house of Israel" (5:7); God looked for justice and found oppression. Six woes follow — on those who join house to house until they live alone in the land, on drunkards whose feasts have music and wine but who "don't respect the work of the LORD" (5:12), on those who drag iniquity along with cart ropes and dare God to hurry, on those "who call evil good, and good evil" (5:20), on the "wise in their own eyes" (5:21), and on champions at drinking "who acquit the guilty for a bribe" (5:22–23). Therefore the LORD's anger burns against his people, and he whistles for a distant nation whose soldiers come tireless, arrows sharp, roaring like lions — until the land holds only darkness and distress.
**Tags:** `sin` — the woes catalog a people's sins, from those "who draw iniquity with cords of falsehood" to defiant demands that God hurry his work (5:18–19, 8–23); `divine-judgment` — "I will lay it a wasteland" (5:6), and "the LORD's anger burns against his people" as he summons a devouring nation (5:25–30); `self-deception` — "Woe to those who call evil good, and good evil," and to those "wise in their own eyes, and prudent in their own sight" (5:20–21); `humble-exaltation` — "man is brought low, mankind is humbled, and the eyes of the arrogant ones are humbled; but the LORD of Armies is exalted in justice" (5:15–16); `justice-and-oppression` — the parable's verdict: "the vineyard of the LORD of Armies is the house of Israel," where God looked for justice and found oppression (5:7), against champions at drinking "who acquit the guilty for a bribe" (5:22–23)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings
**Sections:**
- **5:1–7 — The Song of the Vineyard** (title: BSB) — A love song turns into an indictment: the vineyard given every care yields wild grapes and will be laid waste — and the vineyard is the house of Israel, where God looked for justice and heard a cry of distress.
- **5:8–30 — Woes to the Wicked** (title: BSB) — Six woes name the sins of land-grabbers, drunkards, defiant sinners, moral inverters, the self-wise, and bribe-taking judges; in answer the LORD's anger burns and he whistles for a far-off nation that comes roaring like a lion.

## Isaiah 6
**Summary:** In the year King Uzziah died, Isaiah sees the Lord on a throne, "high and lifted up," his train filling the temple, while six-winged seraphim call to one another, "Holy, holy, holy, is the LORD of Armies! The whole earth is full of his glory!" (6:1–3). The thresholds shake, the house fills with smoke, and Isaiah cries out that he is undone — "a man of unclean lips" among a people of unclean lips who has seen the King (6:5). A seraph touches his mouth with a live coal from the altar: "your iniquity is taken away, and your sin forgiven" (6:7); and when the Lord asks, "Whom shall I send, and who will go for us?" Isaiah answers, "Here I am. Send me!" (6:8). The commission itself is sobering: speak to a people who hear without understanding, until cities lie waste and the land is emptied — yet as an oak's stump survives its felling, "the holy seed is its stump" (6:13). The scene stands behind everything that follows: the prophet of this book has seen the King.

## Isaiah 7
**Summary:** When Rezin of Syria and Pekah of Israel march on Jerusalem to tear Judah apart and install a puppet king, the heart of Ahaz and his people trembles "as the trees of the forest tremble with the wind" (7:2). Isaiah, taking his son Shearjashub, meets the king with God's word: keep calm and don't fear "these two tails of smoking torches" (7:4); the plot "shall not stand, neither shall it happen" (7:7) — but "If you will not believe, surely you shall not be established" (7:9). Told to ask for any sign, Ahaz refuses with a pious excuse, so the Lord gives one himself: "the virgin will conceive, and bear a son, and shall call his name Immanuel" (7:14) — a name meaning "God with us" — and before the child knows to refuse evil and choose good, the land of the two dreaded kings will be forsaken. Christians have historically read this sign as reaching beyond Ahaz's crisis to the birth of Jesus; within the chapter it stands as God's pledge against the coalition. Judgment still comes for unbelief: the LORD will whistle for Egypt's flies and Assyria's bees, shave the land with a hired razor — the king of Assyria — and leave thousand-vine vineyards to briers and thorns.
**Tags:** `trust-in-god` — the crisis turns on believing God's word over visible armies: "If you will not believe, surely you shall not be established" (7:9, with 7:4–7); `fear-not` — "Don't be afraid, neither let your heart be faint" before the two smoldering kings (7:4); `doubt` — Ahaz will not take God at his word — "If you will not believe, surely you shall not be established" (7:9) — and deflects even the offered sign: "I won't ask. I won't tempt the LORD" (7:12–13); `divine-judgment` — for the house of David's unbelief, "The LORD will bring on you, on your people, and on your father's house days that have not come" — the king of Assyria, and a land of briers and thorns (7:17–25); `messianic-prophecy` — the sign the Lord gives himself: "the virgin will conceive, and bear a son, and shall call his name Immanuel" (7:14) — within the chapter God's pledge against the coalition, and the text this entry already signposts as the one Christians have historically read as reaching to the birth of Jesus
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings
**Sections:**
- **7:1–9 — A Message to Ahaz** (title: BSB) — With Jerusalem threatened by the Syria–Ephraim coalition, Isaiah tells Ahaz to keep calm: the scheme will not stand, both enemy kingdoms are nearly spent, and everything hangs on believing God.
- **7:10–16 — The Sign of Immanuel** (title: BSB) — Ahaz declines to ask for a sign, so the Lord gives his own: the virgin's son Immanuel — and before the child can choose good over evil, the land of the two feared kings will be forsaken.

## Isaiah 8
**Summary:** God has Isaiah write "Maher Shalal Hash Baz" on a large tablet before faithful witnesses, then gives the name to the prophet's newborn son: before the child can say "My father" or "My mother," Assyria will carry off the riches of Damascus and the plunder of Samaria (8:1–4). Because the people refused "the waters of Shiloah that go softly" and rejoiced in Rezin, the River itself — the king of Assyria — will flood over its banks and sweep into Judah, "even to the neck" (8:6–8); yet the nations' war plans "will not stand, for God is with us" (8:10). The LORD then warns Isaiah personally not to walk in this people's way: don't call conspiracy what they call conspiracy or fear what they fear — "The LORD of Armies is who you must respect as holy" (8:13), a sanctuary to those who do, but a stumbling stone and snare to both houses of Israel. Isaiah seals the teaching among his disciples and resolves, "I will wait for the LORD" (8:17), he and his children standing as signs in Israel. The chapter closes against the whispering alternatives: instead of consulting mediums who "chirp" and "mutter" on behalf of the living, "Turn to the law and to the covenant!" ## Isaiah 62
**Summary:** For Zion's sake the speaker will not hold his peace until her righteousness shines out like the dawn and all nations see her glory. She receives a new name: no longer called Forsaken and Desolate but Hephzibah and Beulah, "for the LORD delights in you, and your land will be married" — "as a bridegroom rejoices over his bride, so your God will rejoice over you" (62:4–5). Watchmen posted on Jerusalem's walls are never to be silent: those who call on the LORD are to "take no rest" and "give him no rest" until he makes Jerusalem "a praise on the earth" (62:6–7). The LORD swears that enemies will no longer eat her grain — those who harvest will eat it and praise him — and the call goes out to prepare the way and lift up a banner: "Behold, your salvation comes!" and Zion's people will be called "The Holy People, The LORD's Redeemed" (62:8–12).
**Tags:** `restoration` — Forsaken and Desolate are renamed: the city will be called "Sought Out, A City Not Forsaken" (62:4, 12); `gods-love` — "the LORD delights in you," and "as a bridegroom rejoices over his bride, so your God will rejoice over you" (62:4–5); `prayer` — watchmen who "call on the LORD" are to "take no rest" and "give him no rest" until Jerusalem is established (62:6–7); `salvation` — proclaimed to the end of the earth: "Behold, your salvation comes!" ## Isaiah 63
**Summary:** A question opens the chapter: who is this marching from Edom in dyed garments from Bozrah? The answer — "It is I who speak in righteousness, mighty to save" (63:1) — comes from one whose clothing is stained from treading the wine press alone, "for the day of vengeance was in my heart, and the year of my redeemed has come" (63:4). The prophet then recounts "the loving kindnesses of the LORD": he became Israel's Savior, "in all their affliction he was afflicted," and he bore and carried them all the days of old — yet they rebelled and grieved his Holy Spirit, and he became their enemy (63:7–10). Remembering the days of Moses and the divided sea, the chapter turns to lament: "Look down from heaven" — "You, LORD, are our Father. Our Redeemer from everlasting is your name" (63:15–16) — while adversaries tread down the sanctuary and the people feel like those never called by his name (63:17–19).

## Isaiah 64
**Summary:** The lament crests in a plea: "Oh that you would tear the heavens, that you would come down, that the mountains might quake at your presence" (64:1) — as when God once did awesome things no one looked for. From of old no ear has heard and no eye has seen "a God besides you, who works for him who waits for him" (64:4). Confession follows: "you were angry, and we sinned"; "we have all become like one who is unclean, and all our righteousness is like a polluted garment," fading like a leaf, carried away by the wind of iniquities (64:5–7). Yet the prayer holds on: "you are our Father. We are the clay and you our potter" — do not be furious, do not remember iniquity forever, for Zion has become a wilderness and the holy and beautiful house where the fathers praised God is burned with fire (64:8–12).
**Tags:** `presence-of-god` — the plea for God to come down so that "the mountains might quake at your presence," as he did in awesome deeds of old (64:1–3); `sin` — "we have all become like one who is unclean, and all our righteousness is like a polluted garment"; iniquities carry the people away like the wind (64:5–7); `repentance` — the confession "you were angry, and we sinned" and the plea "Don't remember iniquity forever" (64:5, 9); `surrender-to-god` — "We are the clay and you our potter. We all are the work of your hand" (64:8); `lament` — the communal lament crests here: "Oh that you would tear the heavens, that you would come down" (64:1), grief and confession held together before a burned sanctuary and an unanswered sky (64:1–12); `grace-not-earned` — the confession reaches the people's best works, not their worst: even "all our righteousness is like a polluted garment" (64:6) — spoken as Israel's corporate penitential confession under judgment, the reason mercy must come from God's side and not from works
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings

## Isaiah 65
**Summary:** God answers the long prayer: he was "found by those who didn't seek me," while all day he spread out his hands "to a rebellious people" who sacrifice in gardens, sit among the graves, eat pig's meat, and say "I am holier than you" (65:1–5). He will repay such provocation — yet as new wine is found in the cluster, he will act "for my servants' sake, that I may not destroy them all" (65:8): his servants will eat, drink, and sing for joy of heart, while those who forsook him to set a table for Fortune and Destiny answer for refusing his call (65:11–15). Then the horizon opens: "behold, I create new heavens and a new earth; and the former things will not be remembered" (65:17). Jerusalem becomes a delight and her people a joy; weeping is heard in her no more; people build houses and inhabit them, plant vineyards and eat their fruit, and "before they call, I will answer" (65:18–24). "The wolf and the lamb will feed together," and nothing will hurt or destroy in all God's holy mountain (65:25).
**Tags:** `new-heaven-and-earth` — "behold, I create new heavens and a new earth; and the former things will not be remembered, nor come into mind" (65:17–25); `divine-judgment` — "I will destine you to the sword, and you will all bow down to the slaughter; because when I called, you didn't answer" (65:6–7, 11–15); `joy-in-the-lord` — "be glad and rejoice forever in that which I create," as God himself rejoices in Jerusalem and delights in his people (65:18–19); `prayer` — "before they call, I will answer; and while they are yet speaking, I will hear" — set against a people who would not answer when God called (65:12, 24); `idolatry` — the provocation itemized: sacrificing in gardens, sitting among the graves, eating pig's meat, saying "I am holier than you" (65:3–5), and a table set for Fortune and Destiny by those who forsook the LORD (65:11)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings
**Sections:**
- **65:1–16 — Judgments and Promises** (title: BSB) — God has held out his hands to a rebellious, idolatrous people and will repay them, yet for his servants' sake he preserves offspring from Jacob: his servants will rejoice while those who forsook him are put to shame.
- **65:17–25 — A New Heaven and a New Earth** (title: BSB) — God creates new heavens and a new earth: Jerusalem a delight, weeping gone, long life, secure homes and vineyards, prayer answered before it is spoken, and no harm in all his holy mountain.

## Isaiah 66
**Summary:** The book ends where true worship begins: "Heaven is my throne, and the earth is my footstool" — no house can contain the Maker of all things, who looks instead "to he who is poor and of a contrite spirit, and who trembles at my word" (66:1–2). Sacrifice without such a heart is as offensive as killing a man or offering pig's blood, and God will bring on the mockers the very fears they chose (66:3–6). Zion travails and gives birth to a nation in a day, and all who mourned over her are called to rejoice with her: "As one whom his mother comforts, so I will comfort you" (66:7–13). Yet the LORD also comes with fire and his sword to "execute judgment by fire and by his sword on all flesh" (66:15–16), sending survivors to declare his glory among nations that have never heard his fame, until "all flesh will come to worship before me" from one new moon and one Sabbath to another (66:18–23). Isaiah closes holding both ends in view: new heavens and a new earth that remain before God, and the dead bodies of the transgressors, whose "worm will not die" (66:22–24).
**Tags:** `humble-exaltation` — past every temple, God looks "to he who is poor and of a contrite spirit, and who trembles at my word" (66:2, 5); `god-of-all-comfort` — "As one whom his mother comforts, so I will comfort you. You will be comforted in Jerusalem" (66:13); `divine-judgment` — "the LORD will execute judgment by fire and by his sword on all flesh; and those slain by the LORD will be many" (66:15–16, 24); `nations-and-peoples` — "I will gather all nations and languages, and they will come, and will see my glory," declared among peoples who have not heard (66:18–20); `worship` — "from one new moon to another, and from one Sabbath to another, all flesh will come to worship before me" (66:23); `new-heaven-and-earth` — "as the new heavens and the new earth, which I will make, shall remain before me," so the people's offspring and name remain (66:22); `fear-of-the-lord` — the posture God looks to past every temple, in the trembling register: "he who is poor and of a contrite spirit, and who trembles at my word" (66:2, 5); `empty-worship` — sacrifice without the contrite, trembling heart of 66:2 is as offensive as killing a man or offering pig's blood: offerings God rejects, answering the offerers with the very fears they chose (66:3–4)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings
**Sections:**
- **66:1–6 — Heaven Is My Throne** (title: BSB) — No temple can contain the Maker of all; God looks to the contrite who tremble at his word, and turns heartless sacrifice and mockery back on the offerers.
- **66:7–14 — Rejoice with Jerusalem** (title: BSB) — Zion gives birth to a nation in a day; all who love her are called to rejoice and be satisfied as God extends peace to her like a river and comforts his people as a mother comforts her child.
- **66:15–24 — Final Judgments against the Wicked** (title: BSB) — The LORD comes with fire and sword; survivors carry his glory to nations that have not heard, all flesh comes to worship, and the book ends contrasting the enduring new creation with the fate of the transgressors.

Example refs: Isaiah 2:6; 8:19–20; 47:9–13.

Collision resolved at critic Round 3: as of the live-file re-read at 2026-08-23T06:35:48Z the live file carries a `kingdom-of-heaven` (or `kingdom-of-god`) row (Matthew (Gospels+Acts) / 2026-08-23) — the NT kingdom-announcement register ("kingdom of heaven meaning," "seek first the kingdom"). Rationale: "is there only one God" / "no other gods" queries; the trial speeches' refrain "besides me there is no God" (44:6) has no concept home (`deity-of-christ` is a different claim). Overlaps the `idolatry` theme (now a ref-append on its live owner row, Micah — see the append list below) — decide whether one concept or two before minting. Example refs: Isaiah 43:10–11; 44:6–8; 45:5–6, 21–22; 46:9.

9. **`servant-of-the-lord`** (or `suffering-servant`) — the servant poems. Rationale: "servant songs" / "suffering servant" are extremely common study queries; only `the-cross` touches Isaiah 53, and nothing serves the other three poems. Overlaps the `messianic-promise` proposal above. Example refs: Isaiah 42:1–9; 49:1–13; 50:4–11; 52:13–15; 53:1–12.
