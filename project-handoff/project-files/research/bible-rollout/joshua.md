# Joshua — book summary

## Short

Joshua tells how God kept a promise centuries old. After Moses' death, God commissions Joshua — "be strong and courageous" — and leads Israel across a flooded Jordan on dry ground, into the land sworn to Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob. Jericho's wall falls to trumpets and a shout; hidden sin at Ai brings defeat before restored obedience brings victory; southern and northern kings fall in turn "because the LORD, the God of Israel, fought for Israel." The conquered land is then measured out, tribe by tribe, as an inheritance. The book's own verdict stands near the end: "Nothing failed of any good thing which the LORD had spoken to the house of Israel. All came to pass." It closes with an aged Joshua putting a choice to the people that still carries: "choose today whom you will serve... but as for me and my house, we will serve the LORD."

## Full

Joshua opens where Deuteronomy ends: Moses is dead, the promise is not. The LORD commissions Joshua the son of Nun — "Moses' servant," the WEB calls him — with words that set the book's tone — be strong and courageous, keep this book of the law on your lips and in your thoughts day and night, and "as I was with Moses, so I will be with you."

The book moves in three great movements. In chapters 1–5 Israel enters the land: the prostitute Rahab hides Israel's spies and confesses that Israel's God "is God in heaven above, and on earth beneath"; the Jordan, at flood stage, is cut off the moment the priests carrying the ark step in; twelve stones are stacked at Gilgal so that children who ask "What do these stones mean?" will hear the story; and a new generation is circumcised and keeps the Passover before the manna ceases and the produce of Canaan takes its place. On the eve of battle Joshua meets a sword-bearing figure who names himself commander of the LORD's army — and Joshua worships on holy ground.

Chapters 6–12 tell the conquest, and they insist throughout that the victories are the LORD's: Jericho's wall falls to a seven-day procession and a shout; Achan's theft of devoted plunder brings national defeat at Ai until the sin is exposed and judged; the renewed attack succeeds, and the whole law is read aloud at Mount Ebal. A Gibeonite ruse extracts a sworn treaty because Israel "didn't ask counsel from the LORD's mouth" — and honoring that oath draws Israel into the battle where the sun stands still over Gibeon. Southern and northern coalitions fall in turn, and the section closes with a sober roster of thirty-one defeated kings. These chapters do not soften the destruction they narrate; the text presents it as the LORD's own commanded judgment on the peoples of the land, promised since Moses — a hard word the book asks readers to hear on its own terms.

Chapters 13–24 turn from winning the land to receiving it. Lot by lot, "before the LORD," the tribes are given their inheritances — with living portraits breaking through the border lists: eighty-five-year-old Caleb claiming the hill country of the Anakim on the strength of having "wholly followed the LORD"; Zelophehad's daughters receiving the inheritance the LORD had commanded for them; Levi receiving no land because the LORD himself is their inheritance; cities of refuge shielding the accidental killer from the avenger of blood. The narrator also keeps count of what Israel failed to do — Jebusites, Geshurites, and Canaanites who were never driven out — quiet seeds of the book of Judges. Then comes the summary verdict: the LORD gave the land, gave rest all around, and "Nothing failed of any good thing which the LORD had spoken to the house of Israel."

The book ends with words rather than wars. A near-war over an altar by the Jordan is defused when the eastern tribes explain it is a witness, not a rival shrine. The aged Joshua charges Israel's leaders to love the LORD and hold fast to him, warning that the God whose every good word came true will be equally true to his warnings. And at Shechem, Joshua rehearses the whole story in God's own voice — from Terah beyond the River to vineyards Israel never planted — and puts the choice: serve the LORD, or the gods your fathers served — "but as for me and my house, we will serve the LORD." The people choose, a covenant is made, a stone is set up as witness — and the book closes with three graves in the promised land: Joshua's, Joseph's bones brought up from Egypt, and Eleazar's. The promise-keeping God has done all he said; whether Israel will keep its own promise is the question the next books answer.

# The twenty-four chapters

## Joshua 1
**Summary:** After Moses' death the LORD commissions Joshua to lead Israel across the Jordan into the land sworn to their fathers, promising that no one will be able to stand before him: "As I was with Moses, so I will be with you." Three times God tells him to be strong and courageous, tying success not to force of arms but to the book of the law — it is to stay in his mouth, meditated on day and night, and done. Joshua orders the people to prepare food for crossing within three days, and reminds Reuben, Gad, and half of Manasseh of their pledge to fight ahead of their brothers before returning to their own land east of the Jordan. They answer with full allegiance — "All that you have commanded us we will do" — and echo God's own charge back to their new leader: "Only be strong and courageous."
**Tags:** `fear-not` — "Don't be afraid. Don't be dismayed, for the LORD your God is with you wherever you go" (1:9; existing anchor); `presence-of-god` — "As I was with Moses, so I will be with you. I will not fail you nor forsake you" (1:5, 9); `obedience-to-the-word` — "Be careful to observe to do according to all the law... Don't turn from it to the right hand or to the left" (1:7–8); `delight-in-the-word` — "you shall meditate on it day and night" (1:8; existing anchor); `do-not-lose-heart` — the commissioning word against dismay, spoken to a man facing a task too big for him: "Haven't I commanded you? Be strong and courageous" (1:9; existing anchor); `leadership` — the succession charge itself: "As I was with Moses, so I will be with you" (1:5), answered by the people's pledge to their new leader, "All that you have commanded us we will do" (1:16–18)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings

## Joshua 2
**Summary:** Joshua secretly sends two spies from Shittim, and in Jericho they find shelter with Rahab, a prostitute, who hides them under flax stalks on her roof and misdirects the king's men. She tells the spies what Jericho already knows: the LORD has given Israel the land, the city's hearts have melted at the news of the Red Sea and the Amorite kings, "for the LORD your God, he is God in heaven above, and on earth beneath." She asks that the kindness she showed be returned — that her father's house be spared — and the spies bind themselves by oath, telling her to tie a line of scarlet thread — our WEB translation's wording for the familiar "scarlet cord" — in her window and gather her family inside. Lowered through the window in the city wall, the men hide three days in the mountain and return to Joshua with her own words on their lips: "Truly the LORD has delivered all the land into our hands."
**Tags:** `faith` — a Canaanite outsider stakes her life on what she believes about Israel's God: "I know that the LORD has given you the land... he is God in heaven above, and on earth beneath" (2:9–13); `hospitality` — Rahab takes the two strangers into her house, hides them at her own risk, and plans their escape (2:1, 4–6, 15–16); `oaths-and-vows` — the spies bind themselves by oath to spare Rahab's father's house — sought on the strength of her confession, "the LORD your God, he is God in heaven above, and on earth beneath" — with its conditions spelled out at the window (2:11–14, 17–21)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings

## Joshua 3
**Summary:** Israel moves from Shittim to the Jordan's edge, where the people are told to follow the ark of the covenant at a distance — "for you have not passed this way before" — and to sanctify themselves, "for tomorrow the LORD will do wonders among you." The LORD promises to begin magnifying Joshua as he did Moses, and gives the sign: when the feet of the priests carrying the ark rest in the water, the river will be cut off. Though the Jordan overflows all its banks at harvest time, the moment the priests' feet dip in the edge, the water piles up in one heap far upstream at Adam, and the flow toward the Salt Sea is wholly cut off. The priests stand firm on dry ground in the middle of the riverbed while all Israel crosses over — proof, Joshua says, "that the living God is among you" and will drive out the nations ahead of them.
**Tags:** `presence-of-god` — "By this you shall know that the living God is among you," with the ark of the covenant leading the way into the river (3:10–11); `trust-in-god` — the priests must carry the ark into a river in full flood and stand still in it before the waters are cut off (3:8, 13, 15–17); `guidance` — Israel follows the ark "that you may know the way by which you must go; for you have not passed this way before" (3:3–4)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings

## Joshua 4
**Summary:** When the nation has crossed, the LORD has twelve men — one per tribe — carry twelve stones from the riverbed where the priests stood, and Joshua sets up another twelve in the middle of the Jordan itself. The stones from the river are stacked at Gilgal as a permanent memorial with a built-in question — "What do you mean by these stones?" — so that when children ask in the future, the parents can tell how the waters were cut off before the ark, as the LORD once dried up the Red Sea. About forty thousand armed men cross to the plains of Jericho, the LORD magnifies Joshua in Israel's sight, and when the priests' feet touch the far bank the Jordan returns to flood. The purpose is spelled out: "that all the peoples of the earth may know that the LORD's hand is mighty, and that you may fear the LORD your God forever."

## Joshua 5
**Summary:** The kings of Canaan lose heart at the news of the crossing, and in the safety of that fear Israel pauses for circumcision and Passover before any battle. At God's command Joshua circumcises the generation born in the wilderness — the men of war who left Egypt had died along the way for not listening to the LORD's voice, and the sons born after them had never been circumcised — and the LORD declares, "Today I have rolled away the reproach of Egypt from you," so the place is called Gilgal. The people keep the Passover in the plains of Jericho, eat unleavened cakes and parched grain from the land's own produce, and the next day the manna stops for good: Canaan's fruit now feeds them. Then, near Jericho, Joshua meets a man with a drawn sword who answers his challenge, "No; but I have come now as commander of the LORD's army" — and Joshua falls on his face and worships, and is told, "Take off your sandals, for the place on which you stand is holy."

## Joshua 6
**Summary:** Jericho is sealed tight, but the LORD tells Joshua the city is already given into his hand and prescribes a battle plan with no battle in it: march around the city once a day for six days, seven priests with seven rams' horn trumpets before the ark, then seven circuits on the seventh day and a great shout. Israel obeys to the letter, the wall falls down flat, and the city is taken — all of it "devoted" to the LORD (the WEB's word for what is set apart wholly to God, here for destruction), its people put to the sword and its silver, gold, bronze, and iron reserved for the LORD's treasury. Only Rahab and her father's household are brought out alive, as the spies swore; "she lives in the middle of Israel to this day." Joshua lays an oath-curse on whoever rebuilds Jericho. "So the LORD was with Joshua; and his fame was in all the land."
**Tags:** `faith` — for seven days Israel marches in silence around a shut city on nothing but God's word, "I have given Jericho into your hand," until the shout of trust: "Shout, for the LORD has given you the city!" (6:2–5, 16, 20); `obedience-to-the-word` — the strange instructions are carried out exactly as commanded, day by day (6:8–15, 20); `divine-judgment` — the city and all in it are devoted to the LORD and destroyed at his command (6:17, 21, 24)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings

## Joshua 7
**Summary:** Achan of the tribe of Judah secretly takes some of the devoted things, and "the LORD's anger burned against the children of Israel" — the sin surfaces as military disaster when Ai's few men rout Israel's overconfident force of about three thousand and kill about thirty-six. Joshua tears his clothes and lies before the ark until evening, pleading for God's great name, but the LORD's answer is bracing: "Get up! ... Israel has sinned," and "I will not be with you any more, unless you destroy the devoted things from among you." Israel is brought near tribe by tribe, family by family, man by man, until the LORD singles out Achan; at Joshua's plea — "give glory to the LORD... and make confession" — he admits coveting and burying a beautiful Babylonian robe, two hundred shekels of silver, and a fifty-shekel wedge of gold under his tent. Achan, his household, and all he has are stoned and burned in the valley of Achor ("trouble"), a stone heap is raised, and "The LORD turned from the fierceness of his anger."

## Joshua 8
**Summary:** With the sin judged, the LORD renews the campaign — "Don't be afraid, and don't be dismayed" — and this time grants Ai's plunder to Israel, prescribing an ambush. Joshua sets a force in hiding behind the city, draws Ai's men out with a feigned retreat, and at the LORD's word stretches out his javelin; the ambush takes and burns the city, twelve thousand fall, and Ai's king is hanged on a tree until evening and buried under a heap of stones at the city gate. Then Joshua builds an altar of uncut stones on Mount Ebal, as Moses had commanded, offers burnt and peace offerings, and writes on stones a copy of Moses' law. With all Israel — elders, women, little ones, and the foreigners among them — standing on either side of the ark facing Gerizim and Ebal, Joshua reads every word of the law, the blessing and the curse: "There was not a word of all that Moses commanded which Joshua didn't read."

## Joshua 10

## Joshua 13
**Summary:** Joshua is old, and the LORD tells him plainly that "there remains yet very much land to be possessed" — the Philistine coast, the Sidonian north, and more — yet the promise holds: "I will drive them out from before the children of Israel. Just allocate it to Israel for an inheritance." Joshua is to divide the land among the nine and a half western tribes, while the chapter records what Moses had already given east of the Jordan to Reuben, Gad, and half of Manasseh — the kingdoms of Sihon and Og, where Israel also killed Balaam the soothsayer. The record is honest about failure too: Geshur and Maacath were never driven out and "live within Israel to this day." # Decisions record (delegated defaults — each reversible on Jesse's word)

1. **Vocabulary per the project-wide rollout ruling: all 131 ids at commit b3f491d are usable, including the nine PR #43 ids** (`asking-in-gods-will`, `baptism`, `benediction`, `caring-for-aging-parents`, `christ-the-cornerstone`, `doubt`, `incarnation`, `justification-by-faith`, `trinity`), with any use of those nine to be recorded here as a reversible delegated default. All nine were considered for Joshua and none is used: they are New-Testament-register concepts with no honest presence in this book (the nearest call, `benediction` on Joshua's farewell blessings in 22:6–7, was judged not to meet the substance bar — the blessings are narrated events, not a benediction text a searcher of that concept would want). Tag ids are YAML file basenames throughout — relevant because fourteen `pastoral-*` files carry divergent inner ids — though no `pastoral-*` concept ended up tagged in Joshua.
2. **Chapter 10 subdivided (three sections).** All three Berean units are conquest narrative, but they read as genuinely distinct movements — the miracle day at Gibeon, the execution of the five kings at Makkedah, and the city-by-city southern sweep. A defensible alternative is keeping the chapter whole; reversible.
