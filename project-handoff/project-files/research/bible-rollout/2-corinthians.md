# 2 Corinthians — book summary (draft)

## Short

2 Corinthians is Paul's most personal letter — a letter about what real ministry looks like when it doesn't look impressive. Written after a painful visit to Corinth and a severe letter sent through tears, it opens with the God of all comfort, who comforts his people in affliction so that they can comfort others. Against rivals who measured apostles by eloquence and force of presence, Paul argues that the treasure of the Good News comes in clay vessels on purpose, so the power is seen to be God's. Along the way he gives the Bible's fullest teaching on giving — grace-shaped, cheerful, never compelled — and the letter peaks at the words the Lord spoke over his unremoved thorn: "My grace is sufficient for you, for my power is made perfect in weakness."

## Full

2 Corinthians is a letter written into a strained relationship. Paul had planted the assembly at Corinth — "assembly" is our WEB translation's word for the familiar "church" — and had already written to it; since then, things had gone wrong. The letter itself lets us reconstruct the outline: Paul made a visit that ended in sorrow ("I would not come to you again in sorrow," 2:1; "when I was present the second time," 13:2), then wrote a severe letter "out of much affliction and anguish of heart... with many tears" (2:4) instead of coming again. He waited for news in real suspense — no relief at Troas, and in Macedonia "Fightings were outside. Fear was inside." — until Titus arrived with word that the letter had worked: the Corinthians had grieved to repentance. Most readers understand that severe letter to be a letter now lost, written between our 1 and 2 Corinthians; this letter is Paul consolidating the repair while confronting the holdouts.

The first movement (chapters 1–7) is Paul reopening his heart. He answers the charge of fickleness over his changed travel plans — his word, like God's promises in Christ, is not "Yes and no" — and explains the severe letter's loving intent, urging the assembly to forgive and restore the man they had disciplined. Then he defends his unimpressive ministry at depth: he needs no letters of commendation, because the Corinthians themselves are his letter, written by the Spirit on hearts; he is a servant of a new covenant whose glory outshines and outlasts the glory that made Moses' face shine; and the treasure of this ministry is carried in clay vessels — pressed, perplexed, pursued, struck down, but never crushed, despairing, forsaken, or destroyed — so that the surpassing power is plainly God's. Looking through mortality to an eternal building from God, he lives to persuade: God was in Christ reconciling the world to himself, and his ambassadors beg on Christ's behalf, "be reconciled to God."

The second movement (chapters 8–9) turns to the relief collection for the saints, which Corinth had begun a year before and stalled. Paul holds up the Macedonians, whose deep poverty overflowed in rich generosity, and grounds the whole appeal in a single sentence of gospel economics: "though he was rich, yet for your sakes he became poor, that you through his poverty might become rich" (8:9). Giving here is grace from first to last — willing, proportionate to what one has, cheerful and never compelled — and its harvest is defined by God himself: sufficiency for every good work, and thanksgiving rising to God from every side. It is never a technique for getting rich.

The third movement (chapters 10–13) drops the buffer and confronts the rivals — "false apostles, deceitful workers, masquerading as Christ's apostles" — whom the Corinthians were tolerating. Goaded into their boasting contest, Paul boasts backwards: not conquests but beatings, shipwrecks, hunger, dangers, and daily anxiety for all the assemblies, ending in the deliberately unheroic picture of escaping Damascus in a basket. The contest peaks with a man caught up into Paradise and a thorn that three prayers did not remove, answered instead with the letter's summit: "My grace is sufficient for you, for my power is made perfect in weakness" (12:9). Warning that his third visit will not spare unrepented sin, Paul turns the demand for proof around — "Examine your own selves, whether you are in the faith" — and closes with one of Scripture's best-loved benedictions: the grace of the Lord Jesus Christ, God's love, and the fellowship of the Holy Spirit.

# The thirteen chapters

## 2 Corinthians 1
**Summary:** Paul opens by blessing "the Father of mercies and God of all comfort," who comforts his people in every affliction so that they can pass the same comfort on to others. He tells the Corinthians how crushing trouble in Asia left him and his companions despairing even of life — a sentence of death within, meant to teach them not to trust in themselves "but in God who raises the dead," who delivered them and will deliver again. Then he turns to a sore point: he had announced a double visit and not come, and some read the change as fickleness. He calls God as witness that he stayed away to spare them — and insists his message never wavered, for in Jesus all God's promises find their "Yes," and God has anointed believers, sealed them, and given "the down payment of the Spirit" in their hearts.

## 2 Corinthians 2
**Summary:** Paul explains why he stayed away: another sorrowful visit would have grieved the very people he needed joy from, so he wrote instead — "out of much affliction and anguish of heart... with many tears" — to show the depth of his love. The punishment the majority imposed on the man who caused the pain has been enough; now they should forgive and comfort him before he is "swallowed up with his excessive sorrow," and Paul adds his own forgiveness to theirs, "that no advantage may be gained over us by Satan." He recalls arriving at Troas to an open door but no Titus, and having no relief of spirit, going on to Macedonia. Then anxiety turns to doxology: God "always leads us in triumph in Christ," spreading the knowledge of Christ everywhere like a sweet aroma — "from life to life" among those being saved, "from death to death" among those perishing — and Paul speaks in Christ sincerely before God, not "peddling the word of God."

## 2 Corinthians 3
**Summary:** Does Paul need letters of commendation, as some do? The Corinthians themselves are his letter — "written not with ink, but with the Spirit of the living God; not in tablets of stone, but in tablets that are hearts of flesh." Such confidence is not self-sufficiency: God made him sufficient as a servant of a new covenant, "not of the letter but of the Spirit. For the letter kills, but the Spirit gives life." If the covenant engraved on stone came with a glory so bright that Israel could not gaze at Moses' face — a glory that was passing away — the Spirit's service exceeds it in a glory that remains. To this day a veil lies on hearts when Moses is read, "but whenever someone turns to the Lord, the veil is taken away"; and "where the Spirit of the Lord is, there is liberty," so that all believers, with unveiled faces beholding the Lord's glory, "are transformed into the same image from glory to glory."

## 2 Corinthians 4
**Summary:** Because this ministry came by mercy, "we don't faint": Paul has renounced hidden shame and craftiness, commending himself by open statement of the truth. If the Good News is veiled, it is veiled to the dying, whose minds "the god of this world" — a title Scripture here gives Satan — has blinded; but the God who said "Light will shine out of darkness" has shone in believers' hearts with "the light of the knowledge of the glory of God in the face of Jesus Christ." This treasure comes in clay vessels deliberately, "that the exceeding greatness of the power may be of God and not from ourselves": pressed on every side yet not crushed, perplexed yet not to despair, pursued yet not forsaken, struck down yet not destroyed — always carrying Jesus' dying so that Jesus' life shows too. Knowing that "he who raised the Lord Jesus will raise us also," Paul does not faint: the outward person is decaying, the inward person is renewed day by day, and this momentary light affliction is working "an eternal weight of glory," while faith looks at what is unseen and eternal.

## 2 Corinthians 5
**Summary:** Paul knows that if "the earthly house of our tent" — this mortal body — is dissolved, there is "a building from God... eternal, in the heavens"; so believers groan, longing to be clothed with life, with the Spirit given as a down payment. Therefore "we walk by faith, not by sight," courageous even toward death, since to be absent from the body is to be at home with the Lord — and the aim, at home or absent, is to please him, "for we must all be revealed before the judgment seat of Christ." The love of Christ compels Paul, "because we judge thus: that one died for all, therefore all died," so that the living no longer live to themselves. In Christ, anyone is "a new creation. The old things have passed away. Behold, all things have become new" — and God, who reconciled us to himself through Christ, has committed to his people the ministry and word of reconciliation, making them "ambassadors on behalf of Christ" who beg, "be reconciled to God." The chapter ends on the great exchange: "him who knew no sin he made to be sin on our behalf, so that in him we might become the righteousness of God."

## 2 Corinthians 6
**Summary:** As God's fellow worker, Paul entreats the Corinthians not to receive God's grace in vain, for "now is the acceptable time... now is the day of salvation." He commends his ministry not by credentials but by how it is carried: great endurance in afflictions, beatings, imprisonments, riots, labors, watchings — sleepless nights — and fastings; and by pureness, kindness, sincere love, and "the armor of righteousness"; living the paradox "as dying and behold—we live... as sorrowful yet always rejoicing, as poor yet making many rich." His mouth is open and his heart enlarged toward them, and he begs them, as his children, to open their hearts in return. Then a sharp charge: "Don't be unequally yoked with unbelievers" — righteousness has no fellowship with iniquity, Christ no agreement with Belial — for "you are a temple of the living God," in whom God has promised to dwell and walk; so come out, be separate, touch no unclean thing, and the Lord Almighty will be a Father to sons and daughters.

## 2 Corinthians 7
**Summary:** Having such promises, Paul writes, "let's cleanse ourselves from all defilement of flesh and spirit, perfecting holiness in the fear of God" — then asks again for open hearts, since he has wronged and corrupted no one. He recounts the suspense in Macedonia — "fightings were outside. Fear was inside" — until "he who comforts the lowly, God, comforted us by the coming of Titus," who brought news of the Corinthians' longing, mourning, and zeal. The severe letter had grieved them, though just for a while, and Paul had regretted sending it; now he rejoices, not that they grieved but that "you were grieved to repentance," for "godly sorrow produces repentance leading to salvation, which brings no regret. But the sorrow of the world produces death." Their earnest care, indignation, and vindication proved them pure in the matter; Titus's spirit was refreshed by them all, Paul's boasting about them was "found to be truth," and he closes confident in them in everything.

## 2 Corinthians 8
**Summary:** Paul reports the grace given to the assemblies of Macedonia: "in a severe ordeal of affliction, the abundance of their joy and their deep poverty abounded to the riches of their generosity" — they gave beyond their power, of their own accord — indeed, "first they gave their own selves to the Lord." He urges the Corinthians, who abound in faith, utterance, knowledge, and love, to abound in this grace too and to complete the collection they were first to begin a year before. This is advice, not command — a proving of love by the earnestness of others — with the decisive motive stated in 8:9: "though he was rich, yet for your sakes he became poor, that you through his poverty might become rich." Readiness counts "according to what you have, not according to what you don't have," and the aim is equality, present abundance supplying present lack. Titus and two brothers — one appointed by the assemblies, one many times proved earnest — will carry the gift, because Paul takes care to be honorable "not only in the sight of the Lord, but also in the sight of men."
**Tags:** `generosity` — deep poverty abounding "to the riches of their generosity," and the call to "abound in this grace" of giving (8:2, 7); `honesty` — handling the collection above reproach, "avoiding this, that any man should blame us," honorable before the Lord and before men (8:20–21)

## 2 Corinthians 9
**Summary:** Paul knows Achaia's readiness — he boasts to the Macedonians that Achaia has been prepared since last year — but sends the brothers ahead so the promised gift will be ready "as a matter of generosity, and not of greediness," sparing everyone the shame of unpreparedness. He states the principle: "he who sows sparingly will also reap sparingly. He who sows bountifully will also reap bountifully" — so let each give as he has determined in his heart, "not grudgingly or under compulsion, for God loves a cheerful giver." The harvest God promises is defined by God's own purposes: "God is able to make all grace abound to you," giving all sufficiency so that givers "may abound to every good work," supplying and multiplying seed and increasing "the fruits of your righteousness." The service does more than meet the saints' needs — it overflows in many thanksgivings to God, proves the obedience of their confession, and draws out the recipients' prayer and longing for the givers. Paul ends where all giving begins: "thanks be to God for his unspeakable gift!"

## 2 Corinthians 10
**Summary:** Paul turns to those in Corinth who say, "His letters... are weighty and strong, but his bodily presence is weak, and his speech is despised." He entreats them "by the humility and gentleness of Christ," begging that when he comes he will not have to show the boldness he intends against those who think he walks "according to the flesh." For though he walks in the flesh he does not wage war by it: "the weapons of our warfare are not of the flesh, but mighty before God to the throwing down of strongholds," demolishing imaginations and every high thing raised against the knowledge of God, "bringing every thought into captivity to the obedience of Christ." Unlike those who measure themselves by themselves and are "without understanding," Paul boasts only within the boundaries God appointed him — which reached all the way to Corinth with the Good News — hoping to preach in the regions beyond. The standard is fixed: "he who boasts, let him boast in the Lord," for "it isn't he who commends himself who is approved, but whom the Lord commends."

## 2 Corinthians 11
**Summary:** Paul asks the Corinthians to bear with "a little foolishness," because he is jealous over them "with a godly jealousy" — he promised them in marriage "to one husband, that I might present you as a pure virgin to Christ," and fears that "as the serpent deceived Eve in his craftiness," their minds might be corrupted; they put up all too readily with anyone preaching "another Jesus." He is not behind "the very best apostles" — the WEB's phrase for the rivals' self-billing — though he is unskilled in speech; and his refusal to take their money was love, not distance, a boast no one in Achaia will silence. The rivals themselves are "false apostles, deceitful workers, masquerading as Christ's apostles" — no wonder, "for even Satan masquerades as an angel of light." Forced to play their boasting game, Paul matches their pedigree — Hebrew, Israelite, Abraham's offspring — then overturns the contest with a catalog of suffering: more labors and prisons, five times "forty stripes minus one," rods, a stoning, three shipwrecks, perils from rivers, robbers, countrymen, Gentiles, city, wilderness, and sea, hunger and cold, and the daily inward weight of "anxiety for all the assemblies." If he must boast, he will boast of his weakness — down to being lowered from a window in a basket to escape Damascus.
