# Leviticus — book summary (draft)

## Short

Leviticus opens where Exodus ended: the LORD's glory has filled the tabernacle, and now "The LORD called to Moses, and spoke to him from the Tent of Meeting." The whole book answers one question — how can a sinful, mortal people live with a holy God camped in their very midst? Its answer unfolds in layers: offerings that make atonement (1–7), an ordained priesthood to serve at the altar (8–10), the boundary between clean and unclean in food, childbirth, disease, and the body (11–15), and one day each year when the high priest carries blood within the veil and a goat carries all Israel's sins away into the wilderness (16). Then the call widens to the whole camp: "You shall be holy; for I, the LORD your God, am holy" — holiness worked out in the fields and the marketplace, in "love your neighbor as yourself," in a calendar of feasts, in the Jubilee's liberty, and in the covenant's blessings and warnings. Underneath everything runs the book's steady refrain: "I am the LORD."

## Full

Leviticus is the middle book of the five books of Moses, and almost nothing happens in it — nearly the whole book is the LORD speaking. Exodus closed with the tabernacle finished and God's glory filling it; Leviticus begins the next morning of that story: "The LORD called to Moses, and spoke to him from the Tent of Meeting" (1:1), and it ends still camped at the mountain — "These are the commandments which the LORD commanded Moses for the children of Israel on Mount Sinai" (27:34). Between those verses God teaches a rescued people how to live close to him without being destroyed by the very holiness that saved them.

The first movement (chapters 1–10) builds the machinery of approach. Five offerings are laid out twice — once for the worshiper, once for the priests: the burnt offering wholly consumed for acceptance and atonement, the meal offering of flour and oil, the peace offering, and the sin and trespass offerings for the guilt even of unintentional wrongs ("meal offering" and "trespass offering" are our WEB translation's names for the familiar "grain offering" and "guilt offering"), these last — the sin and trespass offerings — each ending in the promise "the priest shall make atonement for him, and he will be forgiven." Then Aaron and his sons receive their ordination — washed, clothed, anointed, and consecrated through seven days — and on the eighth day the system comes alive: Aaron blesses the people, "the LORD's glory appeared to all the people," and fire from before the LORD consumes the offerings. The same fire immediately shows its other edge — Aaron's own sons Nadab and Abihu offer "strange fire... which he had not commanded them" and die before the LORD, and the book's sober keynote is spoken over their bodies: "I will show myself holy to those who come near me."

The second movement (chapters 11–16) maps the boundary between clean and unclean — what may be eaten, the days after childbirth, the diagnosis and cleansing of skin disease and even of mildew in garments and houses, and bodily discharges. The point of the map is stated inside it: "You shall therefore be holy, for I am holy" (11:45), spoken by the God who brought Israel up out of Egypt. The movement crests at the book's center, the Day of Atonement: once in the year the high priest enters within the veil with blood, and a live goat, with all the iniquities of the children of Israel confessed onto its head, "shall carry all their iniquities on himself to a solitary land" — "for on this day shall atonement be made for you, to cleanse you. You shall be clean from all your sins before the LORD" (16:30). The logic beneath every altar in the book is given in one sentence: "For the life of the flesh is in the blood... it is the blood that makes atonement by reason of the life" (17:11).

The last movement (chapters 17–27) is often called the Holiness Code, and it carries holiness out of the sanctuary into ordinary life. "You shall be holy; for I, the LORD your God, am holy" (19:2) governs sexuality, honest scales, wages paid on time, care for the poor, the deaf, the blind, and the foreigner — including the command Jesus would later call the second greatest, "you shall love your neighbor as yourself" (19:18). Priests are held to a stricter standard; the year is ordered around the LORD's appointed feasts; every fiftieth year Israel must "proclaim liberty throughout the land to all its inhabitants" (25:10) as the Jubilee returns land and freedom to the poor. The book closes its covenant with blessings and hard warnings — at their heart God's desire, "I will walk among you, and will be your God, and you will be my people" (26:12) — and even its darkest warnings end in hope: if a scattered, exiled people confess their iniquity, "I will remember my covenant" (26:42). A short appendix on vows and valuations ends the book still at Sinai, the people taught, the tent occupied, the way of approach open.

# The twenty-seven chapters

## Leviticus 1
**Summary:** The LORD speaks from the Tent of Meeting his glory has just filled, and begins teaching Israel how to draw near — starting with the burnt offering, the offering given wholly to God. The worshiper brings a male without defect from herd or flock "at the door of the Tent of Meeting, that he may be accepted before the LORD," lays his hand on its head, "and it shall be accepted for him to make atonement for him." He kills the animal, the priests handle the blood and the fire, and the whole of it is burned on the altar — "an offering made by fire, of a pleasant aroma to the LORD." A turtledove or young pigeon may be brought instead, offered whole in the same way: nothing of a burnt offering is kept back.

## Leviticus 2
**Summary:** The meal offering is the offering of the harvest: fine flour with oil poured on it and frankincense laid on it, or unleavened cakes baked in the oven, on a griddle, or in a pan. The priest burns a handful as its "memorial" on the altar, and the rest is for Aaron and his sons, "a most holy part of the offerings of the LORD made by fire." No yeast and no honey may burn on the altar, but no offering may go without salt — "the salt of the covenant of your God." The chapter closes with the meal offering of first fruits: fresh heads of grain parched with fire, offered to the LORD at the front edge of the harvest.

## Leviticus 3
**Summary:** The peace offering may come from herd or flock, male or female, but always without defect. The chapter ends in a perpetual statute for every Israelite household: "all the fat is the LORD's," and "you shall eat neither fat nor blood."

## Leviticus 4
**Summary:** What happens when someone "sins unintentionally, in any of the things which the LORD has commanded not to be done"? This chapter's answer is the sin offering, graded by who sinned: the anointed priest brings a young bull and his blood is carried inside the Tent and sprinkled before the veil; the whole congregation, discovering a corporate sin, brings a bull through its elders; a ruler brings a male goat; one of the common people brings a female goat or lamb. In every case the pattern holds — hand on the head, blood applied to the altar, fat burned — and for congregation, ruler, and commoner it ends the same way: "the priest shall make atonement for them, and they shall be forgiven." Even sin done in ignorance is real guilt, and even real guilt has an appointed remedy.

## Leviticus 5
**Summary:** The sin-offering law turns to concrete cases: hearing a public call for witnesses and staying silent, touching something unclean without realizing it, swearing a rash oath. When guilt is known, "he shall confess that in which he has sinned" and bring his offering — and the offering bends to the offerer's means: a lamb or goat, or "If he can't afford a lamb," two turtledoves or pigeons, or even a measure of fine flour, with the same atonement and forgiveness at the end. The chapter then introduces the trespass offering for unwitting trespass against the LORD's holy things: a ram, plus restitution of the loss with a fifth added. Its last word is bracing: one who sins without ever knowing it "is still guilty, and shall bear his iniquity" — and for that person, too, there is a ram and forgiveness.

## Leviticus 6
**Summary:** The trespass offering reaches into ordinary dishonesty: dealing falsely with a neighbor "in a matter of deposit, or of bargain, or of robbery," oppressing him, or lying about lost property and swearing to the lie. At 6:8 the audience changes: now Aaron and his sons receive the working instructions for the offerings, beginning with the altar fire that must be tended every morning and never allowed to die — "Fire shall be kept burning on the altar continually; it shall not go out" — then the priests' portions of the meal offering, the anointed priest's own daily offering, and the careful handling of the most holy sin offering.

## Leviticus 7

## Leviticus 25
**Summary:** From Mount Sinai the LORD extends the Sabbath to the land itself: six years of sowing, then "a Sabbath of solemn rest for the land," with everyone — householder, servant, stranger, even livestock — living from what grows of itself. After seven sevens of years comes the Jubilee: on the Day of Atonement the trumpet sounds and Israel is to "proclaim liberty throughout the land to all its inhabitants" — each person returns to his property and family, sale prices are prorated by the years remaining, and to the anxious question "What shall we eat the seventh year?" God answers with a pledge: "I will command my blessing on you in the sixth year, and it shall bear fruit for the three years." Beneath it all lies a single claim: "The land shall not be sold in perpetuity, for the land is mine; for you are strangers and live as foreigners with me" — so land can always be redeemed by a kinsman, the poor brother must be upheld without interest or profit, and an Israelite who sells himself serves as a hired servant, not a slave, going free with his children at the Jubilee, "For they are my servants, whom I brought out of the land of Egypt." The chapter permits Israel to buy slaves from the surrounding nations and hold them as property — stated in the text without softening — while barring all harsh rule over Israelite brothers, and it closes on God's ownership of the people themselves.
**Tags:** `sabbath-rest` — the Sabbath principle written into the land: "in the seventh year there shall be a Sabbath of solemn rest for the land, a Sabbath to the LORD" (25:2–5, 11); `gods-provision` — the covenant pledge that makes the fallow year possible: "I will command my blessing on you in the sixth year, and it shall bear fruit for the three years" (25:19–22); `generosity` — "If your brother has become poor... you shall uphold him," with no interest on money and no profit on food (25:35–37); `justice-and-oppression` — "You shall not wrong one another": sales priced fairly by the years to Jubilee, no interest from the poor, no harsh rule over Israelite bondservants (25:14, 17, 35–43, 53); `kinsman-redeemer` — the institution's statute base: sold land redeemed by a kinsman, and a brother sold into servitude redeemed by his family or himself, with Jubilee behind both (25:25–28, 47–55); `sojourners-and-strangers` — the claim beneath the whole chapter: "The land shall not be sold in perpetuity, for the land is mine; for you are strangers and live as foreigners with me" (25:23)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings
**Sections:**
- **25:1–7 — The Seventh Year** (title: BSB) — Each seventh year the land keeps a Sabbath to the LORD: no sowing or pruning, and whatever grows of itself feeds household, servant, stranger, and animal alike.
- **25:8–22 — The Year of Jubilee** (title: BSB) — After forty-nine years the trumpet proclaims liberty in the fiftieth: everyone returns to property and family, sales are priced by the years to Jubilee so no one wrongs his neighbor, and God pledges a triple harvest in the sixth year.

## Leviticus 26
**Summary:** After a last word against idols and for Sabbaths and sanctuary, the covenant is laid out in its own terms of blessing and curse — terms addressed to Israel as a nation in the land, not a formula for anyone's private prosperity. If Israel walks in God's statutes: rain in season, harvests that run into each other, "You shall eat your bread to the full, and dwell in your land safely," peace, victory, fruitfulness — and above all God himself: "I will set my tent among you... I will walk among you, and will be your God, and you will be my people," the God who says "I have broken the bars of your yoke, and made you walk upright." But if Israel rejects the covenant, the curses come in escalating waves — "seven times more for your sins" — disease and defeat, sky like iron, wild animals, sword and pestilence, famine so severe that parents eat the flesh of their own sons and daughters, ruined sanctuaries, and exile that scatters them among the nations while the emptied land finally "will enjoy its Sabbaths." Yet the chapter refuses to end there: if the exiles confess their iniquity and their "uncircumcised heart is humbled," God "will remember my covenant with Jacob... Isaac, and... Abraham; and I will remember the land" — even in the enemy's land, "I will not reject them... to destroy them utterly and to break my covenant with them."
**Tags:** `covenant` — the chapter is the covenant's own sanctions: "I will... establish my covenant with you," "I will walk among you, and will be your God, and you will be my people," broken by rebellion (26:15) yet remembered — "I will remember my covenant with Jacob, my covenant with Isaac, and also my covenant with Abraham" (26:9–12, 15, 42–45); `obedience-to-the-word` — everything hangs on the hinge repeated at each turn: "If you walk in my statutes and keep my commandments, and do them" against "if you will not listen to me" (26:3, 14–15, 18, 21, 23, 27); `blessing` — the covenant's promised good for Israel's obedience in the land: rain in season, "You shall eat your bread to the full, and dwell in your land safely," peace and fruitfulness — covenant terms, not a general guarantee (26:3–13); `the-lords-discipline` — the curses escalate as correction, not mere destruction: "I will chastise you seven times more for your sins," each wave answering the refusal to be "turned back to me" (26:18, 23–24, 28); `repentance` — the door out of judgment: "If they confess their iniquity and the iniquity of their fathers... if then their uncircumcised heart is humbled, and they then accept the punishment of their iniquity" (26:40–41); `gods-faithfulness` — even under the curses God holds to his people: "I will not reject them, neither will I abhor them, to destroy them utterly and to break my covenant with them" (26:42, 44–45); `confession-of-sin` — confession as the exile's turning point: "If they confess their iniquity and the iniquity of their fathers," God remembers the covenant (26:40–42); `idolatry` — the covenant's opening line and its curse-side purge: no idols, engraved images, pillars, or figured stones, and the high places and incense altars destroyed under judgment (26:1, 30)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings
**Sections:**
- **26:1–13 — The Blessings of Obedience** (title: adapted from BSB) — No idols, Sabbaths kept, sanctuary revered; then the covenant's promises for obedience — rain, bread, peace, victory, fruitfulness, and God's own dwelling and walking among his people.
- **26:14–39 — Punishments for Disobedience** (title: BSB) — If Israel rejects the covenant, judgment comes in intensifying waves — sickness, defeat, drought, wild animals, sword, pestilence, famine, and finally desolation and exile, while the land at last enjoys the Sabbaths it was denied.
- **26:40–46 — God Remembers Those Who Repent** (title: BSB) — Confession and a humbled heart meet a God who remembers his covenant with Jacob, Isaac, and Abraham and will not destroy his exiled people utterly; the section closes the Sinai statutes given through Moses.
**Motifs (candidates):** "I will walk among you" (26:11–12) — "God dwelling with his people", "I will be your God and you will be my people meaning"; the land keeping its lost Sabbaths (26:34–35, 43) — "why seventy years of exile", "the land enjoyed its sabbaths"; the humbled, uncircumcised heart (26:41) — "circumcision of the heart", "what does a humbled heart mean".

## Leviticus 27
**Summary:** The book closes with an appendix on vows and consecrated things — what happens when someone freely promises persons, animals, houses, or fields to the LORD. Persons vowed to God are released by a fixed valuation in sanctuary shekels, scaled by age and sex, and mercy is built into the scale: "But if he is poorer than your valuation... The priest shall assign a value according to his ability to pay." Clean animals once given become holy and cannot be swapped; unclean animals, houses, and fields are valued by the priest and may be bought back by adding a fifth, with fields reckoned by the years to Jubilee. Some things cannot be vowed at all because they are already God's — the firstborn of animals — and things "permanently devoted" are most holy and beyond redemption, including the person under the ban of destruction, who cannot be ransomed. So too the tithe: "All the tithe of the land, whether of the seed of the land or of the fruit of the trees, is the LORD's. It is holy to the LORD," and of the herds, "whatever passes under the rod, the tenth shall be holy to the LORD." Vows are voluntary — but once made, they are holy.
**Tags:** `tithing` — the concept's core Old Testament text: "All the tithe of the land... is the LORD's. It is holy to the LORD," and the tenth of herd and flock "shall be holy to the LORD" (27:30–33). (Only one honest tag from the 2026-08-23 vocabulary; the one below was added 2026-08-25 from the adopted display-tag list.); `oaths-and-vows` — the Bible's fullest vow-valuation system: persons, animals, houses, and fields freely vowed to the LORD, released by valuation with mercy scaled to the poor, and devoted things beyond redemption — vows voluntary, but once made, holy (27:1–29)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings
**Motifs (candidates):** vows made freely, kept faithfully (27:2–13) — "making a vow to God", "should Christians make vows", "keeping promises to God"; valuation adjusted to ability to pay (27:8) — "does God expect the same from everyone", "God and the poor"; what is devoted is the LORD's (27:28) — "what does devoted to the LORD mean", "consecration in the Bible".
