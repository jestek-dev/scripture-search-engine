# Acts — book summary (draft)

## Short

Acts is Luke's second volume — “The first book I wrote, Theophilus, concerned all that Jesus began both to do and to teach” — and it tells what the risen and ascended Jesus kept doing through his people. Its whole shape is given in his last sentence before the ascension: “You will be witnesses to me in Jerusalem, in all Judea and Samaria, and to the uttermost parts of the earth.” The Holy Spirit falls at Pentecost, and the story follows that promise outward step by step: a praying, sharing community in Jerusalem; persecution that scatters believers into Judea and Samaria preaching as they go; a persecutor named Saul stopped on the Damascus road; a Roman centurion's household receiving the Spirit while Peter is still speaking; and then journey after journey across the Roman world, until the book ends with Paul in Rome — under guard, yet “preaching God’s Kingdom and teaching the things concerning the Lord Jesus Christ with all boldness, without hindrance.”

## Full

Acts opens by looking back at Luke's Gospel — “The first book I wrote, Theophilus, concerned all that Jesus began both to do and to teach” (1:1) — and the word “began” carries the book's premise: the story is not over. For forty days the risen Jesus shows himself alive “by many proofs” and speaks about God's Kingdom; then, told to wait in Jerusalem for the promise of the Father, the disciples receive his program for everything that follows: “You will receive power when the Holy Spirit has come upon you. You will be witnesses to me in Jerusalem, in all Judea and Samaria, and to the uttermost parts of the earth” (1:8). He is taken up into a cloud, and the book spends its twenty-eight chapters tracing exactly that geography.

The first movement (chapters 1–7) stays in Jerusalem. At Pentecost the Spirit comes with a sound like a rushing mighty wind and tongues like fire, and Peter — the man who denied Jesus weeks earlier — stands up and preaches that “God has made him both Lord and Christ, this Jesus whom you crucified”; about three thousand are baptized. The new community devotes itself to the apostles' teaching, fellowship, breaking of bread, and prayer, holding possessions in common and selling property for anyone in need. Healing at the temple gate brings crowds and arrests; forbidden to speak, the apostles answer, “We must obey God rather than men,” and pray for boldness rather than safety. There is failure inside as well as pressure outside — Ananias and Sapphira fall dead after lying about a gift — and the movement's first structures appear when seven men are chosen so the Greek-speaking widows are not neglected. The Jerusalem section closes with Stephen, the first to die for Jesus' name, praying like his Lord: “Lord, don’t hold this sin against them!”

Stephen's death triggers the second movement (chapters 8–12): “They were all scattered abroad throughout the regions of Judea and Samaria… those who were scattered abroad went around preaching the word.” Philip takes the Good News to Samaria and to an Ethiopian official reading Isaiah in his chariot. Saul, the persecution's fiercest agent, is confronted on the Damascus road — “Saul, Saul, why do you persecute me?” — and becomes the movement's most driven preacher. Peter, led by a vision he does not want and a Spirit he cannot withstand, enters the Gentile house of the centurion Cornelius, and while he is still speaking the Holy Spirit falls on everyone listening; the Jerusalem assembly (“assembly” is the WEB's word for what other translations call the church) can only conclude, “Then God has also granted to the Gentiles repentance to life!” In Antioch an assembly of Jews and Greeks grows up where “the disciples were first called Christians,” and when Herod kills James and imprisons Peter, an angel walks Peter out of the cell while the assembly prays — and Herod, accepting a crowd's cry of “The voice of a god,” is struck down. The refrain lands again: “But the word of God grew and multiplied.”

The third movement (chapters 13–28) belongs to the uttermost parts. Sent out from Antioch at the Holy Spirit's word, Barnabas and Saul — now Paul — carry the message through Cyprus and Asia Minor, turning deliberately to the Gentiles when synagogues refuse it. The Jerusalem council (chapter 15) settles the great question that success creates — must Gentile believers be circumcised? — with Peter's testimony that God “made no distinction between us and them, cleansing their hearts by faith” (15:9). A night vision calls Paul across to Macedonia, and the word plants assemblies in Philippi, Thessalonica, Beroea, Corinth, and Ephesus, among jailers and merchants, philosophers and rioting silversmiths — opponents in Thessalonica complain that “these who have turned the world upside down have come here also” (17:6). Warned that bonds await, Paul goes to Jerusalem anyway — “I am ready not only to be bound, but also to die at Jerusalem for the name of the Lord Jesus” (21:13) — is seized in the temple, and spends the book's last quarter testifying before crowds, councils, governors, and kings, appealing at last to Caesar. The Lord had stood by him: “as you have testified about me at Jerusalem, so you must testify also at Rome” (23:11) — and through storm and shipwreck he arrives. The book ends deliberately open: two years in a rented house in Rome, “preaching God’s Kingdom and teaching the things concerning the Lord Jesus Christ with all boldness, without hindrance” (28:31). The witness has reached the empire's center; the road to the uttermost parts is still open.

Throughout, Acts presents the spread as God's own doing: the Spirit fills, forbids, sends, and speaks; angels open prison doors; visions redirect journeys; and every persecution scatters the seed farther. Luke keeps counting — three thousand, five thousand, multitudes, “the word of God grew” — not to celebrate the assembly but to record that, as Gamaliel warned the council, a work that is of God cannot be overthrown.

# The twenty-eight chapters

## Acts 1
**Summary:** Luke addresses his second book to Theophilus: over forty days the risen Jesus showed himself alive “by many proofs,” spoke about God's Kingdom, and commanded the apostles to wait in Jerusalem for the promise of the Father — “you will be baptized in the Holy Spirit not many days from now.” To their question about restoring the kingdom to Israel he answers that times and seasons are the Father's, but “you will receive power when the Holy Spirit has come upon you. You will be witnesses to me in Jerusalem, in all Judea and Samaria, and to the uttermost parts of the earth.” Then he is taken up, a cloud receives him, and two men in white promise that “this Jesus… will come back in the same way as you saw him going into the sky.” About a hundred twenty believers — including the eleven, the women, Mary, and Jesus' brothers — continue steadfastly in prayer in the upper room, and at Peter's word they put forward two men to replace Judas as a witness of the resurrection; after prayer the lot falls on Matthias.

## Acts 2
**Summary:** On the day of Pentecost a sound from the sky “like the rushing of a mighty wind” fills the house, tongues like fire rest on each of them, and “they were all filled with the Holy Spirit and began to speak with other languages, as the Spirit gave them the ability to speak” — and devout Jews from every nation under the sky each hear “the mighty works of God” in their own native language, while some mock about new wine. Peter, standing with the eleven, answers: this is what Joel promised — “I will pour out my Spirit on all flesh” — and Jesus of Nazareth, attested by God with mighty works, “being delivered up by the determined counsel and foreknowledge of God,” was crucified by lawless hands and raised by God, as David foresaw; “God has made him both Lord and Christ, this Jesus whom you crucified.” Cut to the heart, the crowd asks what to do, and Peter answers, “Repent and be baptized, every one of you, in the name of Jesus Christ for the forgiveness of sins, and you will receive the gift of the Holy Spirit” — and about three thousand souls are added that day. The new community continues steadfastly “in the apostles’ teaching and fellowship, in the breaking of bread, and prayer,” holding all things in common, selling possessions to meet any need, eating together with gladness — “The Lord added to the assembly day by day those who were being saved.”

## Acts 3
**Summary:** Going up to the temple at the hour of prayer, Peter and John meet a man lame from his mother's womb, laid daily at the Beautiful gate to beg. Peter tells him, “I have no silver or gold, but what I have, that I give you. In the name of Jesus Christ of Nazareth, get up and walk!” — and the man leaps up and enters the temple with them, “walking, leaping, and praising God,” to the amazement of all who recognize him. To the crowd that runs together in Solomon's porch, Peter denies that any power or godliness of his own did it: the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob has glorified his Servant Jesus — the one they denied before Pilate when they “killed the Prince of life, whom God raised from the dead” — and “by faith in his name, his name has made this man strong.” He calls them, as people who acted in ignorance, to “repent therefore, and turn again, that your sins may be blotted out,” promising times of refreshing and the times of “restoration of all things,” and reminds them they are children of the prophets and of the covenant: God raised up his servant and sent him to bless them first, “in turning away every one of you from your wickedness.”

## Acts 4

## Acts 6

## Acts 7

## Acts 8

## Acts 16

## Acts 25

## Acts 26
**Summary:** Given leave to speak, Paul makes his defense before Agrippa as testimony: a Pharisee who lived “after the strictest sect of our religion,” he now stands judged “for the hope of the promise made by God to our fathers” — and asks the court, “Why is it judged incredible with you if God does raise the dead?” He recounts his rage against the name of Jesus of Nazareth — locking up saints, casting his vote for their deaths, trying to make them blaspheme — and then the noon light “brighter than the sun” on the Damascus road, where the voice said, “Saul, Saul, why are you persecuting me? It is hard for you to kick against the goads,” and Jesus appointed him a servant and witness, sent to the Gentiles “to open their eyes, that they may turn from darkness to light and from the power of Satan to God.” “I was not disobedient to the heavenly vision,” Paul says: from Damascus to Jerusalem, Judea, and the Gentiles he has declared “that they should repent and turn to God, doing works worthy of repentance,” testifying to small and great “nothing but what the prophets and Moses said would happen, how the Christ must suffer, and how, by the resurrection of the dead, he would be first to proclaim light both to these people and to the Gentiles.” Festus interrupts loudly — “Paul, you are crazy!” — but Paul answers that he speaks “words of truth and reasonableness,” for “this has not been done in a corner,” and presses the king directly: “King Agrippa, do you believe the prophets? I know that you believe.” Agrippa deflects — “With a little persuasion are you trying to make me a Christian?” — Paul wishes all his hearers were as he is, “except for these bonds,” and the court withdraws agreeing, “This man does nothing worthy of death or of bonds”; Agrippa adds that he could have been set free had he not appealed to Caesar.

## Acts 27
**Summary:** Handed with other prisoners to the centurion Julius, Paul sails for Italy by stages — kindly allowed at Sidon to visit his friends — and at Myra the party transfers to an Alexandrian ship, which struggles against contrary winds to Fair Havens on Crete. With the sailing season past, Paul warns that the voyage will end “with injury and much loss,” but the centurion heeds the shipmaster and owner, and a soft south wind lures them out — into the storm-wind called Euroclydon. Driven for days with neither sun nor stars, cargo and tackle thrown overboard, “all hope that we would be saved was now taken away” — until Paul stands up among them with a word from “an angel, belonging to the God whose I am and whom I serve”: “Don’t be afraid, Paul. You must stand before Caesar. Behold, God has granted you all those who sail with you.” “Therefore, sirs, cheer up! For I believe God, that it will be just as it has been spoken to me,” he says — and on the fourteenth night, as land nears, he foils the sailors' escape attempt, urges all two hundred seventy-six aboard to eat, promising “not a hair will perish from any of your heads,” and gives thanks to God in front of everyone as he breaks bread. At daylight they run the ship aground in an unknown bay; the stern breaks up in the violence of the waves, the soldiers' plan to kill the prisoners is stopped by the centurion “desiring to save Paul,” and — swimming, or floating in on planks and wreckage — “they all escaped safely to the land.”

You must stand before Caesar” (27:23–24); `gods-protection` — “God has granted you all those who sail with you,” “not a hair will perish from any of your heads,” and all two hundred seventy-six “escaped safely to the land” (27:24, 34, 37, 44)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings

- **27:1–12 — Paul Sails for Rome** (title: BSB) — Under the courteous centurion Julius the voyage crawls to Fair Havens too late in the season; Paul's warning of disaster is overruled by the ship's officers and the majority.
- **27:13–26 — The Storm at Sea** (title: BSB) — A deceptive south wind gives way to Euroclydon; after days without sky or hope, Paul relays the angel's promise — no life lost, Caesar ahead, the ship to run aground on an island — and calls all aboard to take heart.
- **27:27–44 — The Shipwreck** (title: BSB) — Soundings, anchors, a thwarted escape, and a shared meal fill the fourteenth night; at day the ship grounds and breaks apart, the prisoners are spared for Paul's sake, and every soul reaches land safely.

## Acts 28
**Summary:** Ashore, the survivors learn the island is Malta, whose people show them “uncommon kindness,” kindling a fire against the rain and cold. When a viper fastens on Paul's hand, the islanders first take him for a murderer whom Justice will not let live, then — watching him unharmed — “said that he was a god”; at the estate of Publius, the island's chief man, Paul prays and lays hands on his host's fever-stricken father and heals him, and the rest of the island's sick come and are cured. Three months later an Alexandrian ship carries the party by Syracuse and Rhegium to Puteoli, where brothers keep them seven days; more brothers walk out from Rome as far as The Market of Appius and The Three Taverns, and “When Paul saw them, he thanked God and took courage.” In Rome, allowed to live by himself with his guard, Paul calls together the local Jewish leaders, explains that “because of the hope of Israel I am bound with this chain,” and on an appointed day testifies about God's Kingdom and Jesus “from morning until evening,” arguing from the law of Moses and the prophets; some believe, some disbelieve, and Paul answers the divided verdict with Isaiah's words about a calloused-hearted people, adding, “the salvation of God is sent to the nations, and they will listen.” The book ends open-ended and unstoppable: two whole years in his own rented house, receiving all who come, “preaching God’s Kingdom” and teaching about the Lord Jesus Christ “with all boldness, without hindrance.”
**Tags:** `hospitality` — “The natives showed us uncommon kindness”; Publius “received us and courteously entertained us for three days”; the brothers of Puteoli keep the travelers seven days (28:2, 7, 14); `pastoral-prayer-for-healing` — “Paul entered in to him, prayed, and laying his hands on him, healed him” — and the island's other sick “came and were cured” (28:8–9); `gods-protection` — the viper shaken off into the fire: Paul “wasn’t harmed,” against every watching expectation (28:3–6); `sharing-your-faith` — “testifying about God’s Kingdom, and persuading them concerning Jesus” from morning till evening, then two unhindered years of preaching and teaching all comers (28:23, 30–31)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings

- **28:1–10 — Ashore on Malta** (title: BSB) — Kind islanders, a harmless snakebite that flips their verdict from “murderer” to “god,” and healings beginning with Publius's father fill three honored winter months.
- **28:11–15 — Paul Arrives in Italy** (title: BSB) — An Alexandrian ship brings the company to Puteoli and a week with the brothers; believers walking out from Rome to meet Paul move him to thanksgiving and courage.
