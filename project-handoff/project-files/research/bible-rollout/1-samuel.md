# 1 Samuel — book summary (draft)

## Short

1 Samuel is the story of how Israel got its first kings — and of the God who "looks at the heart" while man looks at the outward appearance. It opens small: a childless woman weeping at Shiloh, whose answered prayer becomes Samuel, the prophet who will anoint both kings. When Israel demands a king "like all the nations," God grants the request while naming its cost, and tall, impressive Saul rises — then unravels, rejected for refusing to obey the word of the LORD that made him king. In his place God sends Samuel to a Bethlehem shepherd boy, and the rest of the book runs on two tracks: David killing Goliath, sparing Saul twice, and gathering the desperate in the wilderness, while Saul descends through jealousy to a night visit to a medium at Endor and death on Mount Gilboa. The book's verdicts stand in its own sentences: "to obey is better than sacrifice," and "those who honor me I will honor."

## Full

1 Samuel opens where Judges left Israel — no king, a corrupt priesthood, and the word of the LORD rare — and it begins the answer not with a battle but with a barren woman's prayer. Hannah, provoked and grieved, pours out her soul at Shiloh and vows her hoped-for son to the LORD; the boy is Samuel, and her song over him — the LORD brings low and lifts up, raises the poor from the dust, and "will give strength to his king, and exalt the horn of his anointed" — announces the book's whole theology before there is any king to sing about.

The first movement (chapters 1–7) hands Israel from failed priests to a faithful prophet. Eli's sons treat the LORD's offering with contempt, and judgment is pronounced on their house; the boy Samuel, sleeping by the ark, hears the LORD call his name and grows into a prophet whose words do not fall to the ground. Israel, treating the ark as a battle charm, loses it to the Philistines — "The glory has departed from Israel!" — but the LORD needs no army to defend himself: Dagon falls on his face before the ark, plague drives the Philistines to send it home on a cart pulled by unled cows, and when Israel finally returns to the LORD at Mizpah, putting away its foreign gods, God thunders the Philistines into confusion. Samuel raises a stone and names the book's quiet confidence: Ebenezer, "The LORD helped us until now."

The second movement (chapters 8–15) gives Israel the king it asked for. When Samuel is old, the elders demand a king "like all the nations," and the LORD's word to Samuel exposes what the request really is: "they have not rejected you, but they have rejected me as the king over them." Saul — head and shoulders above the people, from the smallest tribe — is anointed privately, confirmed publicly, and begins well, rescuing Jabesh Gilead and crediting the LORD with the victory. But two failures of obedience end his dynasty and then his kingship: at Gilgal he offers the burnt offering rather than wait for Samuel, and against Amalek he spares King Agag and the best of the flocks in defiance of the LORD's explicit command. Samuel's reply is the book's most quoted sentence — "to obey is better than sacrifice" — and the chapter holds two hard sayings side by side without blushing: "It grieves me that I have set up Saul to be king," and "the Strength of Israel will not lie nor repent; for he is not a man, that he should repent."

The third movement (chapters 16–31) runs the rejected king and the anointed shepherd on a collision course. Samuel, taught that "man looks at the outward appearance, but the LORD looks at the heart," anoints David, Jesse's youngest; the LORD's Spirit comes mightily on David and departs from Saul. David fells Goliath with a sling and a sentence — "the battle is the LORD's" — wins Jonathan's covenant friendship and the people's love, and by that very success becomes the target of Saul's spear. The long middle chapters are a fugitive's story: Nob and its slaughtered priests, the cave at Adullam, Keilah, the wilderness of Ziph, and twice Saul within reach of David's hand — once unaware in a cave, once asleep in his camp — spared both times because "who can stretch out his hand against the LORD's anointed, and be guiltless?" David's waiting is not sinless: he lies to a priest, feigns madness at Gath, nearly massacres a fool's household until Abigail's wisdom stops him, and finally despairs into Philistine service, raiding from Ziklag behind a screen of deception. The book does not gloss any of it; it simply keeps showing a man who will not seize by blood what God has promised to give.

The endings come in darkness. Samuel dies. Saul, facing the massed Philistines with heaven silent, seeks a medium at Endor by night and hears from Samuel one last time: "tomorrow you and your sons will be with me." While David — turned back from the Philistine muster and rescued from his own compromise — recovers everything the Amalekites took from Ziklag, Saul's army breaks on Mount Gilboa; his sons fall, and Saul dies on his own sword. The valiant men of Jabesh Gilead, remembering their first rescue, walk all night to give the bodies an honorable burial. The throne is empty, the anointed shepherd is still in exile, and the book leans forward into 2 Samuel with its question already answered in principle: the LORD's king is the man after his own heart.

# The thirty-one chapters

## 1 Samuel 1
**Summary:** Elkanah of Ramathaim Zophim has two wives: Peninnah, who has children, and Hannah, who has none — and whose rival provokes her year after year at the LORD's house in Shiloh until she weeps and cannot eat. In bitterness of soul Hannah prays and vows that if the LORD of Armies will give her a son, she will give him to the LORD all the days of his life. Eli the priest, watching her lips move soundlessly, mistakes her for a drunk, then blesses her petition; she goes home, and "the LORD remembered her." She names her son Samuel — "Because I have asked him of the LORD" — and when he is weaned she brings him back to Shiloh and keeps the vow to the full: "As long as he lives he is given to the LORD."
**Tags:** `waiting-for-a-child` — the childless years ("the LORD had shut up her womb," 1:5–6), the taunting rival, and the prayer for "a boy" answered when "the LORD remembered her" (1:11, 19–20); `prayer` — "I poured out my soul before the LORD" (1:15), and the testimony "the LORD has given me my petition which I asked of him" (1:27; 1:10–17); `pastoral-god-sees-my-suffering` — Hannah's own plea: "if you will indeed look at the affliction of your servant and remember me" (1:11; personal register); `surrender-to-god` — the vow kept at full cost: "I have also given him to the LORD. As long as he lives he is given to the LORD" (1:11, 28); `worship` — the yearly worship at Shiloh (1:3, 19) and the chapter's last word: "He worshiped the LORD there" (1:28); `oaths-and-vows` — a vow made in anguish and kept to the full: Hannah vows her longed-for son to the LORD for life, and when he is weaned she performs it — "I have also given him to the LORD. As long as he lives he is given to the LORD" (1:11, 24–28)

## 1 Samuel 2
**Summary:** Hannah's prayer of thanksgiving turns her private answer into public theology: there is no one as holy as the LORD; he kills and makes alive, brings low and lifts up, raises the poor out of the dust — and, before Israel has any king, "He will give strength to his king, and exalt the horn of his anointed." The scene then darkens: Eli's sons Hophni and Phinehas are wicked men who bully worshipers out of the LORD's offering and sleep with the women serving at the Tent of Meeting, and their aged father's protest is too little and too late. Between the crimes, the child Samuel ministers before the LORD, clothed with a linen ephod — a priestly vest — while his mother brings him a little robe year by year, and he grows in favor with the LORD and with men. A man of God delivers the sentence on Eli's house — "those who honor me I will honor, and those who despise me will be cursed" — with a sign attached: both sons will die in one day, and God will raise up a faithful priest for himself.
**Tags:** `praise` — Hannah's exultant song: "My heart exults in the LORD!... There is no one as holy as the LORD" (2:1–2); `humble-exaltation` — "He raises up the poor out of the dust... He brings low, he also lifts up" (2:7–8); `sin` — "the sons of Eli were wicked men," and "The sin of the young men was very great before the LORD; for the men despised the LORD's offering" (2:12, 17, 22); `divine-judgment` — the oracle against Eli's house: "those who honor me I will honor, and those who despise me will be cursed," sealed with the sign of the two sons' one-day death (2:30–34)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings
**Sections:**
- **2:1–11 — Hannah's Prayer of Thanksgiving** (title: BSB) — Hannah's song of the God who reverses fortunes — breaking the bows of the mighty, raising the poor, and strengthening his anointed — before Elkanah's family goes home and the child stays to serve the LORD.
- **2:12–26 — Eli's Wicked Sons** (title: BSB) — Hophni and Phinehas despise the LORD's offering and defile the sanctuary; Eli rebukes but does not restrain them; Samuel grows before the LORD as the yearly little robe marks Hannah's continuing care.
- **2:27–36 — A Prophecy against the House of Eli** (title: BSB) — A man of God announces the end of Eli's priestly house — honor forfeited by dishonor — and the raising up of a faithful priest who will walk before God's anointed.

## 1 Samuel 3
**Summary:** In days when the LORD's word is rare and visions few, the boy Samuel lies down near the ark and hears his name called — three times he runs to Eli, until the old priest realizes who is calling and teaches him the answer: "Speak, LORD; for your servant hears." The LORD's message is a heavy one for a child to carry: Eli's house will be judged forever, because his sons brought a curse on themselves and he didn't restrain them. In the morning Samuel is afraid to tell the vision, but Eli insists on hearing everything, and receives it with submission: "It is the LORD. Let him do what seems good to him." Samuel grows, the LORD lets none of his words fall to the ground, and all Israel from Dan to Beersheba learns that a prophet of the LORD is established at Shiloh.
**Tags:** `dreams-and-visions` — the night revelation in a day when "There were not many visions" (3:1), received at the LORD's call and named "the vision" Samuel feared to tell (3:10–15); `divine-judgment` — "I will judge his house forever for the iniquity which he knew" (3:13–14); `surrender-to-god` — Eli's answer to the hardest word: "It is the LORD. Let him do what seems good to him" (3:18)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings

## 1 Samuel 4
**Summary:** Israel loses about four thousand men to the Philistines at Ebenezer, and the elders reach for the ark of the LORD's covenant as if it were a weapon: "that it may come among us and save us." The ark arrives with Hophni and Phinehas, Israel's shout shakes the earth, and the frightened Philistines fight all the harder — thirty thousand of Israel fall, the two priests die as the man of God foretold, and God's ark is taken. The news travels to Shiloh, where old Eli, trembling for the ark, falls backward from his seat at the words and dies; his daughter-in-law dies in childbirth naming her son Ichabod: "The glory has departed from Israel; for God's ark has been taken." The chapter's grief is deliberate — Israel treated the LORD's presence as a talisman and discovered he will not be carried.

## 1 Samuel 5
**Summary:** The Philistines set God's ark in the house of Dagon at Ashdod as a trophy — and twice find their god fallen on his face before it, the second time with head and hands cut off on the threshold. No Israelite lifts a finger in this chapter; captured and unaccompanied, the LORD humiliates the idol and afflicts the idol's people until the lords of Philistia are begging to send the ark home.

## 1 Samuel 6
**Summary:** After seven months of plague the Philistine priests and diviners design the ark's return as a test: a new cart, two milk cows that have never been yoked, their calves shut up at home, and golden images of the tumors and mice as a trespass offering — if the cows head for Israel against every motherly instinct, "he has done us this great evil." The cows walk straight to Beth Shemesh, lowing as they go, and the reaping Israelites rejoice, split the cart for firewood, and offer the cows to the LORD. But the LORD's holiness is not safer on Israel's side of the border: he strikes men of Beth Shemesh for looking into the ark, and the mourners ask the chapter's question — "Who is able to stand before the LORD, this holy God?" — before sending the ark on to Kiriath Jearim.
**Tags:** `divine-judgment` — the plague acknowledged in gold ("images of your tumors," 6:4–5), and the men of Beth Shemesh struck "because they had looked into the LORD's ark" (6:19–20). ## 1 Samuel 7
**Summary:** The ark settles at Kiriath Jearim for twenty years, "and all the house of Israel lamented after the LORD." Samuel names the condition of rescue — return to the LORD with all your heart, put away the foreign gods and the Ashtaroth, serve him only — and Israel does it, gathering at Mizpah to fast, pour out water before the LORD, and confess: "We have sinned against the LORD." When the Philistines attack the penitent assembly, Israel begs Samuel not to stop crying out to God; as he offers a lamb, the LORD thunders the Philistines into confusion, and Israel routs them and recovers its cities. Samuel sets up a memorial stone between Mizpah and Shen and names it Ebenezer — "The LORD helped us until now" — then judges Israel on circuit all his days, with an altar to the LORD at his home in Ramah.

## 1 Samuel 8
**Summary:** Samuel grows old, his sons take bribes and pervert justice, and the elders of Israel use the failure as their opening: "make us a king to judge us like all the nations." Samuel is displeased and prays, and the LORD's answer reframes everything — "they have not rejected you, but they have rejected me as the king over them" — while telling Samuel to listen to the people and to warn them. The warning is a portrait of royal taking: sons for chariots, daughters for kitchens, fields and vineyards and tenths for the court, "and you will be his servants," until the day they cry out because of their chosen king and the LORD will not answer. The people refuse to listen — "No, but we will have a king over us... and fight our battles" — and the LORD tells Samuel to make them a king.

## 1 Samuel 9

## 1 Samuel 10
**Summary:** Samuel pours oil on Saul's head — "Hasn't the LORD anointed you to be prince over his inheritance?" — and gives him three confirming signs for the road home: two men at Rachel's tomb, three pilgrims with bread at the oak of Tabor, and a band of prophets at the hill of God, where the LORD's Spirit will come mightily on him. ## 1 Samuel 11
**Summary:** Nahash the Ammonite besieges Jabesh Gilead and offers a covenant with one condition: every right eye gouged out, as a dishonor to all Israel. When the messengers reach Gibeah, Saul comes in from plowing, hears the news, and God's Spirit comes mightily on him: he cuts a yoke of oxen in pieces and summons Israel on pain of the same — "The dread of the LORD fell on the people, and they came out as one man." Attacking in three companies in the morning watch, Saul scatters the Ammonites until no two are left together, and then refuses the crowd's demand to execute his earlier despisers: "No man shall be put to death today; for today the LORD has rescued Israel." At Gilgal the kingdom is renewed; Saul is made king before the LORD, with peace offerings and great rejoicing.

## 1 Samuel 12
**Summary:** With the king now walking before them, old Samuel puts his own record on trial — whose ox have I taken, whom have I defrauded, from whose hand a bribe? — and all Israel acquits him before the LORD. Then he pleads the LORD's righteous acts, from Jacob's family in Egypt through the deliverers of the judges' era, and names the sin at the heart of the king request: when Nahash came, "you said to me, 'No, but a king shall reign over us,' when the LORD your God was your king." ## 1 Samuel 14
**Summary:** Jonathan and his armor bearer climb alone to the Philistine garrison on a conviction, not a plan: "It may be that the LORD will work for us, for there is no restraint on the LORD to save by many or by few." The sign he asked for is given, about twenty men fall, and God finishes what two started — an earthquake, panic in the camp, Philistines striking each other — until "the LORD saved Israel that day." But Saul has bound the army under a rash oath — cursed is anyone who eats before evening — which Jonathan, not having heard it, breaks with a taste of wild honey; the famished troops later pounce on the plunder and eat meat with the blood, and when God will not answer Saul's inquiry, the lot exposes Jonathan.
