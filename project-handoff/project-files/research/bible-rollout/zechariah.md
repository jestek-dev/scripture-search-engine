## Short

Zechariah spoke to the small, discouraged community rebuilding Jerusalem after the exile, alongside his contemporary Haggai, and no prophet packed more hope into a harder moment. The book opens with a sequence of night visions — horsemen among myrtle trees, horns and craftsmen, a measuring line, the high priest Joshua cleansed of his filthy garments, a golden lamp stand, a flying scroll, a woman in a basket, four chariots — each one an assurance that God is jealous for Jerusalem and will finish what he has started: "Not by might, nor by power, but by my Spirit." A question about fasting becomes a summons to justice, truth, and kindness. Then the later chapters look further out, in some of the Old Testament's most quoted lines: a king coming to Zion "lowly, and riding on a donkey"; a shepherd rejected and valued at thirty pieces of silver; a pierced one mourned; a fountain opened for sin; a struck shepherd and a refined remnant; and at last "The LORD will be King over all the earth."

## Full

Zechariah dates his call precisely: "In the eighth month, in the second year of Darius" (1:1) — 520 BC, two months after Haggai began preaching. The audience is the generation that returned from Babylon to a ruined Jerusalem, where the temple foundation had been laid and then abandoned for years. Haggai's word got the building restarted; Zechariah's book supplies the vision behind the work — why a small, poor, unwalled community should believe its labor matters. His opening sermon sets the book's terms with an offer and a warning: "'Return to me,' says the LORD of Armies, 'and I will return to you'" (1:3) — the LORD of Armies being our WEB translation's rendering of the title often remembered as "the LORD of hosts," God as commander of heaven's armies. The fathers ignored the former prophets and were overtaken; this generation is asked not to repeat them.

Then comes the book's famous first movement: a sequence of visions in a single night (1:7), traditionally counted as eight. Riders patrolling among the myrtle trees report the earth at rest while Jerusalem still waits, and God answers with "kind and comforting words" — he is jealous for Zion, his house will be rebuilt (1:8–17). Four horns that scattered Judah are cast down by four craftsmen (1:18–21). A man with a measuring line learns that Jerusalem will overflow its walls, because "'For I,' says the LORD, 'will be to her a wall of fire around it, and I will be the glory in the middle of her'" (2:1–5). Joshua the high priest stands accused by Satan in filthy garments, and God rebukes the accuser and reclothes his priest: "Behold, I have caused your iniquity to pass from you" (3:1–5) — and promises to bring his servant "the Branch" and to "remove the iniquity of that land in one day" (3:8–9). A golden lamp stand fed by two olive trees carries the word to Zerubbabel, the governor rebuilding the temple: "Not by might, nor by power, but by my Spirit" (4:6) — mountains of difficulty will become a plain, and the hands that laid the foundation will finish it, however small the day of small things looks. A flying scroll carries the curse against theft and false swearing into the offender's own house (5:1–4); Wickedness herself is sealed in a basket and carried out of the land (5:5–11); four chariots patrol the earth from between mountains of bronze (6:1–8). The visions end in a sign-act: crowns set on Joshua's head for "the man whose name is the Branch," who will build the LORD's temple and be "a priest on his throne" (6:9–15).

Two years later a delegation asks whether the fasts mourning Jerusalem's fall should continue now that rebuilding is underway. God's answer turns the question inside out — "did you at all fast to me, really to me?" (7:5) — and restates what he had always asked: true judgment, kindness, compassion, no oppression of widow, fatherless, foreigner, or poor (7:8–10). Chapter 8 then pours out promises: God has returned to Zion, Jerusalem will be called "The City of Truth," its streets "full of boys and girls playing," the fasts will become "joy, gladness, and cheerful feasts," and people from many nations will grab the clothing of a Jew saying, "We will go with you, for we have heard that God is with you" (8:1–23).

The last six chapters are two long oracles (9:1; 12:1) that look past Zechariah's own day. The first sweeps judgment down the coastlands, then pivots to the book's most famous verse: "Behold, your King comes to you! He is righteous, and having salvation; lowly, and riding on a donkey" (9:9) — the verse the Gospels cite when Jesus enters Jerusalem on Palm Sunday (Matthew 21:4–5; John 12:14–15). Under Zechariah's king, chariot and battle bow are cut off — "he will speak peace to the nations" — and prisoners of hope are called back to the stronghold (9:10–12). Chapter 10 promises rain, regathering, and strength from the God who has mercy on his flock, in contrast to the empty comfort of household idols and diviners. Chapter 11 is the book's darkest passage: the prophet plays the shepherd of a "flock of slaughter," is loathed and paid off with thirty pieces of silver — "the handsome price that I was valued at by them!" (11:13) — and then plays the foolish shepherd who devours the flock. Matthew's Gospel later hears this passage in Judas's thirty pieces of silver and the potter's field (Matthew 26:15; 27:9–10).

The second oracle (12–14) circles one phrase — "in that day" — through siege, deliverance, mourning, cleansing, and enthronement. Jerusalem becomes "a cup of reeling" and "a burdensome stone" to the nations that besiege it, and the LORD defends his people (12:1–9). Then God pours out "the spirit of grace and of supplication," and "They will look to me whom they have pierced; and they shall mourn for him as one mourns for his only son" (12:10) — a line John's Gospel cites at the crucifixion (John 19:37) and Revelation echoes (Revelation 1:7). "In that day there will be a fountain opened to David's house and to the inhabitants of Jerusalem, for sin and for uncleanness" (13:1), idols and lying prophets pass from the land, and the sword awakes against God's own shepherd — "Strike the shepherd, and the sheep will be scattered" (13:7), the verse Jesus quotes on the night of his arrest (Matthew 26:31; Mark 14:27) — leaving a remnant refined like silver and tested like gold, who call and are answered: "It is my people... The LORD is my God" (13:9). Chapter 14 gathers everything into one day of the LORD: the nations against Jerusalem, the LORD going out to fight, his feet standing on the Mount of Olives as it splits, living waters flowing from the city in summer and winter, and the declaration the whole book leans toward: "The LORD will be King over all the earth. In that day the LORD will be one, and his name one" (14:9). The survivors of the nations come up year by year to worship the King and keep the feast of booths, and holiness floods the ordinary — "HOLY TO THE LORD" is inscribed even on the bells of the horses, and every cooking pot in Jerusalem is as holy as a temple bowl (14:16–21).

Zechariah is the Minor Prophet the New Testament quotes most, especially in the passion narratives; those citations are noted in this document as later interpretive frames, while the summaries and tags stay within what the book itself says. Read on its own terms, the book holds two things together that the rebuilding community needed and readers still do: God's Spirit is enough for today's unfinished work, and God's future — cleansing, kingship, and a world made holy — is larger than anything the present ruins suggest.

## Zechariah 1
**Summary:** Zechariah opens with a dated sermon: in Darius's second year, the LORD — "very displeased with your fathers" — offers the returned exiles a fresh start: "'Return to me,' says the LORD of Armies, 'and I will return to you'" (1:1–3). The fathers ignored the former prophets and were overtaken by God's words — "Your fathers, where are they? And the prophets, do they live forever?" (1:4–6). Three months later comes a vision in the night: a rider on a red horse stands among myrtle trees with patrolling horses behind him, and the patrol reports that "all the earth is at rest and in peace" — while Jerusalem still lies under seventy years of indignation (1:7–12). The LORD answers the angel's "how long?" with "kind and comforting words": he is jealous for Jerusalem "with a great jealousy," angry with the nations at ease, and has returned to Jerusalem with mercy — his house will be rebuilt, and his cities "will again overflow with prosperity" (1:13–17). A second vision shows four horns — the powers that scattered Judah, Israel, and Jerusalem — and four craftsmen who have come to cast them down (1:18–21).

## Zechariah 2
**Summary:** A man goes out with a measuring line to size up Jerusalem, and an angel runs to stop the survey: the city will be "inhabited as villages without walls," too full of people and livestock to fence in, "'For I,' says the LORD, 'will be to her a wall of fire around it, and I will be the glory in the middle of her'" (2:1–5). Then God calls his people still settled in Babylon to escape — "Come, Zion! Escape, you who dwell with the daughter of Babylon" — because whoever touches them "touches the apple of his eye," and the nations that plundered them will be plundered (2:6–9). Zion is told to sing: "behold, I come and I will dwell within you," and "Many nations shall join themselves to the LORD in that day, and shall be my people" (2:10–12). The chapter ends in hushed awe: "Be silent, all flesh, before the LORD; for he has roused himself from his holy habitation!" (2:13).

## Zechariah 3
**Summary:** Zechariah is shown a courtroom: Joshua the high priest stands before the LORD's angel in filthy garments, with Satan at his right hand "to be his adversary" — and it is Satan, not Joshua, whom God silences: "The LORD rebuke you, Satan!... Isn't this a burning stick plucked out of the fire?" (3:1–2). At a word the filthy garments are stripped away — "Behold, I have caused your iniquity to pass from you, and I will clothe you with rich clothing" — and a clean turban is set on his head (3:3–5). Joshua is recommissioned with a condition and a promise: walk in my ways and you will judge my house and have a place among those who stand here (3:6–7). Then the vision widens past Joshua: he and his fellow priests are "men who are a sign," for God will bring his servant "the Branch"; a stone with seven eyes is set before Joshua, and God will "remove the iniquity of that land in one day," until every man invites his neighbor "under the vine and under the fig tree" (3:8–10). (Christian readers have long seen in the Branch a promise pointing to the Messiah — a later interpretive frame; within the chapter the Branch is God's coming servant, unnamed.)

## Zechariah 4
**Summary:** The angel wakes Zechariah "as a man who is wakened out of his sleep" and shows him a lamp stand all of gold with its bowl on top — seven lamps, with seven pipes to each — and an olive tree on either side (4:1–3). The meaning is a word for Zerubbabel, the governor rebuilding the temple: "'Not by might, nor by power, but by my Spirit,' says the LORD of Armies" (4:6). The mountain of difficulty before him will become a plain, and he will bring out the capstone with shouts of "Grace, grace, to it!" (4:7). The hands that laid this foundation will also finish it — "who despises the day of small things?" — for the seven lamps are the LORD's eyes ranging through the whole earth (4:8–10). Asked about the two olive trees pouring golden oil, the angel answers: "These are the two anointed ones who stand by the Lord of the whole earth" (4:11–14) — in the chapter's own setting, the two anointed offices leading the restoration, priest and governor.

## Zechariah 5
**Summary:** Two visions carry sin out of the restored community. First a flying scroll, twenty cubits by ten, goes out as "the curse... over the surface of the whole land": everyone who steals and everyone who swears falsely by God's name will be cut off, and the curse will enter the offender's own house and "destroy it with its timber and its stones" (5:1–4). Then an ephah basket — a grain measure — appears with a woman sitting inside; the angel names her: "This is Wickedness." She is thrown down into the basket, a lead weight is clapped over its mouth, and two winged women carry the basket away to the land of Shinar — Babylonia — where a house will be built for it, "in her own place" (5:5–11). Together the visions insist that the rebuilt community cannot house both God's glory and settled wickedness: sin is judged where it hides, and evil is removed from the land altogether.

## Zechariah 6
**Summary:** The night visions close where they began — with patrols going out into all the earth. Four chariots drawn by red, black, white, and dappled horses come out from between two mountains of bronze; they are "the four winds of the sky, which go out from standing before the Lord of all the earth" (6:1–5). The black and white teams head to the north country — Babylon's direction — and God announces that they "have quieted my spirit in the north country" (6:6–8). Then vision gives way to a commanded sign-act: from returned exiles' silver and gold Zechariah is to make crowns and set them on the head of Joshua the high priest, with this word: "Behold, the man whose name is the Branch!... He will build the LORD's temple. He will bear the glory, and will sit and rule on his throne. He will be a priest on his throne. The counsel of peace will be between them both" (6:9–13). The crowns stay in the temple as a memorial, and far-off people will come and build — "if you will diligently obey the LORD your God's voice" (6:14–15). (Crowning a priest — kingship and priesthood joined in one figure — points beyond Joshua; Christian readers have long connected the priest-king Branch to Jesus, a later interpretive frame the chapter itself leaves open.)

## Zechariah 7
**Summary:** "In the fourth year of King Darius" — two years after the night visions — a delegation from Bethel asks the priests and prophets whether to keep the fast of the fifth month — the mourning for the temple's destruction — "as I have done these so many years" (7:1–3). God's answer begins with a question that turns the audit around: "When you fasted and mourned in the fifth and in the seventh month for these seventy years, did you at all fast to me, really to me?" — their fasting and their feasting alike had been for themselves (7:4–7). What God had always asked was different: "Execute true judgment, and show kindness and compassion every man to his brother. Don't oppress the widow, the fatherless, the foreigner, nor the poor; and let none of you devise evil against his brother in your heart" (7:8–10). The fathers refused — they "made their hearts as hard as flint" against the law and the words the LORD sent by his Spirit through the former prophets — so wrath came: as he called and they refused to listen, so they called and he would not listen, and the pleasant land was left desolate behind the whirlwind (7:11–14).

## Zechariah 8
**Summary:** Again and again, "The LORD of Armies says" introduces a cascade of promises for Zion. God is jealous for her "with great jealousy"; he has returned to dwell in Jerusalem, which will be called "The City of Truth" — old men and old women in its streets again, and the streets "full of boys and girls playing" (8:1–5). If that seems marvelous to the remnant, "should it also be marvelous in my eyes?" — God will bring his people home: "They will be my people, and I will be their God, in truth and in righteousness" (8:6–8). So the builders should let their hands be strong: where there had been no wages and no peace, there will be "the seed of peace," fruitful vines and ground and dew, and the former curse will become a blessing — "Don't be afraid" (8:9–15). Their part is plain dealing: "speak every man the truth with his neighbor. Execute the judgment of truth and peace in your gates," no evil devised, no false oath loved, "for all these are things that I hate" (8:16–17). The fasting question from chapter 7 finally gets its answer — the fasts "shall be for the house of Judah joy, gladness, and cheerful feasts. Therefore love truth and peace" (8:18–19) — and the chapter closes with the nations pulled in: many peoples seeking the LORD in Jerusalem, ten men from every language grabbing the clothing of a Jew and saying, "We will go with you, for we have heard that God is with you" (8:20–23).

## Zechariah 9
**Summary:** A revelation sweeps down the coast: the LORD's word rests on Hadrach, Damascus, and Hamath, strips proud Tyre of her heaped-up silver, and passes through the Philistine cities, where Gaza's king perishes and the pride of the Philistines is cut off — yet even here a remnant is claimed "for our God," and the LORD encamps around his house so that "no oppressor will pass through them any more" (9:1–8). Against that march of armies comes a different kind of king: "Rejoice greatly, daughter of Zion!... Behold, your King comes to you! He is righteous, and having salvation; lowly, and riding on a donkey, even on a colt, the foal of a donkey" (9:9). Chariot, horse, and battle bow are cut off — "he will speak peace to the nations" — and his dominion reaches "from sea to sea" (9:10). (The Gospels cite 9:9 when Jesus enters Jerusalem on a donkey — Matthew 21:4–5; John 12:14–15 — a later interpretive frame; within the oracle he is Zion's promised king.) Because of "the blood of your covenant," prisoners are freed from the waterless pit and summoned home: "Turn to the stronghold, you prisoners of hope! Even today I declare that I will restore double to you" (9:11–12). Judah becomes God's bow and Ephraim its arrow against Greece, and the closing stanza belongs to the LORD himself — seen over his people, defending them, and saving them "in that day as the flock of his people," jewels of a crown in his land, under the exclamation "For how great is his goodness, and how great is his beauty!" (9:13–17).

## Zechariah 10
**Summary:** The chapter opens by redirecting the community's dependence: "Ask of the LORD rain in the spring time" — it is the LORD who makes storm clouds and gives showers for the plants — for the teraphim (household idols) "have spoken vanity," diviners see lies and comfort in vain, and so the people wander "like sheep... oppressed, because there is no shepherd" (10:1–2). God's anger burns against those failed shepherds, and he himself visits his flock, the house of Judah, making them like his majestic war horse; from Judah will come "the cornerstone," the tent peg, the battle bow — "every ruler together" (10:3–5). (Elsewhere in scripture "cornerstone" becomes a title for the Messiah — Psalm 118:22 and its New Testament citations — a reading many Christians hear here too; in this verse's own setting it stands first in a list of figures for the leaders God will raise from Judah.) Then comes a cascade of restoration promises: "I will strengthen the house of Judah, and I will save the house of Joseph... They will be as though I had not cast them off, for I am the LORD their God, and I will hear them" (10:6). Ephraim will rejoice, children and all; God will signal and gather the redeemed, sow them among the peoples — "They will remember me in far countries" — and bring them back from Egypt and Assyria into a land too small to hold them — a second exodus in which the sea of affliction is struck and the Nile's depths dry up (10:7–12).
**Tags:** `gods-provision` — rain sought from its true giver: "Ask of the LORD rain in the spring time, the LORD who makes storm clouds, and he gives rain showers to everyone for the plants in the field," set against the vanity of teraphim and diviners (10:1–2); `gods-faithfulness` — mercy that undoes the casting-off: "I will bring them back, for I have mercy on them. They will be as though I had not cast them off, for I am the LORD their God, and I will hear them," and "I have redeemed them" (10:6, 8); `restoration-of-israel` — the cascade of regathering promises: "I will strengthen the house of Judah, and I will save the house of Joseph... ## Zechariah 11
**Summary:** The chapter opens with a taunt-song of felled trees — Lebanon's cedars, Bashan's oaks, the ruined pride of the Jordan — and wailing shepherds whose glory is destroyed (11:1–3). Then God assigns Zechariah a bitter role-play: "Feed the flock of slaughter," a people whose buyers slaughter them unpunished, whose sellers say "Blessed be the LORD, for I am rich," and whose own shepherds don't pity them (11:4–6). He shepherds "especially the oppressed of the flock" with two staffs, Favor and Union, but the relationship sours — "my soul was weary of them, and their soul also loathed me" — until he breaks Favor, revoking the covenant of protection, and asks for his severance: "So they weighed for my wages thirty pieces of silver" (11:7–12). God's verdict on the sum drips with irony — "Throw it to the potter—the handsome price that I was valued at by them!" — and the silver is thrown to the potter in the LORD's house; then the second staff, Union, is broken between Judah and Israel (11:13–14). (Matthew's Gospel hears this scene in Judas's thirty pieces of silver, thrown down in the temple and used for the potter's field — Matthew 26:15; 27:9–10 — a later interpretive frame; in the chapter the insult is the flock's contempt for the shepherd God gave them.) Finally the prophet must play the opposite part: a foolish shepherd who abandons the scattered, doesn't heal the broken, and eats the flock — and over him falls the closing woe: "Woe to the worthless shepherd who leaves the flock!" (11:15–17).
**Tags:** `divine-judgment` — the withdrawal of pity as sentence: "I will no more pity the inhabitants of the land... I will deliver every one of the men into his neighbor's hand and into the hand of his king... and out of their hand I will not deliver them" (11:6, with the broken staffs, 11:10, 14, and the woe on the worthless shepherd, 11:17); `shepherds-and-the-flock` — the theme's darkest OT chapter: "Feed the flock of slaughter" (11:4), the shepherding of "especially the oppressed of the flock" with the staffs Favor and Union (11:7), and the closing "Woe to the worthless shepherd who leaves the flock!" (11:17); `justice-and-oppression` — the flock as an oppressed people: buyers who slaughter them unpunished, sellers who say "Blessed be the LORD, for I am rich," and their own shepherds without pity (11:4–6); `messianic-prophecy` — the thirty-silver scene: "So they weighed for my wages thirty pieces of silver" (11:12), thrown to the potter in the LORD's house (11:13) — heard by Matthew's Gospel in Judas's silver (Matthew 26:15; 27:9–10), recorded as the NT's attributed reading per this doc's prose signpost. (The 2026-08-23 note "Only one honest tag from the current vocabulary" described the 131-id vocabulary; the three tags following `divine-judgment` were added 2026-08-25 from the adopted display-tag vocabulary — see Decisions 21.)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings

- **11:1–9 — The Doomed Flock** (title: BSB) — After a taunt over fallen cedars and wailing shepherds, Zechariah is sent to shepherd a flock marked for slaughter, and finally refuses to keep feeding a flock that loathes him.
- **11:10–17 — Thirty Pieces of Silver** (title: BSB) — The staffs Favor and Union are broken, the shepherd's wages — thirty pieces of silver — are thrown to the potter in the LORD's house, and the prophet must then act out the worthless shepherd who devours the flock.

## Zechariah 12
**Summary:** A new oracle opens with the LORD's full credentials — he stretches out the heavens, lays the earth's foundation, and "forms the spirit of man within him" (12:1). "In that day" Jerusalem becomes "a cup of reeling" and "a burdensome stone for all the peoples": every nation that burdens itself with her is wounded, while God watches over Judah, striking the attackers' horses with terror and blindness (12:2–4). The chieftains of Judah become like a pan of fire among wood; the LORD saves "the tents of Judah first," so that no one's glory outranks another's, and "In that day the LORD will defend the inhabitants of Jerusalem" — the feeblest like David, and David's house "like God, like the LORD's angel before them" — while the nations that came against her are destroyed (12:5–9). Then the tone turns entirely: God pours on David's house and Jerusalem "the spirit of grace and of supplication," and "They will look to me whom they have pierced; and they shall mourn for him as one mourns for his only son, and will grieve bitterly for him as one grieves for his firstborn" (12:10). (John's Gospel cites this line at the crucifixion — John 19:37 — and Revelation 1:7 echoes it; a later interpretive frame for a verse whose grief the chapter leaves unexplained.) The mourning spreads like the mourning of Hadadrimmon in the valley of Megiddo — every family apart, and their wives apart (12:11–14).
**Tags:** `gods-protection` — "In that day the LORD will defend the inhabitants of Jerusalem," the feeble made like David, every besieging nation broken on the burdensome stone (12:2–4, 8–9); `repentance` — grief for wrong poured out by God himself: "the spirit of grace and of supplication," so that "they shall mourn for him as one mourns for his only son," family by family (12:10–14); `divine-judgment` — "I will seek to destroy all the nations that come against Jerusalem," with horses struck with terror and riders with madness (12:4, 9); `day-of-the-lord` — the day-complex in the chapter's own "In that day" refrain (the phrase "day of the LORD" itself does not appear here): Jerusalem "a cup of reeling" and "a burdensome stone for all the peoples," every nation that lifts it wounded, and the attackers destroyed (12:2–9); `messianic-prophecy` — the pierced-one oracle: "They will look to me whom they have pierced; and they shall mourn for him as one mourns for his only son" (12:10) — cited at the crucifixion (John 19:37) and echoed in Revelation 1:7, recorded as the NT's attributed reading per this doc's prose signpost
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings
**Sections:**
- **12:1–9 — The Coming Deliverance of Jerusalem** (title: BSB) — Jerusalem becomes a cup of reeling and a burdensome stone; God defends the city, makes its feeblest like David, and destroys the nations that besiege it.
- **12:10–14 — Mourning the One They Pierced** (title: BSB) — God pours out a spirit of grace and supplication, and the land mourns the pierced one bitterly, as for an only son — every family apart.

## Zechariah 13
**Summary:** The mourning of chapter 12 opens into cleansing: "In that day there will be a fountain opened to David's house and to the inhabitants of Jerusalem, for sin and for uncleanness" (13:1). With the cleansing comes a purge: the names of the idols are cut off and remembered no more, and lying prophecy passes from the land — so completely that a man's own parents would tell him "You must die, because you speak lies in the LORD's name" — and stab him for prophesying — and the prophets themselves grow ashamed of their visions, disclaiming the trade ("I am no prophet, I am a tiller of the ground") and explaining away their scars as wounds received "in the house of my friends" (13:2–6). Then the sword of the LORD awakes against a very different figure — "my shepherd... the man who is close to me": "Strike the shepherd, and the sheep will be scattered" (13:7). (Jesus quotes this verse of himself on the night of his arrest, as the disciples scatter — Matthew 26:31; Mark 14:27 — a later interpretive frame; Christian readers likewise hear 13:1's fountain answered at the cross.) In the whole land two thirds are cut off, and the remaining third is brought through fire — "will refine them as silver is refined, and will test them like gold is tested" — until the covenant exchange is spoken again: "They will call on my name, and I will hear them. I will say, 'It is my people;' and they will say, 'The LORD is my God'" (13:8–9).
**Tags:** `forgiveness-of-sins` — cleansing provided at the source: "a fountain opened to David's house and to the inhabitants of Jerusalem, for sin and for uncleanness" (13:1); `testing` — the remnant refined and proven: "I will bring the third part into the fire, and will refine them as silver is refined, and will test them like gold is tested" (13:9); `divine-judgment` — idols cut off, lying prophets silenced, and "two parts in it will be cut off and die" (13:2–3, 8); `false-prophets` — lying prophecy purged so completely that a man's own parents would tell him "You must die, because you speak lies in the LORD's name" (13:3), and the prophets disclaim the trade — "I am no prophet, I am a tiller of the ground" (13:5) — explaining their scars as wounds received "in the house of my friends" (13:6); `idolatry` — the purge's other half, under the BSB heading "An End to Idolatry": the names of the idols cut off out of the land, remembered no more (13:2); `messianic-prophecy` — the struck-shepherd word: "Strike the shepherd, and the sheep will be scattered" (13:7) — quoted by Jesus of himself on the night of his arrest (Matthew 26:31; Mark 14:27), recorded as the NT's attributed reading per this doc's prose signpost; `remnant` — the preserved third as the surviving people: two thirds cut off, but the third brought through fire, refined "as silver is refined," until the covenant exchange is spoken again — "It is my people... ## Zechariah 14
**Summary:** The book ends on a single panorama: "Behold, a day of the LORD comes." All nations gather against Jerusalem; the city is taken, "the houses rifled, and the women ravished" — that is, raped — and half of the city goes out into captivity — and then the LORD himself goes out to fight, "as when he fought in the day of battle" (14:1–3). His feet stand on the Mount of Olives, which splits east to west into a great valley of escape, "and all the holy ones" come with him (14:4–5). The day is "a unique day which is known to the LORD—not day, and not night," but at evening time there is light; living waters flow from Jerusalem to the eastern and western seas, summer and winter alike; and the book's summit is reached: "The LORD will be King over all the earth. In that day the LORD will be one, and his name one" (14:6–9). Jerusalem is raised and settled safely, "and there will be no more curse," while a wasting plague and mutual panic fall on the armies that fought against her (14:10–15). Then the survivors of those very nations come up "from year to year to worship the King, the LORD of Armies, and to keep the feast of booths" — the harvest festival of shelters — with rain withheld from any family that will not come (14:16–19). And holiness overflows its old boundaries: "HOLY TO THE LORD" is inscribed on the bells of the horses, every pot in Jerusalem and Judah is as holy as an altar bowl, and "there will no longer be a Canaanite in the house of the LORD of Armies" (14:20–21). (Christian teaching has long connected this day to the return of Christ, and readers differ over how literally to map its details; this summary keeps to what the vision shows: God arriving as King, rescuing, and making the whole of life holy.)
**Tags:** `divine-judgment` — the day itself: nations gathered to battle, then struck — "their flesh will consume away while they stand on their feet," with "a great panic from the LORD" turning hand against hand (14:2–3, 12–15); `worship` — the nations' survivors going up "from year to year to worship the King, the LORD of Armies, and to keep the feast of booths," with rain withheld from families that do not go up to worship (14:16–19); `holiness` — holiness extended to the ordinary: "HOLY TO THE LORD" on the horses' bells, and "every pot in Jerusalem and in Judah will be holy to the LORD of Armies" (14:20–21); `nations-and-peoples` — "everyone who is left of all the nations that came against Jerusalem" joining "all the families of the earth" in the pilgrimage to the King (14:16–17); `day-of-the-lord` — the phrase's own closing panorama: "Behold, a day of the LORD comes" (14:1) — all nations gathered against Jerusalem, the LORD going out to fight, "a unique day which is known to the LORD" (14:7), ending at the summit "The LORD will be King over all the earth" (14:9)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings
**Sections:**
- **14:1–15 — The Destroyers of Jerusalem Destroyed** (title: BSB) — On the day of the LORD the nations take the city, but the LORD goes out to fight: the Mount of Olives splits, living waters flow, the LORD becomes King over all the earth, and a plague breaks the armies that fought against Jerusalem.
- **14:16–21 — All Nations Will Worship the King** (title: BSB) — The nations' survivors come up year by year to worship the King and keep the feast of booths, and holiness spreads to horse bells and cooking pots — nothing in Jerusalem left common.

## Decisions record

Delegated judgment calls, each a reversible default Jesse can overturn:

1. **Subdivision set: chapters 1, 2, 5, 6, 9, 11, 12, 13, 14 subdivided; 3,
   4, 7, 8, 10 left whole.** Zechariah is structurally a multi-vision,
   multi-oracle book, and 9 of 14 chapters carry more than one BSB heading
   marking genuine divergence: ch. 1 (sermon vs. two distinct visions), ch. 2
   (vision vs. summons-and-promise), ch. 5 (two separate visions), ch. 6
   (vision vs. crowning sign-act), ch. 9 (judgment on the nations vs. the
   coming king vs. the LORD's rescue), ch. 11 (the doomed flock vs. the
   broken staffs/wages and foolish shepherd), ch. 12 (deliverance vs.
   mourning), ch. 13 (fountain-and-purge vs. struck shepherd), ch. 14
   (battle-and-theophany vs. worship-and-holiness aftermath). Chapters 3, 4,
   7, 8, and 10 each carry a single BSB heading, so subdivision is
   structurally unavailable and each reads as one unit anyway. All section
   titles are BSB verbatim; every range exactly partitions its chapter.
2. **Chapter 14 subdivided rather than held as one panorama.** The chapter
   is one "day of the LORD" sweep, and holding it whole was considered; the
   BSB boundary at 14:16 marks a genuine turn (from battle, theophany, and
   plague to annual worship and universal holiness), so the subdivision was
   kept. Reversible — collapsing to one unsubdivided block loses nothing
   factual.
3. **`christ-the-cornerstone` NOT applied to 10:4.** The id exists (PR #43)
   and 10:4 reads "From him will come the cornerstone". Declined after
   reading the yaml: the pack is by design a New Testament christology
   locator (anchors 1 Peter 2:4–7, Ephesians 2:19–22, Acts 4:11 — the
   living-stone/spiritual-house texts; the OT cornerstone verses it defers
   are Ps 118:22 / Isa 28:16, not Zech 10:4). In 10:4's own setting the
   cornerstone heads a list of four figures — cornerstone, tent peg, battle
   bow, "every ruler together" — for the leaders God will raise from Judah;
   tagging it as the christology concept would be exactly the contested
   later-revelation read-back the conventions forbid. Handled instead as a
   careful prose signpost in the chapter summary. Reversible.
4. **NT passion touchpoints are prose signposts only.** Zechariah is the
   most NT-quoted Minor Prophet, and each citation is noted in prose as a
   later interpretive frame with its NT reference — 9:9 (Matthew 21:4–5;
   John 12:14–15), 11:12–13 (Matthew 26:15; 27:9–10), 12:10 (John 19:37;
   Revelation 1:7), 13:7 (Matthew 26:31; Mark 14:27) — plus lighter
   signposts for the Branch (3:8; 6:12), the fountain (13:1), and ch. 14's
   day (return-of-Christ readings). No tag asserts any of them; no
   `incarnation`, `deity-of-christ`, `the-cross`, `salvation`, or
   `second-coming` tag appears anywhere in this doc, all being
   later-revelation read-backs on this book.
5. **Holy-Spirit-family id declined for 4:6.** "Not by might, nor by power,
   but by my Spirit" is the chapter's center, but the only Spirit id,
   `holy-spirit-the-comforter`, is by design the John 14–16 paraclete
   register (Comforter/Counselor/Advocate), not Spirit-empowerment for work.
   Tagging it would misfile the verse. The verse is instead appended (with a
   register caveat) to the sibling `outpouring-of-the-spirit` gap row and
   captured as a motif candidate. Reversible.
6. **`dreams-and-visions` tagged on all six vision chapters (1–6).** The
   pack is a descriptive locator for God-speaks-by-vision passages, and each
   of chapters 1–6 is literally a night-vision narrative with an
   interpreting angel ("What are these, my lord?"). Tagging all six is
   honest but repetitive; it is listed last in each chapter's tag line so
   the thematic tags lead. Reversible en bloc if judged noise.
7. **`repentance` on chapter 12.** The mourning of 12:10–14 is grief poured
   out by "the spirit of grace and of supplication" over the pierced one —
   read here, with standard consensus, as penitential mourning (it opens
   directly into 13:1's fountain "for sin"). The chapter never uses
   return/turn vocabulary, so this is a substance call, not a word match.
   `pastoral-grief-and-loss` was considered and declined: that id is the
   bereavement-crisis register, and this mourning is a simile ("as one
   mourns for his only son"), not a death in the narrative. Reversible.
8. **`refuge-in-trouble` on chapter 9.** Anchored on 9:11–12: prisoners in
   the waterless pit told "Turn to the stronghold, you prisoners of hope!"
   — the distress-shelter substance with the pack's own "stronghold"
   vocabulary. The stronghold is Zion/God's protection rather than a
   personal-crisis prayer, so the register is national; substance judged
   honestly present. Reversible.
9. **`gods-protection` on chapters 2, 9, and 12.** The pack's lexicon leans
   to the personal protection-prayer register (Psalm 91), but its substance
   — God keeping his people from harm — is exactly what "a wall of fire
   around it" (2:5), "I will encamp around my house" (9:8), and "the LORD
   will defend the inhabitants of Jerusalem" (12:8) depict. Same reversible
   call the Micah doc made for Micah 5.
10. **`do-not-lose-heart` on chapter 4.** The id's register is
    NT discouragement/perseverance, but the vision's entire aim is a
    discouraged builder told the mountain will flatten, his hands will
    finish the work, and the day of small things is not to be despised
    (4:7–10). Substance judged honestly present. Reversible.
11. **`god-of-all-comfort` on chapter 1.** Anchored on the text's own
    comfort vocabulary: "kind and comforting words" (1:13) and "the LORD
    will again comfort Zion" (1:17), spoken over a community under seventy
    years of indignation. Reversible.
12. **`resisting-the-devil` NOT tagged on chapter 3** — the exact
    Genesis-3-type trap the conventions warn about, inverted: Satan appears
    by name, but the chapter depicts the LORD rebuking the accuser while
    Joshua stands silent; no one models the believer's resisting. (The
    searcher's "satan" query still routes there via that pack's own lexicon;
    no gap.) Likewise **`grace-not-earned` NOT tagged on chapter 3**: the
    cleansing is unearned in the narrative, but the id is the
    Ephesians-2:8-9 teaching register and tagging it would read the formula
    back into the vision; the free-cleansing point is carried in prose.
13. **Declined borderline tags, in brief:** `salvation` on 9:9 ("having
    salvation" — the id is the how-can-I-be-saved NT register);
    `humble-exaltation` on 9:9 (the king is "lowly," but the chapter does
    not teach the humbled-then-exalted pattern for people);
    `joy-in-the-lord` on 8:19 / 9:9 / 10:7 (real rejoicing vocabulary,
