# James — book summary

## Short

James is the New Testament’s book of practical wisdom, written by "James, a servant of God and of the Lord Jesus Christ" to "the twelve tribes which are in the Dispersion" — believers scattered far from home. Its test throughout is whether faith is real, and its measure is what faith does: joy and endurance under trial, doing the word and not only hearing it, honoring the poor believer as gladly as the rich visitor, bridling the tongue, choosing the peaceable wisdom that comes from above, humbling plans and quarrels before God, and praying with patience through suffering and sickness. Its hinge sentence is blunt: "faith apart from works is dead."

## Full

James opens with a one-line greeting and then wastes no time: "Count it all joy, my brothers, when you fall into various temptations" — the WEB’s word here for what many Bibles call "trials" — "knowing that the testing of your faith produces endurance." That opening sets the letter’s method. James writes to scattered believers in short, sharp runs of teaching, more like the book of Proverbs than like Paul’s step-by-step arguments, and nearly every paragraph ends at the same door: real faith shows.

The letter keeps returning to a handful of tests. There is the test of trouble: trials produce endurance, God gives wisdom "liberally and without reproach" to those who ask in faith, and the one who endures temptation "will receive the crown of life." There is the test of the ears and hands: "be doers of the word, and not only hearers, deluding your own selves" — the hearer-only is like a man who studies his face in a mirror and immediately forgets it. There is the test of the meeting room: seating the gold-ringed visitor up front while telling the poor man to stand violates the "royal law" — "You shall love your neighbor as yourself" — and forgets that God chose "those who are poor in this world to be rich in faith." There is the test of the tongue, a small fire that can burn a forest, a rudder that steers the whole ship, which "nobody can tame." And there is the test of wisdom itself: bitter jealousy and selfish ambition are an "earthly, sensual, and demonic" counterfeit, while "the wisdom that is from above is first pure, then peaceful, gentle, reasonable, full of mercy and good fruits."

At the letter’s center stands its most famous argument: what good is it if a man says he has faith but has no works? Faith that leaves a shivering brother or sister with only "Go in peace. Be warmed and filled" is "dead in itself"; even "The demons also believe—and shudder." James’s own summary is that faith and works go together the way a body and its spirit do — "faith apart from works is dead" — and he points to Abraham and Rahab as people whose acts showed their faith to be alive.

The closing chapters turn outward and forward. Quarrels come from cravings; friendship with the world is hostility toward God; but "he gives more grace" to the lowly, so "Resist the devil, and he will flee from you. Draw near to God, and he will draw near to you." Merchants planning next year’s profits are reminded that life is "a vapor" and taught to say, "If the Lord wills." Rich oppressors who have held back their laborers’ wages by fraud are told those wages "cry out," and the cries of those who reaped have "entered into the ears of the Lord of Armies" — the WEB’s rendering of the title often printed "Lord of hosts." And the scattered church is told to wait like a farmer for "the coming of the Lord," to pray over the sick with anointing oil and confession, to remember that "The insistent prayer of a righteous person is powerfully effective," and to go after the wanderer — for turning a sinner back "will save a soul from death and will cover a multitude of sins."

# The five chapters

## James 1
**Summary:** After a one-verse greeting to "the twelve tribes which are in the Dispersion," James tells believers to "Count it all joy" when they fall into "various temptations," because tested faith produces endurance, and God gives wisdom generously to anyone who asks in faith without doubting. He then distinguishes trial from temptation: God tests but "he himself tempts no one"; temptation is dragged up from a person’s own desire, and its offspring is sin and death, while "Every good gift and every perfect gift is from above, coming down from the Father of lights," who gave us new birth by the word of truth. The response that word deserves is the chapter’s closing demand: be "doers of the word, and not only hearers, deluding your own selves," bridle the tongue, and practice the religion God calls pure — visiting the fatherless and widows in their affliction and staying unstained by the world.
**Tags:** `testing` — "the testing of your faith produces endurance" (1:2–3, 12); `obedience-to-the-word` — "be doers of the word, and not only hearers, deluding your own selves" (1:22–25); `wisdom-from-god` — "if any of you lacks wisdom, let him ask of God, who gives to all liberally and without reproach" (1:5); `doubt` — "he who doubts is like a wave of the sea, driven by the wind and tossed," the "double-minded man, unstable in all his ways" (1:6–8); `remembered-joy-in-trials` — the letter’s own joy-in-trials charge: "Count it all joy, my brothers, when you fall into various temptations" (1:2–4); `self-deception` — hearers-only are "deluding your own selves," and the man who doesn’t bridle his tongue "deceives his heart" (1:22, 26); `temptation` — the letter’s own doctrine of temptation itself: God tests but "he himself tempts no one" — temptation is dragged up from a person’s own desire, and its offspring is sin and death (1:2, 12–15); `care-for-widows` — the definition verse: "Pure religion and undefiled" before God is to visit "the fatherless and widows" in their affliction (1:27)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings
**Sections:**
- **1:1–1 — A Greeting from James** (title: BSB) — James, "a servant of God and of the Lord Jesus Christ," greets the twelve tribes scattered in the Dispersion.
- **1:2–12 — Rejoicing in Trials** (title: BSB) — Trials are to be met with joy because tested faith produces endurance; God gives wisdom to those who ask in faith, poor and rich alike are leveled before him, and the one who endures receives the crown of life.
- **1:13–18 — Good and Perfect Gifts** (title: BSB) — No one may say "I am tempted by God": temptation is conceived in one’s own desire and grows into sin and death, while the unchanging Father of lights gives only good gifts — above all, new birth by the word of truth.
- **1:19–27 — Hearing and Doing** (title: BSB) — Swift hearing, slow speech, and slow anger prepare a person to receive the implanted word; doing that word — not just hearing it — with a bridled tongue and care for the fatherless and widows, is the religion God counts pure.

## James 2
**Summary:** James forbids holding "the faith of our glorious Lord Jesus Christ with partiality": ushering a gold-ringed visitor to the best seat while a poor man in filthy clothing stands dishonors the very poor whom God chose "to be rich in faith and heirs of the Kingdom," breaks the royal law — "You shall love your neighbor as yourself" — and turns those who discriminate into "judges with evil thoughts." Stumbling at one point of the law makes a person guilty of all, so believers must speak and act as those judged by the law of freedom, remembering that "Mercy triumphs over judgment." He then presses his most famous question: what good is a faith that sends a hungry brother or sister away with words — "Go in peace. Be warmed and filled" — but no help? Such faith is "dead in itself"; even "The demons also believe—and shudder"; Abraham and Rahab show faith working through what they did, "For as the body apart from the spirit is dead, even so faith apart from works is dead."
**Tags:** `faith-and-works` — "faith, if it has no works, is dead in itself" and "faith apart from works is dead" (2:14–17, 26); `loving-others` — "the royal law according to the Scripture, “You shall love your neighbor as yourself,”" (2:8), against partiality toward the rich (2:1–9); `divine-judgment` — "So speak and so do as men who are to be judged by the law of freedom," for "judgment is without mercy to him who has shown no mercy" (2:12–13); `favoritism` — Scripture’s direct treatment of the sin: holding "the faith of our glorious Lord Jesus Christ with partiality," seating the gold-ringed visitor above the poor man and becoming "judges with evil thoughts" (2:1–9)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings
**Sections:**
- **2:1–13 — A Warning against Favoritism** (title: BSB) — Seating the rich visitor up front while the poor man stands is partiality — sin the law convicts; God chose the poor to be rich in faith, and the royal law of neighbor-love allows no such rankings.
- **2:14–26 — Faith and Works** (title: BSB) — A claimed faith that gives a needy brother nothing is dead; real faith shows itself by works, as Abraham’s offering of Isaac and Rahab’s sheltering of the messengers showed theirs.

## James 3
**Summary:** James warns that not many should become teachers, "knowing that we will receive heavier judgment," because everyone stumbles — and the tongue is where it shows. Tiny things steer huge ones — a bit turns a horse, a small rudder a great ship — and the tongue is a small member that "boasts great things": a fire that can set a forest ablaze, a "restless evil, full of deadly poison" that "nobody can tame." Its double-speech is the scandal: with the same mouth we bless God and curse people "made in the image of God," though no spring gives both fresh and bitter water. The chapter then contrasts two wisdoms: bitter jealousy and selfish ambition mark a wisdom that is "earthly, sensual, and demonic," while "the wisdom that is from above is first pure, then peaceful, gentle, reasonable, full of mercy and good fruits," sowing righteousness in peace.
**Tags:** `taming-the-tongue` — "nobody can tame the tongue. It is a restless evil, full of deadly poison" (3:5–10); `wisdom-from-god` — "the wisdom that is from above is first pure, then peaceful, gentle, reasonable, full of mercy and good fruits" (3:13–17); `envy-and-jealousy` — "where jealousy and selfish ambition are, there is confusion and every evil deed" (3:14–16); `image-of-god` — the reason cursing people is unthinkable: "with it we curse men who are made in the image of God" (3:9–10)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings
**Sections:**
- **3:1–12 — Taming the Tongue** (title: BSB) — Teachers face stricter judgment because speech is where everyone stumbles: the tongue is a rudder, a spark, an untamable poison, blessing God and cursing his image-bearers from the same mouth.
- **3:13–18 — The Wisdom from Above** (title: BSB) — Real wisdom is shown "by his good conduct ... in gentleness"; jealous, ambitious wisdom is earthly and demonic, but the wisdom from above is pure, peaceful, and full of mercy.

## James 4
**Summary:** Wars and fightings among believers come from cravings at war within them: "You lust, and don’t have ... You don’t have, because you don’t ask. You ask, and don’t receive, because you ask with wrong motives." James calls his readers "adulterers and adulteresses" because "friendship with the world is hostility toward God" — yet "he gives more grace," quoting "God resists the proud, but gives grace to the humble." The remedy is a string of commands: be subject to God, "Resist the devil, and he will flee from you," "Draw near to God, and he will draw near to you," cleanse, purify, mourn over sin, and "Humble yourselves in the sight of the Lord, and he will exalt you" — and stop speaking against and judging one another, since only one is the lawgiver, able to save and to destroy — so who are you to judge another? The chapter closes on presumptuous planners: you are "a vapor that appears for a little time and then vanishes away," so say "If the Lord wills, we will both live, and do this or that" — and remember that knowing the good and not doing it is sin.
**Tags:** `humble-exaltation` — "God resists the proud, but gives grace to the humble" and "Humble yourselves in the sight of the Lord, and he will exalt you" (4:6, 10); `resisting-the-devil` — "Resist the devil, and he will flee from you" (4:7); `love-not-the-world` — "friendship with the world is hostility toward God" (4:4); `prayer` — "You don’t have, because you don’t ask. You ask, and don’t receive, because you ask with wrong motives" (4:2–3); `asking-in-gods-will` — "you ought to say, “If the Lord wills, we will both live, and do this or that.”" (4:13–15), against asking "with wrong motives" (4:3); `stewardship-of-days` — "what is your life? For you are a vapor that appears for a little time and then vanishes away" (4:13–17); `judging-others` — the command given directly: "Don’t speak against one another, brothers", for "Only one is the lawgiver, who is able to save and to destroy. But who are you to judge another?" (4:11–12)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings
**Sections:**
- **4:1–6 — A Warning against Pride** (title: BSB) — Fights among believers rise from pleasures at war within: they don’t have because they don’t ask, and what they do ask for is refused because they ask with wrong motives. Friendship with the world is hostility toward God, but he gives more grace — grace for the humble.
- **4:7–12 — Drawing Near to God** (title: BSB) — A cascade of commands: submit to God, resist the devil, draw near to God, cleanse and purify, mourn over sin, humble yourself for God to exalt — and stop judging your brother, for only one is the lawgiver, able to save and to destroy.
- **4:13–17 — Do Not Boast about Tomorrow** (title: BSB) — Merchants confidently scheduling next year’s profits are reminded that life is a vapor; all planning belongs under "If the Lord wills," and boasting past that is evil.

## James 5
**Summary:** James opens on the rich with prophetic fire: "weep and howl for your miseries that are coming on you" — hoarded riches rot, defrauded laborers’ wages "cry out," and the cries of those who reaped have "entered into the ears of the Lord of Armies." To the suffering brothers he turns gentle: be patient like a farmer waiting for rain "until the coming of the Lord," for "the coming of the Lord is at hand" and "the judge stands at the door"; remember the prophets, and "the perseverance of Job," whose outcome showed the Lord "full of compassion and mercy." Above all, speech must be plain — "let your “yes” be “yes”, and your “no”, “no”" — and every circumstance turned to prayer: the suffering should pray, the cheerful sing praises, and the sick call the elders of the assembly (the WEB’s word here for the congregation) to pray and anoint with oil, for "the prayer of faith will heal him who is sick, and the Lord will raise him up," as Elijah’s praying shut and opened the sky. The letter ends with a rescue: whoever turns a sinner back from wandering "will save a soul from death and will cover a multitude of sins."
**Tags:** `pastoral-prayer-for-healing` — "the prayer of faith will heal him who is sick, and the Lord will raise him up," with elders, anointing oil, and confession (5:14–16); `prayer` — "The insistent prayer of a righteous person is powerfully effective," proved by Elijah (5:13–18); `second-coming` — "Be patient therefore, brothers, until the coming of the Lord ... the coming of the Lord is at hand" (5:7–8); `divine-judgment` — the defrauded laborers’ wages "cry out," the reapers’ cries have "entered into the ears of the Lord of Armies," and "the judge stands at the door" (5:1–6, 9); `do-not-lose-heart` — "Establish your hearts," with the patient farmer, the prophets, and "the perseverance of Job" as models (5:7–11); `justice-and-oppression` — the prophetic indictment of the defrauding rich: "weep and howl for your miseries that are coming on you," for the withheld wages "cry out" and the reapers’ cries have "entered into the ears of the Lord of Armies" (5:1–6); `oaths-and-vows` — the letter’s emphatic teaching against swearing: "let your “yes” be “yes”, and your “no”, “no”" (5:12)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings
**Sections:**
- **5:1–6 — A Warning to the Rich** (title: BSB) — Riches hoarded in the last days corrode into testimony against their owners; the Lord of Armies has heard the cries of the laborers defrauded of their wages. The rich have lived in luxury, and the indictment climaxes: "You have condemned and you have murdered the righteous one."
- **5:7–12 — Patience in Suffering** (title: BSB) — Like a farmer waiting for the early and late rain, believers wait for the Lord’s coming without grumbling, with the prophets and Job as models — and with speech so plain that no oath is needed.
- **5:13–18 — The Prayer of Faith** (title: BSB) — Suffering, cheerfulness, and sickness each get their answer — prayer, praise, and the elders’ anointing prayer of faith; confessed sin and insistent righteous prayer are powerfully effective, as Elijah’s was.
- **5:19–20 — Restoring a Sinner** (title: BSB) — Turning a wandering brother back from error saves a soul from death and covers a multitude of sins.

1. **Vocabulary scope.** All 131 ids in `ontology/concepts/*.yaml` on main @ b3f491d at thread start are treated as taggable per CONVENTIONS §5, including the 9 ids merged in PR #43 (`asking-in-gods-will`, `baptism`, `benediction`, `caring-for-aging-parents`, `christ-the-cornerstone`, `doubt`, `incarnation`, `justification-by-faith`, `trinity`) which the Genesis pilot predated. Reversible; flagged to the coordinator. **PR #43 ids used in this doc:** `doubt` (James 1), `asking-in-gods-will` (James 4). *2026-08-25: ratified by Jesse 2026-08-25 — no longer reversible-pending-review.*
2. **`justification-by-faith` NOT tagged on James 2.** The chapter repeatedly uses the word — "by works a man is justified, and not only by faith" (2:24) — but the concept’s substance is the believer’s justification by faith, and James 2’s teaching, in James’s own terms, is that claimed faith without works is dead. Tagging would route "justified by faith" searches to a chapter arguing a different point, and would in effect stage the Paul-vs-James pairing this document deliberately declines to stage or resolve. `faith-and-works` is the honest home, and the summary describes James’s argument charitably in his own terms. Reversible: Jesse can add the tag if he wants justification searches to surface James 2 as the classic companion text.
3. **`remembered-joy-in-trials` tagged on James 1.** The Genesis-era rule keeps `remembered-*` verse-memory concepts off OT narrative; James 1:2–4 is the classic joy-in-trials memory text and matches the concept’s label exactly, so the tag was judged maximally honest here. The concept’s actual fixture verse was not verifiable from the vocabulary snapshot — if it keys to a different passage (e.g. 1 Peter 4:13), reassign. Reversible.
4. **`doubt` on James 1:6–8.** Checked against the vocabulary entry (label "Doubt"): the chapter teaches directly about doubting in prayer — the concept’s substance, not a mere word match. Reversible.
5. **`pastoral-relapse-and-restoration` DROPPED from James 5:19–20 (coordinator ruling on pastoral-* register).** The draft tagged the passage for its restoration substance ("Rising after a fall") from the restorer’s side. Under the project-wide ruling that pastoral-* tags attach only where the passage’s own personal-crisis register genuinely matches, it fails: 5:19–20 is an exhortation to the community about the wanderer — "if any among you wanders from the truth and someone turns him back" — not the personal-crisis voice of the fallen person the pastoral concept serves. Reversible: Jesse can restore the tag with the draft’s original rationale.
6. **`do-not-lose-heart` on James 5:7–11.** The concept’s label wording is Pauline (2 Cor 4; Gal 6:9), but its substance — persevering without giving up while waiting on God — is exactly what 5:7–11 teaches ("Establish your hearts"; the farmer; Job). Flagged because the phrase itself is not in the chapter. Reversible.
7. **`stewardship-of-days` on James 4:13–17.** The concept ("Redeeming the time") is anchored in Eph 5:16/Ps 90; James 4:14’s "you are a vapor" plus the "If the Lord wills" discipline is the same numbering-your-days substance. Reversible.
8. **All five chapters subdivided.** James is aphoristic; every chapter carries genuinely divergent themes on BSB boundaries (e.g. ch 1: trials / temptation and gifts / hearing and doing; ch 4: pride / drawing near / presuming on tomorrow). This is a higher subdivision rate than Genesis (14 of 50), driven by the book’s form, not a loosened bar. The one-verse greeting section (1:1) is kept as its own section because the BSB gives it its own heading and merging it into 1:2–12 would blur the "Rejoicing in Trials" boundary. Reversible.
9. **Rich-and-poor passages kept strictly descriptive.** Per `docs/DOCTRINAL-BASIS.md` (prosperity-gospel exclusion): 1:9–11, 2:1–7, and 5:1–6 are summarized as what the text says — reversal, honor for the poor believer, judgment on fraud, self-indulgence, and the condemnation and murder of the righteous one — with no prosperity framing (blessing as formula or entitlement) and no class polemic beyond the text’s own words. 5:6’s charge is quoted in full in the 5:1–6 section description ("You have condemned and you have murdered the righteous one"; the verse’s closing clause, "He doesn’t resist you.", is not quoted), and "the righteous one" is left as the text has it, not identified with Christ (a reading some older commentators take). Reversible.
