# John — book summary (draft)

## Short

John is the Gospel that states its own purpose: “these are written that you may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and that believing you may have life in his name” (20:31). Where the other Gospels gather many short episodes, John builds long scenes around a handful of chosen signs — water become wine, a nobleman's dying son, a man sick thirty-eight years, five thousand fed, a walk on the sea, a man born blind, Lazarus called out of his tomb — and each sign opens into a claim about who Jesus is: bread of life, light of the world, good shepherd, resurrection and life. The stakes are total from the first lines, where the Word who “was God” “became flesh and lived among us,” and every audience in the book must decide between believing and refusing. The story climaxes at the cross — “It is finished!” — the empty tomb, and Thomas's confession, “My Lord and my God!”

## Full

John reads differently from Matthew, Mark, and Luke, and it means to. It opens not in Bethlehem but before creation: “In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God” — and that Word “became flesh and lived among us” (1:1, 14). Instead of parables and a gathering crowd of short scenes, John selects a few signs and lets each unfold into a long conversation or dispute about Jesus' identity. The narrator says openly that he has been selective, and why: the signs recorded here “are written that you may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and that believing you may have life in his name” (20:31). Believing, and the life that comes with it, is the book's single thread — the words “believe” and “eternal life” toll through the book.

The first half (chapters 1–12) is Jesus' public ministry, paced by Jewish feasts and punctuated by signs. Each sign presses the question of who he is, and each answer divides the audience: the wine at Cana reveals his glory; a healing at Bethesda on the Sabbath opens into the claim that the Son does whatever the Father does; bread for five thousand becomes “I am the bread of life”; a man born blind receives sight while the seeing go blind; Lazarus walks out of his tomb, and the council resolves that Jesus must die. Alongside the signs run the great “I am” sayings and encounters that have fed Christian imagination ever since — Nicodemus told he must be “born anew” (the WEB's wording where many readers know *born again*), the Samaritan woman offered living water, the woman caught in adultery sent away uncondemned. The deity claims are not a later gloss; they are the story's own pressure point: “before Abraham came into existence, I AM” (8:58) and “I and the Father are one” (10:30) both end with stones in his hearers' hands.

The second half (chapters 13–21) slows to hours. On his last evening Jesus washes his disciples' feet, gives the new commandment — “that you love one another. Just as I have loved you” (13:34) — and, in the long farewell of chapters 14–16, promises “another Counselor” (our WEB translation's name for the one often called the Helper or Comforter), the Spirit of truth, along with peace in the middle of trouble: “In the world you have trouble; but cheer up! I have overcome the world” (16:33). Chapter 17 is his longest recorded prayer, defining the life he gives: “This is eternal life, that they should know you, the only true God, and him whom you sent, Jesus Christ” (17:3). Then comes the arrest in the garden, Peter's denials, the trial before Pilate, and a crucifixion John presents as the Son of Man “lifted up” as Jesus had foretold — finished work, not defeat: “It is finished!” (19:30). The resurrection unfolds in recognitions — Mary Magdalene hearing her name, the disciples behind locked doors, Thomas moving from doubt to “My Lord and my God!” (20:28) — and the book closes on a lakeshore breakfast where the risen Jesus restores Peter: “Feed my lambs” (21:15).

Written, by long-standing tradition, by the disciple the book calls the one whom Jesus loved, John is the Gospel of witness: John the Baptist, the Scriptures, the Father, the works, and finally the evangelist himself all testify to who Jesus is. Its aim is not neutral information but a verdict — the same one it records from Peter at the end of the bread-of-life discourse: “Lord, to whom would we go? You have the words of eternal life” (6:68).

# The twenty-one chapters

## John 1
**Summary:** John begins before creation: the Word who was with God and was God, through whom all things were made, “became flesh and lived among us” — Jesus Christ, full of grace and truth. John the Baptist comes as a witness to this light, telling the questioners sent from Jerusalem that he is only a voice preparing the Lord's way, and then pointing at Jesus: “Behold, the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world!” He testifies that he saw the Spirit descend and remain on Jesus like a dove, and that this is the Son of God. Two of John's own disciples follow Jesus; Andrew brings his brother Simon, whom Jesus renames Peter, and Philip finds the skeptical Nathanael, who ends up confessing Jesus as Son of God and King of Israel. The book's key word is already sounding: to as many as received him, who believe in his name, he gave “the right to become God’s children.”

## John 2
**Summary:** At a wedding in Cana, when the wine runs out, Jesus — at his mother's prompting, though his hour “has not yet come” — has six stone purification pots filled with water and turns it into the feast's best wine. John calls this “this beginning of his signs”: it reveals Jesus' glory, and his disciples believe in him. At Passover in Jerusalem, Jesus drives the livestock sellers and money changers out of the temple with a whip of cords — “Don’t make my Father’s house a marketplace!” — and his disciples remember the scripture about zeal for God's house. Challenged for a sign of his authority, he answers, “Destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up,” speaking, John explains, of the temple of his body — words the disciples understand only after he is raised. Many in Jerusalem believe when they see his signs, but Jesus does not entrust himself to them, because he himself knows what is in man.

## John 3
**Summary:** Nicodemus, a Pharisee and ruler, comes to Jesus by night and hears that no one can see God's Kingdom without being “born anew” — a birth of water and Spirit that, like the wind, can be neither managed nor traced. Jesus points to Moses lifting up the serpent in the wilderness: so must the Son of Man be lifted up, that whoever believes in him may have eternal life. Then comes perhaps the Bible's best-known verse — “For God so loved the world, that he gave his only born Son, that whoever believes in him should not perish, but have eternal life” — with its double edge: God sent the Son to save the world, not to judge it, yet whoever loves darkness rather than the light stands judged already. Meanwhile John the Baptist, told that everyone is now going to Jesus, rejoices like the friend of a bridegroom: “He must increase, but I must decrease.” The chapter closes where it began, on the dividing line of belief: whoever believes in the Son has eternal life; whoever disobeys the Son will not see life.

## John 4
**Summary:** Traveling through Samaria, a tired Jesus asks a Samaritan woman at Jacob's well for a drink — across a boundary the narrator flags, since “Jews have no dealings with Samaritans” — and offers her living water “springing up to eternal life.” He tells her the truth about her five husbands, teaches that “the true worshipers will worship the Father in spirit and truth,” and identifies himself as the Messiah outright: “I am he, the one who speaks to you.” She leaves her water pot and brings out her city — “Come, see a man who told me everything that I have done” — and after two days with Jesus the Samaritans conclude for themselves that he is “indeed the Christ, the Savior of the world.” To disciples puzzled that he will not eat, Jesus says his food is to do the will of the one who sent him, pointing at fields already “white for harvest.” Back in Cana he performs his second Galilean sign, healing a nobleman's dying son at Capernaum from a distance — “Go your way. Your son lives.” — and the father believes, with his whole house.

## John 5
**Summary:** At a feast in Jerusalem, Jesus finds a man at the pool of Bethesda who has been sick for thirty-eight years, asks him, “Do you want to be made well?”, and heals him with a word: “Arise, take up your mat, and walk.” Because it is the Sabbath, the authorities confront first the healed man and then Jesus, whose answer — “My Father is still working, so I am working, too” — makes them seek his death all the more, “because he not only broke the Sabbath, but also called God his own Father, making himself equal with God.” Jesus responds with a sustained claim: the Son does only what he sees the Father doing, gives life as the Father gives it, and holds all judgment, “that all may honor the Son, even as they honor the Father.” Whoever hears his word and believes the one who sent him “has eternal life” and “has passed out of death into life,” and an hour is coming when all in the tombs will hear his voice and rise, some to life and some to judgment. He then calls his witnesses — John the Baptist, his own works, the Father who sent him, and the Scriptures that “testify about me” — and turns the charge around: Moses himself, in whom his accusers hope, wrote about him and will accuse them.

## John 6
**Summary:** By the Sea of Galilee at Passover season, Jesus feeds about five thousand men from a boy's five barley loaves and two fish, with twelve baskets left over — then withdraws alone to the mountain when the crowd moves to seize him and make him king. That night his disciples, rowing into a wind-tossed sea, see him walking on the water toward the boat and are afraid until he speaks: “It is I. Don’t be afraid.” When the crowd catches up at Capernaum, Jesus presses past their appetite — they seek him “because you ate of the loaves” — to the sign's meaning: “I am the bread of life. Whoever comes to me will not be hungry, and whoever believes in me will never be thirsty.” Everyone the Father gives him will come to him and never be cast out or lost, and the bread he gives “for the life of the world” is his flesh — eating that flesh and drinking that blood is his stark picture of receiving him for eternal life and resurrection at the last day. The saying proves too hard, and many disciples walk away; Peter answers for the twelve, “Lord, to whom would we go? You have the words of eternal life,” though Jesus notes that even among the twelve stands a devil.

## John 7
**Summary:** With the Feast of Booths near and the authorities in Judea seeking his life, Jesus' brothers — who do not believe in him — dare him to go public; he stays behind, then goes up privately, on his own timing. Mid-feast he teaches openly in the temple, defends his Sabbath healing, and answers the murmuring city: his teaching is not his own but comes from the one who sent him, and “if anyone desires to do his will, he will know about the teaching.” Jerusalem argues over him — good man or deceiver, prophet or Christ, and can the Christ come from Galilee? — yet no one lays a hand on him, “because his hour had not yet come.” On the feast's last and greatest day he stands and cries out, “If anyone is thirsty, let him come to me and drink!”, promising rivers of living water — which John explains as the Spirit that believers were about to receive. Even the officers sent to arrest him return empty-handed — “No man ever spoke like this man!” — and Nicodemus' plea that the law requires a hearing earns only his colleagues' scorn.

## John 8
**Summary:** At dawn in the temple, the scribes and Pharisees test Jesus with a woman caught in adultery; he stoops to write on the ground, then answers, “He who is without sin among you, let him throw the first stone at her” — and when her accusers withdraw one by one, convicted by conscience, he sends her away uncondemned: “Neither do I condemn you. Go your way. From now on, sin no more.” Teaching on, Jesus declares, “I am the light of the world,” promising the light of life to all who follow him, and meets the challenge to his self-testimony with a second witness: the Father who sent him. To those who believe he offers a demanding freedom — remaining in his word means knowing the truth, “and the truth will make you free” — freedom not from Rome but from slavery to sin, which only the Son can grant. ## John 9

## John 19

## “The Jews” in John — doc-wide handling

Consolidated from both drafters' individual records into one policy. John uses the WEB phrase “the Jews” far more often than the other Gospels, and in more than one sense. In most scenes the context shows it denotes the Jerusalem/Judean authorities opposing Jesus: 1:19 — “the Jews sent priests and Levites”; 7:13 — the crowds, themselves Jewish, fear “the Jews”; 9:22 — “the Jews had already agreed” on synagogue expulsion, i.e. those with power to expel; 18:28–19:16 — those who deliver Jesus to Pilate, where the text itself specifies “the chief priests and the officers” (19:6) and “Your own nation and the chief priests delivered you to me” (18:35). Sometimes it denotes the broader Judean crowd or public (11:19, 31, 36, 45; 12:9, 11 — mourners, and many coming to believe). Doc-wide policy, applied identically in both halves: the summaries render the referent the context indicates — *the authorities*, *the leaders*, "the Jewish leaders," or the groups the chapter itself names (chief priests, Pharisees) — and use neutral wording (*mourners*, *the crowd*) where the phrase denotes ordinary people; the WEB's own phrase is never altered inside quotation marks, and no wording the text doesn't carry is put into anyone's mouth. Spot-checked mechanically at assembly across all 21 chapters: outside quotation marks the bare phrase "the Jews" appears nowhere in the summaries or section descriptions (the 7:45–53 section title *The Unbelief of the Jewish Leaders* is the BSB's verbatim heading). This is standard contextual reading, not a text change; recorded because it is applied doc-wide. Reversible — Jesse may prefer echoing the WEB phrase verbatim throughout.
