# Ezra — book summary

## Short

Ezra is the book of the return. Decades after Babylon destroyed the temple and carried Judah away into captivity, the LORD keeps the promise spoken through Jeremiah — by stirring the spirit of the emperor of Persia. Cyrus sends the captives home to build “the house of the LORD, the God of Israel,” and the book’s first movement (chapters 1–6) follows that building through joy and grief mingled at the foundation, years of opposition that stop the work, two prophets who restart it, and a second emperor’s decree that funds it to completion — Passover kept again in a finished temple. The second movement (chapters 7–10) belongs to Ezra the scribe, who comes up from Babylon decades later with the king’s blessing and the law of Moses in his hand, only to find the returned community — leaders first — intermarried with the surrounding peoples in defiance of that law. His torn robe, his kneeling confession, and the assembly’s hard covenant to separate close the book on one of Scripture’s most difficult pages, which the text itself ends without comment: a list of names, and the note that some of the wives had borne children.

## Full

Ezra opens exactly where 2 Chronicles ends — with the proclamation of Cyrus — and the seam is deliberate: the story of God’s people did not die in Babylon. The book’s first verse names the engine of everything that follows: “that the LORD’s word by Jeremiah’s mouth might be accomplished, the LORD stirred up the spirit of Cyrus king of Persia.” Ezra is a book about God governing empires from the inside — stirring Cyrus, watching over elders, turning a king’s heart, putting generosity into Artaxerxes — so that his house is rebuilt and his people re-formed around his law, all without a single miracle in the usual sense. Its recurring signature is a quieter phrase: “the good hand of his God on him.”

The first movement (chapters 1–6) is the story of a building. Cyrus’s decree sends home whoever will go — a carefully counted company of about fifty thousand, listed family by family in chapter 2 — with the temple’s plundered vessels restored and their neighbors’ silver and gold besides. They build the altar before anything else, keeping the set feasts “as it is written in the law of Moses” even while afraid of the peoples around them, and when the foundation of the new temple is laid, the shout of joy and the weeping of the old men who had seen the first house rise together into one indistinguishable noise. Then come the adversaries: an offer of help refused, hands weakened, counselors hired, and — in letters the book quotes at length in their original language (“Syrian,” the WEB’s name for the language elsewhere called Aramaic) — accusations laid before the Persian court, until the work stops for years. It restarts at a word: Haggai and Zechariah prophesy in the name of the God of Israel, Zerubbabel and Jeshua rise up and build, and when the governor beyond the River asks who authorized it, the answer travels up to Darius — whose archive search turns up Cyrus’s original decree. Darius not only confirms it but pays for it from the royal tribute, and the house is finished and dedicated with joy, the returned exiles keeping Passover “because the LORD had made them joyful, and had turned the heart of the king of Assyria to them” — the Persian ruler here bearing, in the text’s phrase, the old imperial title.

The second movement (chapters 7–10) is the story of a man and a law. “Now after these things,” in the reign of Artaxerxes — on the standard chronology, nearly six decades after the dedication — Ezra comes up from Babylon: a priest descended from Aaron, “a skilled scribe in the law of Moses,” whose character the narrator compresses into one verse: “For Ezra had set his heart to seek the LORD’s law, and to do it, and to teach statutes and ordinances in Israel.” Artaxerxes grants him everything — travelers, treasure, supplies, tax exemptions for temple personnel, and authority to appoint judges and teach the law — and Ezra blesses the LORD who “has put such a thing as this in the king’s heart.” The journey itself becomes a testimony: having told the king that “The hand of our God is on all those who seek him, for good,” Ezra is ashamed to ask for a military escort, so the company fasts and prays at the river Ahava instead — and arrives with the treasure weighed in complete.

Then the book turns hard. The princes report that the people, the priests, and the Levites — the leaders “chief in this trespass” — have intermarried with the peoples of the lands, the very entanglement the law had forbidden so that the “holy offspring” would not be lost to the abominations around them. Ezra tears his garment, pulls hair from his head and beard, and sits confounded until the evening offering; then he kneels and prays one of Scripture’s great corporate confessions — no excuses and no petitions, only guilt owned (“our iniquities have increased over our head”) and grace remembered (“to leave us a remnant to escape”). A weeping assembly gathers, Shecaniah proposes a covenant “to put away all the wives and those who are born of them… according to the law,” and over three months a commission examines every case. Chapters 9–10 are the book’s hard passage, and honesty requires saying so: wives and children are sent away, and readers in every age have felt the weight of it. The text presents the community’s decision as covenant faithfulness as they understood the law’s demand in their crisis, records that a few men stood against the proceedings without saying why, and passes no comment in either direction — and neither do the summaries below. The book simply ends with the list of the guilty and one flat closing sentence: “All these had taken foreign wives. Some of them had wives by whom they had children.” The story of the restored community continues in Nehemiah, where Ezra reappears with the law in his hands.

# The ten chapters

## Ezra 1
**Summary:** In the first year of Cyrus king of Persia, the narrator names the real mover behind the king’s proclamation: “that the LORD’s word by Jeremiah’s mouth might be accomplished, the LORD stirred up the spirit of Cyrus king of Persia,” who proclaims in writing throughout his kingdom that “The LORD, the God of heaven, has given me all the kingdoms of the earth; and he has commanded me to build him a house in Jerusalem.” Whoever belongs to his people may go up, and those who stay are to supply the goers with silver, gold, goods, and animals. The heads of Judah and Benjamin, with the priests and Levites — “all whose spirit God had stirred” — rise up to build, and their neighbors do their part, having “strengthened their hands” with treasure “in addition to all that was willingly offered.” Cyrus himself brings out the vessels of the LORD’s house that Nebuchadnezzar had carried off to the house of his gods — five thousand four hundred of them, counted out to Sheshbazzar the prince of Judah, who brings them up with the returning captives from Babylon to Jerusalem.
**Tags:** `providence` — the whole return is set moving by God’s governance of an emperor’s inner life: “the LORD stirred up the spirit of Cyrus king of Persia” (1:1), and the returnees are likewise “all whose spirit God had stirred” (1:5); `gods-faithfulness` — the decree exists “that the LORD’s word by Jeremiah’s mouth might be accomplished” (1:1) — the promised return delivered; `generosity` — those who stay give freely to those who go: neighbors “strengthened their hands” with silver, gold, goods, animals, and precious things, “in addition to all that was willingly offered” (1:4, 6); `restoration-of-israel` — the return’s opening document: the proclamation made “that the LORD’s word by Jeremiah’s mouth might be accomplished,” and “all whose spirit God had stirred” rise up to go home and build (1:1–5, 11); `the-house-of-god` — the house is the decree’s whole object: “he has commanded me to build him a house in Jerusalem” (1:2), with its plundered vessels counted back for the return (1:2–7); `exile-and-captivity` — the captivity ends where the book begins: the community formed by deportation comes home, “when the captives were brought up from Babylon” to Jerusalem (1:11)

## Ezra 2
**Summary:** The returning company is counted, family by family: the men of the people of Israel by clan and town, then the priests, the Levites, the singers, the gatekeepers, the temple servants, and the descendants of Solomon’s servants — a whole society in list form, coming home under Zerubbabel, Jeshua, and their companions. Some “could not show their fathers’ houses and their offspring, whether they were of Israel,” and priests among them are “deemed disqualified and removed from the priesthood,” told they should not eat “of the most holy things until a priest stood up to serve with Urim and with Thummim.” The whole assembly comes to forty-two thousand three hundred sixty, besides seven thousand three hundred thirty-seven servants and two hundred singing men and singing women. Arriving at the site of the LORD’s house, some family heads give “according to their ability” into the treasury for the rebuilding, and everyone settles in their cities.

## Ezra 3
**Summary:** In the seventh month the people gather “as one man” to Jerusalem, and Jeshua and Zerubbabel build the altar of the God of Israel first — “In spite of their fear because of the peoples of the surrounding lands” — restoring the morning and evening burnt offerings, the feast of booths, and all the set feasts of the LORD, “as it is written in the law of Moses the man of God,” while the foundation of the LORD’s temple is not yet laid. Money then goes to masons and carpenters, and food, drink, and oil to Sidon and Tyre for cedar from Lebanon to the sea, to Joppa. In the second month of the second year the builders lay the foundation of the LORD’s temple, and priests with trumpets and Levites with cymbals sing to one another the old refrain: “For he is good, for his loving kindness endures forever toward Israel.” But the sound that carries far away is double — the old men who had seen the first house “wept with a loud voice” while many shouted aloud for joy, “so that the people could not discern the noise of the shout of joy from the noise of the weeping of the people.” The text reports the mingled weeping and shouting side by side and explains neither, and so does this summary.

## Ezra 4
**Summary:** Hearing of the building, “the adversaries of Judah and Benjamin” come to Zerubbabel with an offer — “Let us build with you, for we seek your God as you do” — and the heads of Israel refuse it: “You have nothing to do with us in building a house to our God”; the house will be built as King Cyrus commanded. The people of the land then set out to stop what they could not join: they “weakened the hands of the people of Judah, and troubled them in building,” and “hired counselors against them to frustrate their purpose” all the days of Cyrus and down to the reign of Darius. The account then runs forward through the written accusations of later reigns — one in the days of Ahasuerus; then, in the days of Artaxerxes, a letter from Bishlam and his companions “written in Syrian” (the WEB’s name for the language elsewhere called Aramaic, in which the book’s official documents are recorded); and after it the letter of Rehum the chancellor and Shimshai the scribe, warning the king that Jerusalem is “the rebellious and bad city” whose finished walls will cost him tribute, custom, and toll; Artaxerxes orders a search of the records, agrees, and has the work stopped “by force of arms.” The chapter closes back at the temple: “Then work stopped on God’s house which is at Jerusalem.” It stopped until the second year of Darius king of Persia.

## Ezra 5
**Summary:** Two prophets, Haggai and Zechariah, prophesy to the Jews in Judah and Jerusalem “in the name of the God of Israel,” and Zerubbabel and Jeshua “rose up and began to build God’s house” — with “the prophets of God, helping them.” Tattenai, the governor beyond the River, arrives with his companions and puts the official question: “Who gave you a decree to build this house and to finish this wall?” — “But the eye of their God was on the elders of the Jews,” and the work is not made to stop while the matter goes to Darius by letter. Tattenai’s letter reports the elders’ own answer: “We are the servants of the God of heaven and earth,” rebuilding the house a great king of Israel built long ago; it was destroyed and the people exiled after “our fathers had provoked the God of heaven to wrath”; and Cyrus himself decreed its rebuilding and returned its vessels. The letter closes by asking Darius to search the royal treasure house and confirm whether such a decree exists.

## Ezra 6
**Summary:** Darius orders the archives searched, and at Achmetha in Media a scroll is found: Cyrus’s original decree — “let the house be built,” its expenses paid from the king’s house, its gold and silver vessels restored. Darius then goes beyond confirming it: Tattenai must keep far away; the full cost is to be paid “with all diligence” from the royal tribute; and animals, wheat, salt, wine, and oil are to be supplied daily, “that they may offer sacrifices of pleasant aroma to the God of heaven, and pray for the life of the king and of his sons” — with a beam pulled out from the house of anyone who alters the decree. ## Ezra 7

## Ezra 8
**Summary:** Ezra lists the heads of households who go up with him, then camps three days at the river that runs to Ahava — where he looks around and finds “there were none of the sons of Levi.” He sends chief men to Iddo at Casiphia for “ministers for the house of our God,” and “According to the good hand of our God on us” they return with “a man of discretion,” Sherebiah, with eighteen of his house, Hashabiah and Jeshaiah with twenty, and two hundred twenty temple servants. Then Ezra proclaims a fast, “that we might humble ourselves before our God, to seek from him a straight way for us, for our little ones, and for all our possessions” — “For I was ashamed to ask of the king a band of soldiers and horsemen,” he explains, “because we had spoken to the king, saying, ‘The hand of our God is on all those who seek him, for good; but his power and his wrath is against all those who forsake him’” — and the outcome is stated as plainly as the resolve: “So we fasted and begged our God for this, and he granted our request.” Twelve chiefs of the priests receive the weighed-out offering of silver, gold, and vessels — “You are holy to the LORD, and the vessels are holy” — and the company departs on the twelfth day of the first month: “The hand of our God was on us, and he delivered us from the hand of the enemy and the bandits by the way.” In Jerusalem everything is counted and weighed and the weight written down, burnt offerings and a sin offering are made for all Israel, and the king’s commissions go to the governors, who support the people and God’s house.
**Tags:** `prayer` — corporate fasting and petition before a dangerous road, and the answer recorded: “So we fasted and begged our God for this, and he granted our request” (8:21, 23); `trust-in-god` — Ezra stakes the caravan’s safety on the confession he made to the king — “The hand of our God is on all those who seek him, for good” — and is ashamed to hedge it with soldiers and horsemen (8:22); `gods-protection` — protection sought for the journey, the little ones, and the possessions (8:21) and granted: “he delivered us from the hand of the enemy and the bandits by the way” (8:31); `fasting` — the practice itself, proclaimed and answered: a corporate fast “that we might humble ourselves before our God, to seek from him a straight way for us, for our little ones, and for all our possessions” — “So we fasted and begged our God for this, and he granted our request” (8:21, 23); `deliverance` — the plain rescue register, confessed beforehand and narrated afterward: “he delivered us from the hand of the enemy and the bandits by the way” (8:22, 31)

## Ezra 9
**Summary:** With the work done, the princes bring Ezra a report: the people of Israel, the priests, and the Levites “have not separated themselves from the peoples of the lands, following their abominations,” but have taken their daughters for themselves and their sons, “so that the holy offspring have mixed themselves with the peoples of the lands” — and “the hand of the princes and rulers has been chief in this trespass.” Ezra tears his garment and robe, pulls hair from his head and beard, and sits confounded until the evening offering, while “everyone who trembled at the words of the God of Israel” gathers around him. Then, on his knees with hands spread out to the LORD, he prays — a confession with no petition in it — owning the guilt without distance (“our iniquities have increased over our head, and our guiltiness has grown up to the heavens”), tracing the captivity to the people’s own iniquities, and marveling at what God has done anyway: “Now for a little moment grace has been shown from the LORD our God, to leave us a remnant to escape,” for “our God has not forsaken us in our bondage.” Since God has “punished us less than our iniquities deserve,” how can they break his commandments again? The prayer ends where it began, with guilt laid open before a righteous God: “Behold, we are before you in our guiltiness; for no one can stand before you because of this.”
**Tags:** `prayer` — Ezra’s kneeling, hands-spread confession on behalf of the whole people (9:5–15), prayed publicly while those who tremble at God’s words gather to him (9:4); `sin` — the chapter’s substance is guilt diagnosed and owned: “our iniquities have increased over our head, and our guiltiness has grown up to the heavens” (9:6), with the exile itself read as sin’s wage (9:7, 13); `gods-faithfulness` — the confession turns on grace that outran the guilt: “our God has not forsaken us in our bondage, but has extended loving kindness to us” (9:9), leaving “a remnant to escape” (9:8); `confession-of-sin` — the OT’s paradigm corporate confession, prayed by a leader identifying with sins not personally his: “O my God, I am ashamed and blush to lift up my face to you” and “our iniquities have increased over our head, and our guiltiness has grown up to the heavens” (9:5–15); `remnant` — the phrase witness prayed by the survivors it names: grace shown “to leave us a remnant to escape,” and “we are left a remnant that has escaped” (9:8, 13–15); `unequally-yoked` — the covenant-separation crisis behind the query family: Israel’s leaders “have not separated themselves from the peoples of the lands,” “so that the holy offspring have mixed themselves with the peoples of the lands” (9:1–2, 12, 14) — a covenant-national background text, never a direct template for the modern question (register caveat per the log row)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings
**Sections:**
- **9:1–4 — Intermarriage with Neighboring Peoples** (title: BSB) — The princes’ report — leaders chief in the trespass — leaves Ezra with torn robe and pulled hair, sitting confounded until the evening offering as the tremblers at God’s word gather.
- **9:5–15 — Ezra’s Prayer of Confession** (title: BSB) — A corporate confession without excuses or requests: guilt grown up to the heavens, exile deserved, a remnant spared by grace, and no standing before the righteous God.

## Ezra 10
**Summary:** While Ezra prays and confesses, “weeping and casting himself down before God’s house,” a very great assembly of men, women, and children gathers, and “the people wept very bitterly.” Shecaniah gives the crisis its turning point: “We have trespassed against our God, and have married foreign women of the peoples of the land. Yet now there is hope for Israel concerning this thing” — let a covenant be made with God “to put away all the wives and those who are born of them,” and “Let it be done according to the law.” Ezra makes the chiefs of the priests, the Levites, and all Israel swear to do it, then withdraws, fasting and mourning; a proclamation summons every returned exile to Jerusalem within three days on pain of forfeiture and exclusion. The people sit in the wide place in front of God’s house, “trembling because of this matter, and because of the great rain”; they answer, “We must do as you have said concerning us,” but ask for an orderly process through appointed elders and judges, “until the fierce wrath of our God is turned from us” — the text notes that two men “stood up against this,” with two others helping them, and does not say why. For three months the commission examines the cases — the record notes of the first-listed priests, the sons of Jeshua and his brothers, that “They gave their hand that they would put away their wives; and being guilty, they offered a ram of the flock for their guilt” — and the book ends with the list of names and the text’s own last, unadorned word — an ending it leaves without comment, as does this summary: “All these had taken foreign wives. Some of them had wives by whom they had children.”
**Tags:** `repentance` — the narrated arc is confession and turning: bitter corporate weeping (10:1), “make confession to the LORD, the God of your fathers and do his pleasure. Separate yourselves” (10:11), and the assembly’s “We must do as you have said” carried through (10:12, 16–17) — the tag marks that arc as the text tells it, not a verdict on the remedy (see the Decisions record); `covenant` — a covenant solemnly made and sworn: “let’s make a covenant with our God” (10:3), and Ezra “made the chiefs of the priests, the Levites, and all Israel to swear… So they swore” (10:5); `confession-of-sin` — the practice enacted corporately: Ezra “weeping and casting himself down before God’s house” as he prays and confesses, and the charge to the assembly, “make confession to the LORD, the God of your fathers and do his pleasure” (10:1, 11); `oaths-and-vows` — oath-taking as the mechanism binding a national decision: Ezra “made the chiefs of the priests, the Levites, and all Israel to swear… So they swore” (10:5), and the guilty “gave their hand” in pledge (10:19); `unequally-yoked` — the crisis named and its remedy narrated: “We have trespassed against our God, and have married foreign women of the peoples of the land. Yet now there is hope for Israel concerning this thing” — resolved “to put away all the wives and those who are born of them” (10:2–3, 10–11, 44) — narrated, not prescribed: a covenant-national background text, never a direct template for the modern question (register caveat per the log row); `revival-and-reformation` — community reform carried through to completion: the assembly’s “We must do as you have said concerning us,” an orderly three-month examination, and the matter finished (10:12–17)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings
**Sections:**
- **10:1–5 — Shecaniah’s Encouragement** (title: BSB) — Before the weeping assembly Shecaniah names the trespass and the hope, proposes the covenant according to the law, and Ezra puts the leaders under oath.
- **10:6–17 — The People’s Confession of Sin** (title: BSB) — Summoned within three days, the trembling assembly consents in the rain, asks for an orderly process by cities and judges — two men opposing, unexplained — and the three-month examination runs to completion.
- **10:18–44 — Those Guilty of Intermarriage** (title: BSB) — The list, priests first: the sons of Jeshua give their hand to put away their wives and offer a ram for their guilt, and the book’s final sentence — foreign wives taken, some with children — is left exactly where the text leaves it.

# Decisions record (delegated defaults — each reversible on Jesse’s word)

1. **Vocabulary per the project-wide rollout ruling: all 131 ids at commit b3f491d are usable, including the nine PR #43 ids** (`asking-in-gods-will`, `baptism`, `benediction`, `caring-for-aging-parents`, `christ-the-cornerstone`, `doubt`, `incarnation`, `justification-by-faith`, `trinity`), with any use of those nine to be recorded here as a reversible delegated default. All nine were considered for Ezra and **none is used** — none comes close (the nearest scan: `benediction` on 7:27’s “Blessed be the LORD” was checked and dropped; that pack’s register is the blessing pronounced over people, and 7:27 is doxology to God, which `praise` carries honestly). Tag ids are YAML file basenames throughout; no `pastoral-*` id appears in this book, so no basename/inner-id divergence is in play.
2. **No `pastoral-*` tag anywhere, and specifically none on chapters 9–10 (project-wide pastoral-register ruling, 2026-08-23).** The rule: the fourteen `pastoral-*` packs are the personal-crisis register and are never tagged on national-scale material. Ezra 9–10’s intermarriage crisis, grief, and mass separation are covenant-national from first verse to last (“the holy offspring,” princes and rulers “chief in this trespass,” an assembly of all Judah and Benjamin); in particular `pastoral-marriage-divorce-teaching` (the register of Jesus’ marriage teaching and personal marriage crisis) and `pastoral-betrayal-and-marriage-crisis` were each checked and are **not** used — tagging either would route a person in a present-day marriage crisis to a national covenant proceeding, harming both the searcher and the text. Reversible only by Jesse.
3. **Chapters 9–10 handled by report, signposted once, adjudicated nowhere.** The summaries carry Ezra’s grief, the covenant language, the oath, the process, the recorded dissent (10:15 — reported with the text’s own silence about its grounds), and the closing list exactly as the text gives them. The difficulty is signposted honestly in the book summary (“the book’s hard passage… readers in every age have felt the weight of it”) with an explicit statement that the text presents the community’s decision as covenant faithfulness as they understood the law’s demand and passes no comment — and that the summaries do the same: the decision is neither endorsed as a template nor condemned. The ch. 10 ending (the list and the note about children) is left standing as the text’s own ending, explicitly uncommented. Reversible.
4. **`repentance` on ch. 10 — kept, with its justification fenced.** The concern: tagging the chapter with `repentance` could be read as endorsing the putting-away as the required shape of repentance. The tag is kept because the chapter’s narrated arc — bitter weeping, “make confession to the LORD… and do his pleasure” (10:11), the assembly’s consent carried through — is the concept’s teaching substance (confession and turning; the pack’s Torrey provenance includes CONFESSION OF SIN), and the justification text states explicitly that the tag marks the arc “as the text tells it, not a verdict on the remedy.” If Jesse judges the fence insufficient, the tag drops and ch. 10 keeps `covenant` alone. Reversible.
5. **`repentance` NOT placed on ch. 9 — the split argued.** Ezra 9 is confession without yet any turning (the prayer asks nothing and resolves nothing; the turning is ch. 10’s story), so ch. 9 carries the confession under `prayer` and the guilt under `sin`, and `repentance` sits only where confession joins action. A reviewer could defend `repentance` on ch. 9 via the confession register alone; this draft keeps the sharper split. Reversible.
6. **Ch. 4 honest-and-empty.** The chapter depicts opposition inflicted, slander, and a work halted; God is present only in the construct “God’s house.” Every candidate was the failure/attack mode of a concept, not its substance: `do-not-lose-heart` (the hands are successfully weakened — the Genesis-3 rule), `honesty` (the letter’s slander is never answered or evaluated in-chapter), `gods-protection` (the work is stopped, not protected — protection is ch. 5’s story and is tagged there). The opposition theme itself goes to the gap table (`opposition-to-gods-work`). Reversible.
7. **`praying-for-leaders` on ch. 6 — borderline, kept.** The pack’s claim is “submit, honor, pray. Nothing more” (its own design note), and 6:10 is exactly the pray-for-the-ruler substance: the temple’s sacrifices are to be offered with prayer “for the life of the king and of his sons.” The caution: the arrangement is decreed by the king himself rather than taught to Israel. Kept as honest presence (the restored temple’s service genuinely includes prayer for the ruler); drop it first if the decreed-not-taught origin is judged disqualifying — ch. 6 keeps three tags either way. Reversible.
8. **`obedience-to-the-word` on chs. 3 and 5 — two registers, each argued.** Ch. 3: the restored worship is repeatedly measured against the written word (“as it is written in the law of Moses the man of God,” 3:2; “as it is written,” 3:4) — the hearing-and-doing substance applied to public worship. Ch. 5: the prophets speak “in the name of the God of Israel” (5:1) and the leaders “rose up and began to build” (5:2) — the prophets’ word heard and acted on at once; the compression (two verses) is noted, and if judged too thin the ch. 5 instance drops first, leaving `gods-protection`. Each reversible independently.
9. **`gods-protection` on ch. 5 without a prayer scene.** The pack is the protection-prayer register by design, but the Genesis pilot’s precedent (Gen 7, “the LORD shut him in”) accepts narrated divine protection; 5:5 (“the eye of their God was on the elders… and they didn’t make them cease”) is exactly that.
