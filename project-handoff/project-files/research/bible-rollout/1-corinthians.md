# 1 Corinthians — book summary (draft)

## Short

1 Corinthians is Paul's long letter to a young assembly in trouble (our WEB translation's word for the familiar "church") — the one he had founded in Corinth, a wealthy Greek port city full of temples and competing philosophies. Reports have reached him of quarreling factions, a scandalous sexual case, believers suing each other, and chaotic worship, and the Corinthians themselves have written with questions about marriage, idol food, and spiritual gifts. Paul answers every problem the same way: by reasoning from the Good News itself — Christ crucified is God's wisdom, your body belongs to the Lord, and the God who raised the Lord will raise it, love outranks knowledge and every gift, and the resurrection of Christ, the letter's climax, is the foundation holding all of it up.

## Full

Paul had planted the assembly in Corinth and moved on; this letter answers what happened next. Word has come from two directions — a report from Chloe's household about contentions in the assembly, and a letter from the Corinthians themselves ("Now concerning the things about which you wrote to me", 7:1) — and Paul works through the docket item by item. The letter is not a doctrinal treatise but a pastor's answer sheet, and its method is remarkably consistent: name the problem, then argue from the Good News down to the practice.

Chapters 1–4 take up the factions. The Corinthians have sorted themselves behind celebrity names — "I follow Paul," "I follow Apollos," "I follow Cephas" — and Paul's answer is the cross: the word of the cross looks like foolishness but is the power of God, God deliberately chose the foolish and weak and lowly so that no flesh should boast before him, and the teachers being idolized are only servants — one planted, another watered, but God gave the increase. There is one foundation, Jesus Christ, and every builder's work will be tested by fire.

Chapters 5–7 turn to the body. A man has his father's wife and the assembly is proud rather than grieved, so Paul commands them to remove him — a little yeast leavens the whole lump, and Christ, our Passover, has been sacrificed. Believers are dragging each other before pagan courts; better to be wronged. Sexual immorality is answered not with a bare rule but with ownership: your bodies are members of Christ, your body is a temple of the Holy Spirit, you were bought with a price. Chapter 7 then answers their marriage questions — mutual obligation within marriage, the value of both singleness and marriage, the Lord's command against divorce, and counsel for believers married to unbelievers.

Chapters 8–10 handle food sacrificed to idols, a question dividing the knowledgeable from the tender of conscience. Paul grants the knowledge — idols are nothing and God is one — but crowns it with love: liberty that destroys a weak brother sins against Christ. He offers himself as the pattern, an apostle who refuses his own right to support and becomes all things to all men to save some, and he warns from Israel's wilderness story that privilege without self-control ends in overthrow. The unit closes with the letter's rule of thumb: "do all to the glory of God" (10:31).

Chapters 11–14 enter the worship gathering: head coverings in prayer and prophecy, a Lord's supper deformed by class division, and services dominated by uninterpreted spiritual speech. Paul's answer is the body — one body, many members, every gift given by the one Spirit "for the profit of all" — with chapter 13, love's famous portrait, placed deliberately at the center: without love, the most impressive gift is a clanging cymbal. Worship must build others up, intelligibly, "decently and in order," because God is not a God of confusion but of peace.

Chapter 15 is the summit. Some in Corinth say there is no resurrection of the dead, and Paul stakes everything on the fact he first delivered: Christ died for our sins according to the Scriptures, was buried, was raised on the third day, and appeared — to Cephas, to the twelve, to over five hundred at once. If Christ has not been raised, faith is vain and we are still in our sins; but Christ has been raised, the first fruits, and in Christ all will be made alive — sown a perishable body, raised imperishable, changed in the twinkling of an eye at the last trumpet, until death itself, the last enemy, is swallowed up in victory. The letter's every earlier answer leans on this one. Chapter 16 closes with practical grace: a weekly collection for the saints in Jerusalem, travel plans, commendations, the charge "Let all that you do be done in love" (16:14), and a farewell greeting in Paul's own hand (16:21).

# The sixteen chapters

## 1 Corinthians 1
**Summary:** Paul greets the assembly of God at Corinth and thanks God for the grace and gifts given them in Christ. Then the problem: Chloe's household reports contentions, with parties forming behind names — "I follow Paul," "I follow Apollos," "I follow Cephas," and even "I follow Christ." Paul's counterquestion, "Is Christ divided?", opens into his answer: the word of the cross is foolishness to those who are dying, but to those being saved it is the power of God. God chose the foolish, weak, and lowly things of the world precisely so that no flesh should boast before him — Christ Jesus "was made to us wisdom from God, and righteousness and sanctification, and redemption," so "He who boasts, let him boast in the Lord."
**Tags:** `the-cross` — "the word of the cross... to us who are being saved it is the power of God" (1:18); "we preach Christ crucified" (1:23); `harmony-with-others` — the plea "that there be no divisions among you" against the party quarrels reported by Chloe's household (1:10–12); `wisdom-from-god` — "Christ is the power of God and the wisdom of God" (1:24, 30) against the wisdom of this world made foolish (1:20); `humble-exaltation` — God chose the foolish, weak, and lowly to put to shame the wise and strong, "that no flesh should boast before God" (1:26–29); `gods-faithfulness` — "God is faithful," who "will also confirm you until the end, blameless" (1:8–9)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings; bibleproject-framing
**Sections:**
- **1:1–9 — Greeting and Thanksgiving** (title: adapted from BSB) — Paul and Sosthenes greet the sanctified-and-called assembly at Corinth; Paul thanks God for the grace, speech, knowledge, and gifts given them, and for the faithful God who will confirm them blameless to the end.
- **1:10–17 — Unity in the Church** (title: BSB) — The report from Chloe's household: contentions and name-brand factions. Paul asks whether Christ is divided, is relieved to have baptized only a few, and states his commission — to preach the Good News, not with wisdom of words, lest the cross be made void.
- **1:18–31 — The Message of the Cross and Wisdom from God** (title: adapted from BSB) — The cross is foolishness to the perishing but God's power to the saved; Jews ask signs and Greeks seek wisdom, but God saves through the preached Christ crucified — and chose the lowly Corinthians themselves as proof that all boasting belongs in the Lord.

## 1 Corinthians 2
**Summary:** Paul reminds them how he first arrived: in weakness, fear, and much trembling, resolved "not to know anything among you except Jesus Christ and him crucified," so that their faith would stand on God's power rather than persuasive human wisdom. There is a wisdom he speaks among the mature — God's hidden wisdom, foreordained before the worlds, which none of this age's rulers knew — "For had they known it, they wouldn't have crucified the Lord of glory." That wisdom cannot be reasoned up to; God revealed it through the Spirit, who "searches all things, yes, the deep things of God." So the natural man finds God's things foolishness and cannot know them, but those who have received God's Spirit can — "we have Christ's mind."
**Tags:** `wisdom-from-god` — "we speak God's wisdom in a mystery... which God foreordained before the worlds for our glory," revealed through the Spirit (2:7, 10); `the-cross` — "I determined not to know anything among you except Jesus Christ and him crucified" (2:2, 8)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings; bibleproject-framing

## 1 Corinthians 3
**Summary:** The factions prove the Corinthians are still spiritual infants: "I fed you with milk, not with solid food," and their jealousy and strife show them walking like anyone else. Paul cuts the celebrity teachers down to size — "I planted. Apollos watered. But God gave the increase" — servants in God's field, builders on God's building. There is only one foundation, Jesus Christ; each builder's work on it, gold or straw, will be tested by fire on the Day, bringing reward if it survives and loss if it burns, "but he himself will be saved, but as through fire." And the building matters because of what it is: "Don't you know that you are God's temple and that God's Spirit lives in you?" Since the wisdom of this world is foolishness with God, no one should boast in men — all things are yours, and you are Christ's.
**Tags:** `spiritual-growth` — "I fed you with milk, not with solid food, for you weren't yet ready" — babies in Christ who should be maturing (3:1–3); `christ-the-cornerstone` — "no one can lay any other foundation than that which has been laid, which is Jesus Christ" (3:10–11); `heavenly-reward` — "each will receive his own reward according to his own labor"; work that remains is rewarded (3:8, 14); `judgment-seat-of-christ` — "the Day will declare it... the fire itself will test what sort of work each man's work is," reward or loss for the saved builder (3:13–15); `wisdom-from-god` — "the wisdom of this world is foolishness with God," so let the worldly-wise become fools to become wise (3:18–20); `envy-and-jealousy` — "insofar as there is jealousy, strife, and factions among you," they are still fleshly, walking in the ways of men (3:3)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings; bibleproject-framing

## 1 Corinthians 4
**Summary:** Regard the apostles as "Christ's servants and stewards of God's mysteries" — and a steward's one requirement is to be found faithful. Paul therefore shrugs off the Corinthians' evaluations of him: "he who judges me is the Lord," so judge nothing before the time, when the Lord will expose the hidden things and each will receive praise from God. Their boasting collapses under one question — "what do you have that you didn't receive?" — and Paul sets their imagined reign against the apostles' actual life: a spectacle to the world, hungry, beaten, homeless, blessing when cursed, "the filth of the world, the dirt wiped off by all". He writes not to shame them but to admonish his beloved children — he became their father through the Good News — sending Timothy, and asking whether his own coming should be with a rod or in love and gentleness.
**Tags:** `pleasing-god-not-people` — "it is a very small thing that I should be judged by you... he who judges me is the Lord" (4:3–5); `suffering-for-christ` — the apostles displayed "like men sentenced to death": hunger, thirst, beatings, slander, endurance "for Christ's sake" (4:9–13); `grace-not-earned` — "what do you have that you didn't receive? But if you did receive it, why do you boast...?" (4:7)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings; bibleproject-framing

## 1 Corinthians 5
**Summary:** A report of sexual immorality of a kind "not even named among the Gentiles": a man in the assembly "has his father's wife" — and the assembly is arrogant about it instead of mourning. Paul has already judged the case: gathered in the name of the Lord Jesus, they are to "deliver such a one to Satan for the destruction of the flesh, that the spirit may be saved in the day of the Lord Jesus" — removal from the community, aimed at rescue. The reasoning is the Passover picture: a little yeast leavens the whole lump, and since "Christ, our Passover, has been sacrificed in our place," the community must purge the old yeast and keep the feast "with the unleavened bread of sincerity and truth." Paul then corrects a misreading of his earlier letter: he never meant withdrawing from the world's sinners — that would mean leaving the world — but not keeping company with anyone called a brother who persists in such sin. Those outside, God judges; "Put away the wicked man from among yourselves."
**Tags:** `holiness` — "Purge out the old yeast, that you may be a new lump, even as you are unleavened" — the community kept holy, feasting on sincerity and truth (5:6–8); `sin` — "It is actually reported that there is sexual immorality among you" (5:1): flagrant sin tolerated and even boasted over, with its leavening spread through the whole lump (5:1–2, 6); `the-cross` — "For indeed Christ, our Passover, has been sacrificed in our place" — the sacrifice grounding the call to purge the yeast (5:7); `church-discipline` — the assembly's commanded act, aimed at rescue: gathered in the Lord's name, "deliver such a one to Satan for the destruction of the flesh, that the spirit may be saved in the day of the Lord Jesus," with the association rules and the closing charge "Put away the wicked man from among yourselves." (5:1–13)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings; bibleproject-framing

## 1 Corinthians 6
**Summary:** Two more symptoms of forgetting who they are. First, believers are suing each other before pagan courts: the saints will one day judge the world — even angels — yet can't settle small matters among themselves; "Why not rather be wronged?" beats defrauding a brother. A warning list of unrighteous lifestyles that will not inherit God's Kingdom pivots into the past tense of the Good News: "Some of you were such, but you were washed. You were sanctified. You were justified in the name of the Lord Jesus, and in the Spirit of our God." Second, sexual immorality: the body is not for it but "for the Lord," and God, who "raised up the Lord," will "also raise us up by his power." Your bodies are members of Christ — unthinkable to join them to a prostitute — so "Flee sexual immorality!": your body is a temple of the Holy Spirit, and "You are not your own, for you were bought with a price. Therefore glorify God in your body and in your spirit, which are God's."
**Tags:** `pastoral-sexual-purity` — "Flee sexual immorality!... he who commits sexual immorality sins against his own body," which is the Spirit's temple (6:13, 18–20); `identity-in-christ` — "your bodies are members of Christ... You are not your own, for you were bought with a price" (6:15, 19–20); `harmony-with-others` — brother against brother at law: "Why not rather be wronged? Why not rather be defrauded?" (6:1–8); `forgiveness-of-sins` — the decisive past tense over former lives: "Some of you were such, but you were washed. You were sanctified. You were justified in the name of the Lord Jesus, and in the Spirit of our God." (6:9–11); `living-for-gods-glory` — the charge the whole body argument lands on: bought with a price, "Therefore glorify God in your body and in your spirit, which are God's." (6:19–20)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings; bibleproject-framing
**Sections:**
- **6:1–8 — Lawsuits among Believers** (title: BSB) — Believers with grievances are going before unrighteous judges; Paul calls it already a defeat, urges the assembly to settle its own matters, and says it is better to be wronged than to wrong a brother.
- **6:9–17 — Members of Christ** (title: BSB) — The unrighteous will not inherit God's Kingdom — "Some of you were such" — but no longer: washed, sanctified, justified. Lawful is not the same as expedient; the body belongs to the Lord, and bodies joined to Christ cannot be joined to a prostitute.
- **6:18–20 — The Temple of the Holy Spirit** (title: BSB) — Flee sexual immorality, which is sin against one's own body — the temple of the Holy Spirit. Bought with a price, believers are not their own: glorify God in your body.

## 1 Corinthians 7
**Summary:** Paul begins answering the Corinthians' letter with marriage: against the surrounding immorality, husband and wife each owe the other real affection and belong to each other bodily, with abstinence only by consent, for a season of fasting and prayer. Singleness, like his own, Paul calls good — "each man has his own gift from God" — but "it's better to marry than to burn with passion." To the married the Lord's own command stands: no leaving; a believer married to a willing unbeliever should stay, since the believing spouse sanctifies the home, though "if the unbeliever departs, let there be separation... God has called us in peace." The chapter's wider principle is contentment in place — "Let each man stay in that calling in which he was called," circumcised or not, enslaved or free (though take freedom if offered). On the never-married Paul gives judgment, not command: because the time is short and "the mode of this world passes away," remaining single frees a person to "attend to the Lord without distraction" — yet those who marry do not sin, and a widow is free to remarry, "only in the Lord."
**Tags:** `godly-marriage` — "Let the husband give his wife the affection owed her, and likewise also the wife her husband": mutual belonging, with abstinence only by consent for a season of fasting and prayer (7:2–5); `pastoral-marriage-divorce-teaching` — "not I, but the Lord—that the wife not leave her husband... and that the husband not leave his wife," with counsel for believers married to unbelievers and for separation "if the unbeliever departs" (7:10–16, 39); `self-control` — "if they don't have self-control, let them marry. For it's better to marry than to burn with passion" (7:5, 9); `contentment` — "Let each man stay in that calling in which he was called... in whatever condition he was called, stay in that condition with God" (7:17–24); `singleness` — Scripture's fullest positive treatment of the unmarried life: "each man has his own gift from God," and the judgment that remaining single frees a person to "attend to the Lord without distraction" (7:7–9, 25–35, 39–40)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings; bibleproject-framing
**Sections:**
- **7:1–16 — Principles of Marriage** (title: BSB) — Marriage's mutual obligations; concession and gift; the command against leaving a spouse; and the mixed-marriage cases — stay with a content unbeliever, accept separation if the unbeliever departs, for "God has called us in peace."
- **7:17–24 — Live Your Calling** (title: BSB) — The rule for all the assemblies: remain with God in the condition where you were called — circumcised or uncircumcised, enslaved or free — because what matters is keeping God's commandments, and everyone called in the Lord is his free man and his bondservant at once.
- **7:25–40 — The Unmarried and Widowed** (title: BSB) — Paul's judgment for virgins and widows: in the present distress, with time short, singleness spares trouble and frees undistracted devotion to the Lord; marrying is no sin — one does well, the other better — and a widow may remarry only in the Lord.

## 1 Corinthians 8
**Summary:** Next question: food sacrificed to idols. Paul grants the theology at once — "no idol is anything in the world," for us "there is one God, the Father... and one Lord, Jesus Christ" — but he frames the whole answer with a warning: "Knowledge puffs up, but love builds up." Not everyone possesses that knowledge; some former idolaters still eat such food with a conscience that condemns them, and a knowledgeable believer's public liberty can embolden a weak brother into what is, for him, sin — destroying "the brother for whose sake Christ died." Wounding weak consciences is sinning against Christ himself, so Paul sets the standard by his own resolve: "if food causes my brother to stumble, I will eat no meat forever more, that I don't cause my brother to stumble."
**Tags:** `disputable-matters` — food itself commends no one to God; liberty over idol food is real but must not become "a stumbling block to the weak" (8:8–9); `loving-others` — "Knowledge puffs up, but love builds up," and love gives up meat entirely rather than trip a brother (8:1, 13)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings; bibleproject-framing

## 1 Corinthians 9
**Summary:** Paul holds up his own practice as the pattern for surrendered rights. As an apostle — "you are the seal of my apostleship" — he has every right to material support: the soldier, the vineyard, the flock, the law's unmuzzled ox, the temple's priests, and the Lord's own ordinance that "those who proclaim the Good News should live from the Good News." Yet he has used none of it, preaching free of charge rather than hinder the Good News — not that preaching earns him standing, for "necessity is laid on me; but woe is to me if I don't preach the Good News." Free from all, he enslaves himself to all: a Jew to Jews, weak to the weak, "all things to all men, that I may by all means save some." The chapter closes at the games: every athlete exercises self-control for a fading crown, but Paul runs and fights for an incorruptible one — "I beat my body and bring it into submission, lest by any means, after I have preached to others, I myself should be disqualified."
**Tags:** `sharing-your-faith` — "woe is to me if I don't preach the Good News... I have become all things to all men, that I may by all means save some" (9:16, 19–23); `self-control` — "Every man who strives in the games exercises self-control in all things... I beat my body and bring it into submission" (9:25–27); `heavenly-reward` — run to win the prize: "they do it to receive a corruptible crown, but we an incorruptible" (9:24–25); `supporting-gospel-workers` — the theme's fullest argument: soldier, vineyard, flock, the law's unmuzzled ox, and the temple's priests stacked up to the Lord's own ordinance that "those who proclaim the Good News should live from the Good News" — a right Paul then surrenders rather than hinder the Good News (9:4–15)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings; bibleproject-framing

## 1 Corinthians 10
**Summary:** Israel's story is the warning label on presumption: all the fathers were under the cloud, passed through the sea, ate the same spiritual food and drank from the spiritual rock — "and the rock was Christ" — yet with most of them God was not well pleased, and they were overthrown in the wilderness. Their idolatry, immorality (twenty-three thousand fell in one day), testing of Christ, and grumbling "were written for our admonition," so "let him who thinks he stands be careful that he doesn't fall" — though God faithfully limits every temptation and makes a way of escape. Therefore flee idolatry: the cup and bread of the Lord are a sharing in Christ's blood and body, and "You can't both drink the cup of the Lord and the cup of demons." For everyday cases, liberty holds — eat whatever is sold or served, asking no conscience questions, for "the earth is the Lord's" — but yield the liberty the moment another's conscience is at stake — "Whether therefore you eat or drink, or whatever you do, do all to the glory of God."
**Tags:** `remembered-a-way-of-escape` — this concept's home text: "No temptation has taken you except what is common to man. God is faithful... will with the temptation also make the way of escape" (10:12–13); `lords-supper` — "The cup of blessing which we bless, isn't it a sharing of the blood of Christ?" — the Lord's table set against the table of demons (10:16–17, 21); `divine-judgment` — the wilderness generation "overthrown in the wilderness": twenty-three thousand fallen, serpents, the destroyer — written as our warning (10:5–11); `disputable-matters` — the slogan conceded and capped: "All things are lawful for me," but "not all things are profitable" — eat what is sold, but abstain for the other's conscience (10:23–29); `gods-faithfulness` — "God is faithful, who will not allow you to be tempted above what you are able" (10:13); `living-for-gods-glory` — the unit's closing rule for all of life: "Whether therefore you eat or drink, or whatever you do, do all to the glory of God." (10:31); `temptation` — the plain doctrine the wilderness warnings land on: "No temptation has taken you except what is common to man," with God's faithful limit and the promised way of escape (10:12–13)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings; bibleproject-framing
**Sections:**
- **10:1–13 — Warnings from Israel's Past** (title: BSB) — The exodus generation shared baptism into Moses and Christ's own spiritual food and drink, yet fell to craving, idolatry, immorality, testing, and grumbling; their overthrow warns the confident, while God's faithfulness promises a way of escape under every temptation.
- **10:14–22 — Flee from Idolatry** (title: BSB) — The Lord's cup and loaf are a real sharing in Christ's blood and body, making the many one; pagan sacrifices are offered to demons, and no one can share the Lord's table and the demons' table without provoking the Lord to jealousy.
- **10:23–33 — All to God's Glory** (title: BSB) — Lawful is not the same as profitable: eat freely from market or table without interrogating conscience, but stop for the sake of another's conscience — seeking not your own profit but the many's, doing everything to the glory of God.

## 1 Corinthians 11
**Summary:** Paul turns to the worship gathering, first praising them for holding the traditions he delivered. On head coverings he argues from headship — "the head of every man is Christ, and the head of the woman is man, and the head of Christ is God" — that a man praying or prophesying should be uncovered and a woman covered, appealing to creation's order, to nature's teaching about hair, and to the settled custom of God's assemblies; yet he balances it carefully: "neither is the woman independent of the man, nor the man independent of the woman, in the Lord." (How this applies today — a cultural practice, a lasting principle, or both — is a point where faithful evangelical readers differ; the summary describes what the text says.) On the Lord's supper he cannot praise them: their gatherings do more harm than good, with each grabbing his own meal — "One is hungry, and another is drunken" — shaming those who have nothing. Against that he sets what he received from the Lord: the bread and cup of the night of betrayal, "in memory of me," proclaiming the Lord's death until he comes; eating unworthily brings judgment, so "let a man examine himself" — and the weakness, sickness, and deaths among them are the Lord's discipline, "that we may not be condemned with the world."

## 1 Corinthians 12
**Summary:** "Now concerning spiritual things": the Corinthians once followed mute idols, but the Spirit's first mark is the confession "Jesus is Lord". There are various kinds of gifts, service, and workings, but the same Spirit, Lord, and God behind them all — and every manifestation is given "for the profit of all," distributed to each one as the Spirit desires: wisdom, knowledge, faith, healings, miracles, prophecy, discernment, kinds of languages (the WEB's phrase for what is often called "tongues") and their interpretation. Paul's governing picture is the body: "in one Spirit we were all baptized into one body," and a body is many members — the foot is not less a member for not being a hand, the eye cannot dismiss the hand, and the seemingly weaker members are necessary. God composed the body so that the members "have the same care for one another," suffering and rejoicing together. "Now you are the body of Christ" — not all are apostles or prophets or healers — so desire earnestly the best gifts, while Paul readies "a most excellent way" (12:31).
**Tags:** `spiritual-gifts` — the varieties of gifts, service, and workings from the one Spirit, given "for the profit of all" and distributed "to each one separately as he desires" (12:4–11, 28–31); `harmony-with-others` — the body composed "that there should be no division... but that the members should have the same care for one another," suffering and rejoicing together (12:24–26); `baptism` — "in one Spirit we were all baptized into one body, whether Jews or Greeks, whether bond or free" (12:13); `speaking-in-tongues` — the gift's place among the Spirit's distributions: kinds of languages and their interpretation, given "to each one separately as he desires," with the body's own rhetorical answer that not all speak (12:10–11, 28–30)

## 1 Corinthians 13
**Summary:** The most excellent way. Without love the most spectacular spirituality is nothing: languages of men and angels become sounding brass, prophecy and mountain-moving faith count for nothing, and even giving away everything — one's goods, one's body — profits nothing. ## 1 Corinthians 14
**Summary:** "Follow after love and earnestly desire spiritual gifts, but especially that you may prophesy" — because in the gathered assembly, intelligibility decides value. Speaking in another language addresses God and edifies the speaker, but prophecy — understandable speech from God — edifies, exhorts, and consoles the whole assembly; an uninterpreted language is a trumpet with an uncertain sound, so the speaker should pray to interpret, and Paul, who speaks languages more than them all, would still rather speak five understandable words in the assembly than ten thousand others. Even outsiders feel the difference — a room full of unexplained languages looks like madness, but prophecy lays a visitor's heart bare until he worships, "declaring that God is among you indeed" — so Paul rules that language-speakers be two or three at most, in turn, with interpretation or silence, and prophets two or three, weighed by the others and yielding to fresh revelation, "for God is not a God of confusion but of peace". The instruction that wives be quiet in the assemblies and ask their husbands at home (14:34–35) sits within this same ordering of speech; evangelicals differ on its exact scope — given that 11:5 has women praying and prophesying — and the summary describes rather than settles it. The close balances both gifts: "desire earnestly to prophesy, and don't forbid speaking with other languages. Let all things be done decently and in order."
**Tags:** `spiritual-gifts` — the working rules for prophecy and languages: interpretation, turn-taking, weighing, and the aim "that you may abound to the building up of the assembly" (14:1–5, 12, 26–33, 39–40); `worship` — the assembly's gathered worship ordered for understanding: singing and praying with spirit and understanding, the outsider's "Amen," the visitor who will "fall down on his face and worship God" (14:15–16, 23–25); `gathering-together` — "When you come together, each one of you has a psalm, has a teaching, has a revelation... Let all things be done to build each other up" (14:26); `loving-others` — the unit's own rule carried forward: "Follow after love," with every gift bent toward edifying others rather than oneself (14:1–5, 12); `speaking-in-tongues` — the chapter's full regulation of the gift: an uninterpreted language edifies the speaker rather than the assembly, so speakers are two or three at most, in turn, with interpretation or silence — and still "don't forbid speaking with other languages" (14:2–5, 27–28, 39)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings; bibleproject-framing
**Sections:**
- **14:1–25 — Prophecy and Tongues** (title: BSB) — Why prophecy outranks uninterpreted languages in the assembly: languages speak mysteries to God and edify the speaker, prophecy builds up the assembly and convicts the visitor; instruments, trumpets, and foreigners illustrate that unintelligible sound profits no hearer.
- **14:26–40 — Orderly Worship** (title: BSB) — The gathering's order: each contribution for building up; languages two or three with interpretation or silence; prophets in turn and weighed; the instruction on wives' quietness in the assemblies; Paul's insistence that what he writes is "the commandment of the Lord"; and the closing charge — everything decently and in order.

## 1 Corinthians 15
**Summary:** Paul re-declares the Good News on which they stand and by which they are saved: "Christ died for our sins according to the Scriptures," was buried, "was raised on the third day according to the Scriptures," and appeared — to Cephas, the twelve, over five hundred brothers at once, James, all the apostles, and last of all to Paul, the persecutor made an apostle by grace. Against those saying there is no resurrection of the dead, he runs the logic both ways: if the dead aren't raised, Christ isn't raised, faith is vain, "you are still in your sins," and the fallen asleep have perished — "But now Christ has been raised from the dead," the first fruits, and "as in Adam all die, so also in Christ all will be made alive," each in order — Christ, then those who are Christ's at his coming, then the end, when every enemy including death itself is abolished and God is all in all. To "How are the dead raised?" Paul answers from the seed: what is sown must die, and God gives it its body — "sown perishable... raised imperishable... sown a natural body... raised a spiritual body," bearing the image of the last Adam, the man from heaven. The mystery: not all will sleep, but all will be changed, "in a moment, in the twinkling of an eye, at the last trumpet"; death is swallowed up in victory — "thanks be to God, who gives us the victory through our Lord Jesus Christ. Therefore... be steadfast, immovable... your labor is not in vain in the Lord."
**Tags:** `resurrection` — the chapter is Scripture's fullest resurrection argument: Christ "raised on the third day," the eyewitnesses, and the dead raised imperishable (15:3–8, 12–22, 42–44); `salvation` — the Good News "by which also you are saved," delivered "first of all": Christ died for our sins, was buried, was raised (15:1–4); `the-cross` — "Christ died for our sins according to the Scriptures" — the atoning death at the head of the Good News summary (15:3); `victory-in-christ` — "Death is swallowed up in victory... thanks be to God, who gives us the victory through our Lord Jesus Christ" (15:54–57); `caught-up-together` — "We will not all sleep, but we will all be changed, in a moment, in the twinkling of an eye, at the last trumpet" (15:51–52); `second-coming` — the order of resurrection fixed to his return: "then those who are Christ's at his coming," then the end (15:23–26); `mortality` — the chapter's account of death itself: its origin — "as in Adam all die" (15:21–22); its standing — "The last enemy that will be abolished is death" (15:26); and its end — "Death is swallowed up in victory" (15:54–56); `resurrection-of-the-dead` — the general-resurrection body itself, distinct from Christ's own rising: "So also is the resurrection of the dead. The body is sown perishable; it is raised imperishable. It is sown in dishonor; it is raised in glory. It is sown in weakness; it is raised in power. It is sown a natural body; it is raised a spiritual body." (15:42–44) — Christ the first fruits of the same harvest (15:20–23); both-tags beside `resurrection`, which carries Christ's resurrection and the eyewitness argument (the two ids' boundary; see the Decisions record)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings; bibleproject-framing
**Sections:**
- **15:1–11 — The Resurrection of Christ** (title: BSB) — The received Good News: Christ's death for sins, burial, third-day resurrection according to the Scriptures, and the roll of eyewitnesses ending with Paul himself — least of the apostles, made what he is by the grace of God.
- **15:12–19 — The Resurrection of the Dead** (title: BSB) — If there is no resurrection of the dead, the dominoes fall: Christ not raised, preaching and faith vain, the apostles false witnesses, sins unforgiven, the dead perished, and Christians of all people most pitiable.

## 1 Corinthians 16
**Summary:** Practical instructions close the letter. For the collection for the saints, Paul repeats his rule for the Galatian assemblies: "On the first day of every week, let each one of you save as he may prosper," so no last-minute gathering is needed when he arrives to send their gift to Jerusalem with approved carriers. His plans: through Macedonia, hoping to stay a while — perhaps the winter — with them, but remaining in Ephesus until Pentecost, "for a great and effective door has opened to me, and there are many adversaries." He commends Timothy ("see that he is with you without fear"), notes that Apollos will come when he has opportunity, and urges submission to proven servants like the household of Stephanas, whose arrival with Fortunatus and Achaicus refreshed his spirit. The farewell stacks up its charges — "Watch! Stand firm in the faith! Be courageous! Be strong! Let all that you do be done in love" — before greetings from the assemblies of Asia and from Aquila and Priscilla, Paul's greeting in his own hand, the cry "Come, Lord!", and grace and love to all in Christ Jesus.
**Tags:** `generosity` — the collection for the saints: weekly, proportionate setting-aside, "as he may prosper," carried as "your gracious gift to Jerusalem" (16:1–3); `benediction` — the letter's closing blessing in Paul's own hand: "The grace of the Lord Jesus Christ be with you. My love to all of you in Christ Jesus" (16:21–24)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings
**Sections:**
- **16:1–4 — The Collection for the Saints** (title: BSB) — The weekly, first-day, as-you-prosper pattern for the Jerusalem collection, with approved messengers — and Paul himself if appropriate — to carry the gift.
- **16:5–12 — Paul's Travel Plans, Timothy, and Apollos** (title: adapted from BSB) — Paul hopes to come through Macedonia and stay a while, the Lord permitting, but holds at Ephesus until Pentecost where a great door stands open; Timothy should be received without fear, and Apollos will come when he has opportunity.
- **16:13–24 — Concluding Exhortations and Final Greetings** (title: adapted from BSB) — Watchfulness, firmness, courage, strength, and love; honor for the household of Stephanas and those like them; greetings from Asia, Aquila, and Priscilla; Paul's own-hand signature, the "Come, Lord!" cry, and grace.
