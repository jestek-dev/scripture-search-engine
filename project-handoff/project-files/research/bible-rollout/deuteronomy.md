# Deuteronomy — book summary

## Short

Deuteronomy is Moses' farewell. Israel is camped on the plains of Moab with the promised land in sight across the Jordan, and the old man who cannot enter it preaches the covenant to the children of the generation that refused it. He retells forty years of failure and faithfulness, repeats the Ten Commandments, and presses everything down to one command: "Hear, Israel: The LORD is our God. The LORD is one. You shall love the LORD your God with all your heart, with all your soul, and with all your might." A long code of laws shapes what that love looks like in the land — one place of worship, honest courts, open hands to the poor. Then blessing and curse are set side by side, the people are told to choose life, and Moses climbs a mountain, sees the land, and dies — "there has not arisen a prophet in Israel like Moses, whom the LORD knew face to face."

## Full

Deuteronomy means "second law," but it is not a new law so much as the old covenant preached to a new generation. The people Moses addresses were children when their parents turned back at Kadesh Barnea; now, "in the fortieth year," they stand where their parents stood, and Moses insists the covenant is theirs: "The LORD didn't make this covenant with our fathers, but with us, even us, who are all of us here alive today" (5:3). The whole book is a series of farewell addresses delivered in one place — beyond the Jordan, in the land of Moab — by a leader who knows he will die before the crossing.

The first address (chapters 1–4) retells the journey: the command to leave Horeb (the book's usual name for Sinai), the spy rebellion and its forty-year sentence, the deaths of the old generation, and the first victories over Sihon and Og east of the Jordan. The retrospective lands as a warning — a God so near, and a people so quick to forget — capped by the plea to obey and the reminder that "the LORD himself is God in heaven above and on the earth beneath. There is no one else" (4:39). The second address begins with the covenant's heart (chapters 5–11): the Ten Commandments repeated from Sinai, the Shema and the command to love God totally, the warning that election was love and not merit — "Not for your righteousness" (9:5) — and blessing and curse set before the people. Then comes the law code itself (chapters 12–26): one chosen place of worship, purging idolatry, clean foods and tithes, the seventh-year release of debts and servants, the pilgrim feasts, judges, kings, priests, and prophets, and a long run of case laws that keep bending toward the vulnerable — the Levite, the foreigner, the fatherless, and the widow.

Deuteronomy's recurring word is *remember* — remember the slavery, the rescue, the manna, the mountain that burned with fire — because its recurring fear is forgetting: "lest, when you have eaten and are full... your heart might be lifted up, and you forget the LORD your God" (8:12–14). Obedience in this book is never a bargain for blessing; it is the fitting answer of a redeemed people to the God who "set his love on you" when they were "the fewest of all peoples" (7:7). The covenant is then formally renewed (chapters 27–30): blessings and extended curses pronounced from twin mountains, exile foreseen, restoration promised — God himself will circumcise hearts to love him — and the choice stated plainly: "I have set before you life and death, the blessing and the curse. Therefore choose life" (30:19).

The book closes with succession and a death (chapters 31–34). Joshua is commissioned, the law is written down and deposited beside the ark, and Moses leaves Israel a song that will testify against them and a blessing that shelters them — "The eternal God is your dwelling place. Underneath are the everlasting arms" (33:27). Then Moses climbs Nebo, sees the whole land he cannot enter, and dies at the LORD's word; he is buried in the valley in Moab, and no one knows his grave. Israel stands at the Jordan with the law in its ears and the land ahead, and the story leans forward into Joshua.

# The thirty-four chapters

## Deuteronomy 1
**Summary:** On the plains of Moab, forty years after the exodus, Moses begins his farewell address by retelling the road that led here. At Horeb God said the people had stayed long enough and set the land before them; Moses, unable to carry the growing nation alone, appointed wise and respected men as captains and judges, charged to judge righteously and show no partiality. But at Kadesh Barnea the story broke: the spies brought back good fruit and a good report of the land, yet the people believed their brothers' fear over God's promise, murmured that the LORD hated them, and refused to go up. The sentence followed — none of that evil generation would see the land except Caleb and Joshua — and a late, presumptuous attack without the LORD ended in defeat at Hormah and unheeded weeping.
**Tags:** `fear-not` — the repeated charge at the border: "Don't be afraid, neither be dismayed" and "Don't be terrified. Don't be afraid of them. The LORD your God, who goes before you, he will fight for you" (1:21, 29–30); `doubt` — the rebellion's root named twice: "Where are we going up? Our brothers have made our heart melt," and "in this thing you didn't believe the LORD your God" (1:28, 32); `sin` — rebellion against the commandment, the confession "We have sinned against the LORD," and a presumption that repeats the disobedience in reverse (1:26, 41, 43); `divine-judgment` — the oath that "not one of these men of this evil generation shall see the good land," the rout at Hormah, and weeping the LORD didn't listen to (1:34–40, 44–45); `justice-and-oppression` — the judicial charge at the founding: judges must hear small and great alike and show no partiality, "for the judgment is God's" (1:16–17); `leadership` — the load shared at the start: wise, respected men set over thousands, hundreds, fifties, and tens, charged to judge righteously (1:9–18)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings
**Sections:**
- **1:1–8 — The Command to Leave Horeb** (title: BSB) — Moses opens his address beyond the Jordan: at Horeb the LORD declared the waiting over — "Go in and possess the land which the LORD swore to your fathers."
- **1:9–18 — Moses Appoints Leaders** (title: BSB) — Unable to bear the people alone, Moses set wise, respected men over thousands, hundreds, fifties, and tens, and charged the judges to hear small and great alike, "for the judgment is God's."
- **1:19–46 — The Spies, the Rebellion, and the Penalty** (title: adapted from BSB) — At Kadesh Barnea the twelve spies confirmed the land was good, but the people refused to go up; the LORD swore that generation out of the land, and a presumptuous counterattack was crushed at Hormah.
**Motifs (candidates):** eleven days' journey that became forty years (1:2–3) — "eleven day journey forty years", "why did Israel wander so long"; God carried you "as a man carries his son" (1:31) — "God as a father in the Old Testament", "God carries us".

## Deuteronomy 2
**Summary:** Moses retells the long detour: Israel circled Mount Seir "many days" until God said, "You have encircled this mountain long enough. Turn northward." Passing Edom, Moab, and Ammon, Israel was forbidden to take a foot of their land, because the LORD had given Seir to Esau and Ar to the children of Lot — the God of Israel assigns other nations their homes too. Thirty-eight years pass in two verses, until the whole generation of the men of war was consumed, "the LORD's hand was against them." Then the tone turns: God gives Sihon king of Heshbon — whose spirit the LORD hardened — into Israel's hand after he refuses a peaceable passage, and the conquest east of the Jordan begins. Through it all, Moses reminds them, "These forty years, the LORD your God has been with you. You have lacked nothing."
**Tags:** `gods-provision` — the wilderness verdict: "the LORD your God has blessed you in all the work of your hands... These forty years, the LORD your God has been with you. You have lacked nothing" (2:7); `nations-and-peoples` — lands deeded by God to other peoples: "I have given Mount Seir to Esau for a possession," Ar to the children of Lot, with the chapter's asides on the Emim, Horites, and Zamzummim each dispossessed in their turn (2:5, 9, 12, 19–23); `divine-judgment` — the Kadesh sentence carried out to the last man: "the LORD's hand was against them, to destroy them from the middle of the camp, until they were consumed" (2:14–16); `hardness-of-heart` — the hardening set on God's side of the story: Sihon's spirit hardened by the LORD, so that he refuses a peaceable passage and is delivered into Israel's hand (2:30)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings
**Motifs (candidates):** God gives other nations their lands (2:5, 9, 19) — "does God care about other nations", "God and the nations in the Old Testament"; a hardened king delivered up (2:30) — "why did God harden Sihon's heart".

## Deuteronomy 3
**Summary:** The retrospective's second victory: Og king of Bashan, last of the Rephaim, marches out at Edrei, and God says, "Don't fear him; for I have delivered him, with all his people and his land, into your hand." Sixty fortified cities fall, and the whole land from the Arnon to Mount Hermon is taken and parceled out east of the Jordan — to Reuben, Gad, and half of Manasseh — on the condition that their fighting men cross over armed ahead of their brothers until the LORD gives them rest too. Then Moses turns personal: he begged to cross and see the good land, and the LORD, angry with him on the people's account, refused — "That is enough! Speak no more to me of this matter" — granting him instead a view from Pisgah and the charge to commission and strengthen Joshua.
**Tags:** `fear-not` — "Don't fear him; for I have delivered him... into your hand," and the charge to Joshua, "You shall not fear them; for the LORD your God himself fights for you" (3:2, 22); `gods-protection` — victory credited to God alone: "So the LORD our God also delivered into our hand Og, the king of Bashan," and Joshua is pointed to it as the pattern of God's keeping in the battles ahead: "Your eyes have seen all that the LORD your God has done to these two kings. So shall the LORD do to all the kingdoms where you go over" (3:3, 21); `prayer` — Moses' plea in full: "I begged GOD at that time... Please let me go over and see the good land" — a real prayer, honestly answered with a no, a view from the mountain, and a task (3:23–28)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings
**Sections:**
- **3:1–11 — The Defeat of Og** (title: BSB) — Og of Bashan comes out to battle and is destroyed as Sihon was; sixty walled cities are taken, and the aside about Og's iron bedstead marks the last of the Rephaim.
- **3:12–22 — Land Division East of the Jordan** (title: BSB) — The conquered land is assigned to Reuben, Gad, and half-Manasseh; their men of valor must cross armed before their brothers until the LORD gives rest to all.
- **3:23–29 — Moses Forbidden to Cross the Jordan** (title: BSB) — Moses begs to enter the land; the LORD refuses, grants him the view from Pisgah, and directs him to commission and encourage Joshua.
**Motifs (candidates):** when God says no to a prayer (3:23–27) — "when God says no", "unanswered prayer in the Bible"; rest promised to the whole family before any tribe settles down (3:20) — "no rest until everyone is home".

## Deuteronomy 4
**Summary:** The retrospective becomes a sermon: "Now, Israel, listen to the statutes and to the ordinances... that you may live." Israel must neither add to the word nor take from it, and its obedience will be its wisdom in the world's eyes — for "what great nation is there that has a god so near to them as the LORD our God is whenever we call on him?" Moses recalls Horeb, where the people heard a voice out of the fire but saw no form, and turns that memory into the great warning against idols: no carved image, no worship of sun, moon, or stars, "for the LORD your God is a devouring fire, a jealous God." He foresees the far future — corruption, scattering among the nations — and beyond it a promise: from there you will find him "when you search after him with all your heart," for "the LORD your God is a merciful God" who will never forget the covenant sworn to the fathers. The chapter closes with two structural seams: three cities of refuge set apart east of the Jordan, and the formal heading that introduces the law.
**Tags:** `obedience-to-the-word` — the sermon's frame: "listen to the statutes and to the ordinances... to do them, that you may live," with the charge to neither add nor take away (4:1–2, 5–6, 40); `presence-of-god` — "what great nation is there that has a god so near to them as the LORD our God is whenever we call on him?" (4:7); `repentance` — the promise inside the exile warning: "you shall seek the LORD your God, and you will find him when you search after him with all your heart and with all your soul... you shall return to the LORD your God and listen to his voice" (4:29–30); `gods-faithfulness` — "the LORD your God is a merciful God. He will not fail you nor destroy you, nor forget the covenant of your fathers which he swore to them" (4:31); `divine-judgment` — heaven and earth called to witness: idolatry will end in perishing from the land and scattering among the peoples (4:26–28); `idolatry` — the Horeb memory turned against images: Israel saw no form, so no carved image, no worship of sun, moon, or stars, "for the LORD your God is a devouring fire, a jealous God" (4:15–28); `cities-of-refuge` — the institution's founding act east of the Jordan: Bezer, Ramoth, and Golan set apart, where a man who kills his neighbor unintentionally may flee and live (4:41–43); `no-other-god` — the stated lesson of the whole retrospective (4:32–40), declared at 4:35 and sealed in the closing charge: "the LORD himself is God in heaven above and on the earth beneath. There is no one else" (4:35, 39)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings
**Sections:**
- **4:1–40 — An Exhortation to Obedience** (title: BSB) — One sustained appeal: obey and live, remember the fire at Horeb where no form was seen, make no image, and know that "the LORD himself is God in heaven above and on the earth beneath. There is no one else."
- **4:41–43 — Cities of Refuge** (title: BSB) — Moses sets apart Bezer, Ramoth, and Golan east of the Jordan, where a man who kills his neighbor unintentionally may flee and live.
- **4:44–49 — Introduction to the Law** (title: BSB) — A formal superscription locates the law-giving: beyond the Jordan, opposite Beth Peor, in the conquered lands of Sihon and Og.
**Motifs (candidates):** a God heard but never seen (4:12, 15) — "why can't we see God", "worship without images"; "a devouring fire, a jealous God" (4:24) — "consuming fire in the Bible", "is God jealous"; no god has ever done this (4:32–34) — "what makes Israel's God different".

## Deuteronomy 5
**Summary:** Moses summons all Israel and hands the covenant to the living: "The LORD didn't make this covenant with our fathers, but with us, even us, who are all of us here alive today." He repeats the Ten Commandments spoken at Horeb out of the fire — no other gods, no carved image, no misuse of the name, the Sabbath kept holy (grounded here in the exodus: "remember that you were a servant in the land of Egypt"), honor for father and mother, and the protections of life, marriage, property, truth, and a neighbor's household from covetous desire. The people, terrified by the voice and the burning mountain, beg Moses to stand between them and God, and the LORD approves their words with an ache in them: "Oh that there were such a heart in them that they would fear me and keep all my commandments always, that it might be well with them and with their children forever!"
**Tags:** `the-ten-commandments` — the commandments themselves, repeated in full to the new generation (5:6–21); `covenant` — "The LORD our God made a covenant with us in Horeb," claimed for the living generation, not the dead (5:2–3); `sabbath-rest` — the Sabbath command with its rest extended to son, daughter, servants, livestock, and stranger — "that your male servant and your female servant may rest as well as you" (5:12–15); `caring-for-aging-parents` — "Honor your father and your mother, as the LORD your God commanded you, that your days may be long" (5:16); `obedience-to-the-word` — the chapter's closing charge and God's own longing: "Oh that there were such a heart in them... You shall walk in all the way which the LORD your God has commanded you" (5:29, 32–33); `idolatry` — the covenant's first table renewed to the living generation: no other gods, no carved image, no bowing down to serve them (5:7–9)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings
**Sections:**
- **5:1–4 — The Covenant at Horeb** (title: BSB) — Moses assembles Israel and declares that the Horeb covenant belongs to this generation: "The LORD spoke with you face to face on the mountain out of the middle of the fire."
- **5:5–21 — The Ten Commandments** (title: BSB) — With Moses standing between the LORD and a fearful people, the ten words are repeated: exclusive loyalty to the God of the exodus, and the ordering of worship, time, family, and neighbor.
- **5:22–33 — Moses Intercedes for the People** (title: BSB) — The people, sure the fire will consume them, ask Moses to hear God for them; the LORD approves the request, wishes them such a heart always, and gives Moses the commandments to teach.
**Motifs (candidates):** "God does speak with man, and he lives" (5:24) — "can anyone hear God and live"; the asked-for mediator (5:25–28) — "why did Israel need a mediator", "go-between with God".

## Deuteronomy 6
**Summary:** The commandments narrow to their center. "Hear, Israel: The LORD is our God. The LORD is one. You shall love the LORD your God with all your heart, with all your soul, and with all your might" — the confession and command later quoted as the greatest commandment. These words are to live everywhere: on the heart, taught diligently to children, talked of at home and on the road, lying down and rising up, bound on the hand, worn as frontlets (bands on the forehead) between the eyes, written on doorposts and gates. The danger Moses names is not hardship but arrival: cities you didn't build, houses full of good things you didn't fill, cisterns you didn't dig — "then beware lest you forget the LORD, who brought you out of the land of Egypt." Israel must fear, serve, and not test the LORD as at Massah, and when a son asks what all the statutes mean, the answer is a story: "We were Pharaoh's slaves in Egypt. The LORD brought us out of Egypt with a mighty hand."
**Tags:** `delight-in-the-word` — the words kept close around the clock: "These words... shall be on your heart," talked of "when you sit in your house, and when you walk by the way, and when you lie down, and when you rise up," bound, and written on the doorposts (6:6–9); `parenting` — "you shall teach them diligently to your children," and the scripted answer to a son's question, handing the exodus story down a generation (6:7, 20–25); `obedience-to-the-word` — "You shall diligently keep the commandments of the LORD your God," commanded "for our good always, that he might preserve us alive" (6:3, 17–18, 24); `loving-god` — the Shema itself: "Hear, Israel: The LORD is our God. The LORD is one. You shall love the LORD your God with all your heart, with all your soul, and with all your might" (6:4–5); `no-other-god` — the Shema's confession of the LORD's oneness, a register distinct from the love command beside it: "Hear, Israel: The LORD is our God. The LORD is one" (6:4)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings
**Motifs (candidates):** the Shema (6:4–9) — "shema meaning", "greatest commandment", "love the LORD your God with all your heart"; wells you didn't dig (6:10–12) — "blessings you didn't earn", "forgetting God in prosperity"; "You shall not tempt the LORD your God" (6:16) — "putting God to the test".

## Deuteronomy 7
**Summary:** When the LORD brings Israel in, seven nations greater and mightier must be utterly destroyed — no covenant, no mercy, no intermarriage — and their altars, pillars, Asherah poles, and engraved images demolished. The command is total because the stakes are total: "For you are a holy people to the LORD your God," and Canaan's worship would turn Israel's sons to other gods. Then comes the book's clearest word on election: the LORD did not choose Israel for its size — "you were the fewest of all peoples" — "but because the LORD loves you," and because he keeps the oath sworn to the fathers; he is "the faithful God, who keeps covenant and loving kindness to a thousand generations." Within that covenant, obedience carries promised blessing — fruitfulness of body, ground, and flock, and diseases of Egypt withheld — stated as the covenant's own terms to Israel in its land, not a general bargain. And the conquest itself will be God's, "little by little," so a fearful people need only remember what he did to Pharaoh. Everything devoted (the WEB's term for what is irrevocably given over to God, here for destruction) must be utterly detested, not carried home.
**Tags:** `gods-love` — election explained from inside God's heart: "The LORD didn't set his love on you nor choose you, because you were more in number... but because the LORD loves you" (7:7–8); `gods-faithfulness` — "the faithful God, who keeps covenant and loving kindness to a thousand generations with those who love him and keep his commandments" (7:9); `covenant` — the sworn oath to the fathers kept in the exodus and carried forward: "the LORD your God will keep with you the covenant and the loving kindness which he swore to your fathers" (7:8–9, 12); `holiness` — the ground of separation: "For you are a holy people to the LORD your God. The LORD your God has chosen you to be a people for his own possession" (7:6); `fear-not` — "If you shall say in your heart, 'These nations are more than I'... you shall not be afraid of them. ## Deuteronomy 19
**Summary:** Israel must set apart three cities of refuge in the land — with roads prepared and the territory divided so that one is always within reach — "that every man slayer may flee there." The law walks through the case: a man whose ax head flies off and kills his neighbor "unintentionally, and didn't hate him in time past" can run to the city and live, safe from "the avenger of blood" whose hot anger might overtake him on a long road; three more cities are to be added if God enlarges the border, "so that innocent blood will not be shed" in the land. ## Deuteronomy 31

## Deuteronomy 32
**Summary:** The Song of Moses summons heaven and earth to listen and moves through Israel's whole story — past and future — in poetry. It opens in praise: "The Rock: his work is perfect, for all his ways are just. A God of faithfulness who does no wrong, just and right is he." Then the turn: "Jeshurun" — a poetic name for Israel — "grew fat, and kicked," abandoned his Maker, and "sacrificed to demons, not God"; so the LORD hides his face, provoking with "a foolish nation" the people who provoked him with "that which is not God," and heaping on them fire, famine, plague, and sword — restrained only so Israel's enemies cannot boast, "Our hand is exalted." Moses urges Israel to take the words to heart — "it is no vain thing for you, because it is your life." The same day, the LORD sends Moses up Mount Nebo to see the land and die on the mountain, as Aaron did on Hor, "because you didn't uphold my holiness among the children of Israel" at Meribah.
