# Daniel — book summary

## Short

Daniel opens with Jerusalem's treasures carried into a conqueror's temple and spends twelve chapters proving the conquest an illusion: "the Most High rules in the kingdom of men, and gives it to whomever he will" (4:25). The first half is court narrative — four exiled young men keep faith inside the Babylonian machine, and God honors them through a forbidden-food test, a dream no magician can tell, a furnace, a hand that writes on the palace wall, and a den of lions, until pagan emperors themselves decree the praise of the God of heaven. The second half is Daniel's own visions: kingdoms rise from the sea like animals and fall, an Ancient of Days takes his seat in judgment, "one like a son of man" receives an everlasting dominion (7:13–14), and history runs on a decreed schedule to "the time of the end." At the center stands a man who prays three times a day with his windows open toward a ruined Jerusalem, and at the end stands the Bible's clearest early word of resurrection: "Many of those who sleep in the dust of the earth will awake, some to everlasting life, and some to shame and everlasting contempt" (12:2).

## Full

Daniel is a book about staying faithful when the empire holds every visible lever of power — and about the God who holds the invisible ones. It begins in the third year of Jehoiakim, when Nebuchadnezzar besieges Jerusalem and "The Lord gave Jehoiakim king of Judah into his hand" (1:2) — even the catastrophe is the Lord's doing. Daniel and his three companions are deported, renamed, and enrolled in the imperial academy, and the book's two halves then tell one story from two sides: chapters 1–6 show God ruling the kingdoms of men from inside the court's daily dramas, and chapters 7–12 show him ruling them across the whole sweep of history in visions given to Daniel himself.

The court narratives run on a repeated rhythm: the empire demands what faith cannot give, the faithful refuse quietly and take the consequences, and God vindicates them so plainly that the king ends up preaching. Daniel declines the king's delicacies and is made "ten times better" than every magician in the realm (1:20); he tells Nebuchadnezzar both the forgotten dream and its meaning, because "there is a God in heaven who reveals secrets" (2:28); Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego walk out of the fiery furnace unsinged, having told the king that God "is able to deliver us" — and that they would not serve his gods even if he did not (3:17–18); Nebuchadnezzar is driven from men to eat grass like oxen until he lifts his eyes to heaven and confesses that "those who walk in pride he is able to abase" (4:37); Belshazzar toasts his idols from the temple's vessels and is weighed in the balances that same night (5:27, 30); and Daniel, rather than close his windows for thirty days, kneels three times a day "as he did before" (6:10) and spends a night among lions whose mouths God shuts. Again and again the stories end in the king's own words — a confession (2:47), a decree (3:29; 6:25–27), an open letter (4:1–3, 34–37) — the book's most subversive feature: the empire's own rulers keep declaring the sovereignty of Israel's God.

The visions widen the same claim to all of history. Four great animals rise from a storm-tossed sea — a lion, a bear, a leopard, and a terrifying fourth with iron teeth and a little horn "speaking arrogantly" — but the scene Daniel cannot look away from is a courtroom: thrones placed, the Ancient of Days seated, and "one like a son of man" coming with the clouds to receive a dominion that will never pass away (7:9–14). A ram and a male goat replay the rise and shattering of "Media and Persia" and "Greece" — this time named by the text itself (8:20–21) — and a fierce king who magnifies himself is "broken without human hands" (8:25). Chapter 9 turns from receiving visions to praying: Daniel, reading Jeremiah's seventy years, fasts and confesses the nation's sin in the Bible's model prayer of corporate repentance, and Gabriel answers with the mysterious decree of seventy weeks. The final vision (chapters 10–12) pulls back the curtain twice — once on an unseen conflict, where the messenger is delayed by "the prince of the kingdom of Persia" until Michael comes to help (10:13), and once on the long wars of the kings of the south and north, through desecration and persecution, to a self-exalting king who "will come to his end, and no one will help him" (11:45).

The book refuses to end in the wars. Michael stands up, the worst trouble in history breaks, and then the promise: the dead awake — some to everlasting life — the wise "shine as the brightness of the expanse" and those who turn many to righteousness "as the stars forever and ever" (12:3), and Daniel is told to seal the book and go his way, "for you will rest, and will stand in your inheritance at the end of the days" (12:13). Daniel never explains its timetables, and this document does not pretend to; what the book repeats past all misunderstanding is its center: God reveals mysteries, God removes kings and sets up kings, God delivers those who trust him — sometimes from the fire, and finally through it, all the way to resurrection.

# The twelve chapters

## Daniel 1
**Summary:** In the third year of Jehoiakim, Nebuchadnezzar besieges Jerusalem, and "The Lord gave Jehoiakim king of Judah into his hand" — the exile begins as God's own act, not Babylon's triumph (1:1–2). Four young Judean nobles — Daniel, Hananiah, Mishael, and Azariah — are carried off, given Babylonian names, and enrolled in a three-year royal training with a menu from the king's table. Daniel "purposed in his heart that he would not defile himself" with the delicacies and wine (1:8), and proposes a ten-day test on vegetables and water; at its end the four look healthier than every youth who ate the king's food. God, who had already made Daniel find "kindness and compassion" with the chief official (1:9), gives the four "knowledge and skill in all learning and wisdom" (1:17), and the king finds them "ten times better" than all his magicians and enchanters (1:20). A closing note — Daniel continues to the first year of King Cyrus — quietly spans the whole exile the book will cover.
**Tags:** `providence` — God steers events on both sides of the catastrophe: "The Lord gave Jehoiakim king of Judah into his hand" (1:2), and "God made Daniel find kindness and compassion in the sight of the prince of the eunuchs" (1:9); `wisdom-from-god` — "God gave them knowledge and skill in all learning and wisdom" (1:17), proven when the king finds them "ten times better" than his experts (1:20); `holiness` — Daniel "purposed in his heart that he would not defile himself" (1:8) — a deliberate set-apartness kept inside a pagan court; `sojourners-and-strangers` — the exile frame the whole book lives in, set here: "The Lord gave Jehoiakim king of Judah into his hand" (1:2), and the four are carried off, given Babylonian names, and enrolled in the conqueror's academy — a life of faithfulness begun entirely as foreigners in a pagan empire (1:1–7)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings

## Daniel 2
**Summary:** Nebuchadnezzar demands the impossible: his wise men must tell him both his troubling dream and its meaning, or die — and the Chaldeans (in this book the word names both the Babylonian nation and its guild of scholar-diviners) protest that only the gods could do it (2:10–11). Facing execution with them, Daniel and his companions "desire mercies of the God of heaven" (2:18), the secret is revealed in a night vision, and Daniel breaks into praise: "Blessed be the name of God forever and ever; for wisdom and might are his... He removes kings and sets up kings" (2:20–21). Before the king he claims nothing for himself — "there is a God in heaven who reveals secrets" (2:28) — then recounts the dream: a huge image with a head of gold, chest of silver, belly of bronze, legs of iron, and feet of iron mixed with clay, struck on its feet by a stone "cut out without hands" that grinds the whole to chaff and "became a great mountain and filled the whole earth" (2:31–35). The interpretation names only the first kingdom — "You are the head of gold" (2:38) — with three unnamed kingdoms to follow (readers have traditionally identified them as Medo-Persia, Greece, and Rome, an identification the chapter itself does not make), until "the God of heaven will set up a kingdom which will never be destroyed" (2:44). Nebuchadnezzar falls on his face, confesses Daniel's God as "the God of gods, and the Lord of kings" (2:47), and promotes Daniel and his friends.
**Tags:** `dreams-and-visions` — Nebuchadnezzar "dreamed dreams; and his spirit was troubled" (2:1), and "the secret was revealed to Daniel in a vision of the night" (2:19); `prayer` — under a death decree the four seek God together, that they "would desire mercies of the God of heaven concerning this secret" (2:17–18), and the answer comes; `wisdom-from-god` — "He gives wisdom to the wise" (2:21), and Daniel gives thanks: "I thank you and praise you, O God of my fathers, who have given me wisdom and might" (2:23), while disclaiming any wisdom of his own (2:30); `providence` — "He changes the times and the seasons. He removes kings and sets up kings" (2:21) — the dream's whole point, sealed by the kingdom God himself will set up (2:44); `praise` — "Then Daniel blessed the God of heaven" and answers with a doxology, "I thank you and praise you, O God of my fathers" (2:19–23); `occult-and-divination` — the chapter's contest: the scholar-diviner guild itself protests that no magician can tell the king his dream (2:10–11), and their failure is answered by "there is a God in heaven who reveals secrets" (2:28) — divination shown empty where God reveals, described and never taught; `god-reigns` — the dream's destination: "the God of heaven will set up a kingdom which will never be destroyed" (2:44)

**Sections:**
- **2:1–13 — Nebuchadnezzar's Troubling Dream** (title: BSB) — The king demands that his wise men recover the dream itself as proof they can interpret it; when they cannot, he orders every wise man in Babylon destroyed — Daniel and his friends included.
- **2:14–23 — The Dream Revealed to Daniel** (title: BSB) — Daniel wins a stay of execution, the four pray, and the secret comes in a night vision; Daniel answers with praise to the God who reveals deep and secret things.
- **2:24–45 — Daniel Interprets the Dream** (title: BSB) — Daniel recounts the image of four metals and the stone cut without hands, names Nebuchadnezzar the head of gold, and declares that after the successive kingdoms the God of heaven will set up a kingdom that will never be destroyed.
- **2:46–49 — Nebuchadnezzar Promotes Daniel** (title: BSB) — The king honors Daniel, confesses his God as God of gods and revealer of secrets, and sets Daniel and his three friends over the affairs of Babylon.

## Daniel 3
**Summary:** Nebuchadnezzar erects a golden image sixty cubits high on the plain of Dura and gathers every official of the empire: at the sound of the music, all peoples must "fall down and worship the golden image" or be thrown into a burning fiery furnace (3:1–6). Certain Chaldeans denounce Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego, and before the furious king the three give the book's most famous answer: "our God whom we serve is able to deliver us from the burning fiery furnace... But if not... we will not serve your gods or worship the golden image which you have set up" (3:17–18) — faith with no conditions attached to the outcome. The furnace is heated seven times hotter, killing the soldiers who throw them in; then the astonished king sees four men walking loose in the fire and says, "The appearance of the fourth is like a son of the gods" (3:25) — his own pagan description of the figure, which he restates afterward as God having "sent his angel" (3:28); Christians have historically seen in that fourth figure an appearance of the Son of God before his birth, a reading the text neither states nor rules out. The three come out with not even the smell of fire on them, and Nebuchadnezzar blesses the God who "delivered his servants who trusted in him", decreeing that none may speak against him, "because there is no other god who is able to deliver like this" (3:28–29).
**Tags:** `faith` — the three stake everything on God without demanding the outcome: he "is able to deliver us... But if not", they still refuse (3:17–18); `worship` — the chapter's whole contest is who may be worshiped: commanded worship of the image (3:5–6, 12) refused so that they "might not serve nor worship any god except their own God" (3:28); `trust-in-god` — the king's own verdict names it: God "delivered his servants who trusted in him" (3:28); `gods-protection` — the fire "had no power on their bodies" and not even its smell was on them (3:27), for "there is no other god who is able to deliver like this" (3:29); `angels` — the king restates what he saw in the furnace as God having "sent his angel" to deliver his servants (3:28) — reported alongside his own pagan description of the fourth figure (3:25), which the entry records without adjudicating; `idolatry` — state-compelled worship of a made image: all peoples must "fall down and worship the golden image" (3:5–6), which the three refuse — "we will not serve your gods or worship the golden image which you have set up" (3:18); `persecuted-for-gods-word` — denounced to the king and thrown into the furnace precisely for their fidelity to God against commanded worship (3:8–23), with faith that attaches no conditions to the outcome (3:17–18)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings

## Daniel 4
**Summary:** This chapter is Nebuchadnezzar's own open letter "to all the peoples, nations, and languages", telling how the Most High humbled him: "His kingdom is an everlasting kingdom" (4:1–3). He dreams of a tree that feeds and shelters the whole earth until "a holy watcher" — the dream's word for a heavenly messenger — comes down and orders it cut to a banded stump, its heart changed to an animal's until "seven times" pass, "to the intent that the living may know that the Most High rules in the kingdom of men, and gives it to whomever he will" (4:10–17). Daniel, dismayed, tells the king he is the tree, and dares to counsel him: "break off your sins by righteousness" (4:27). Twelve months later, boasting "Is not this great Babylon, which I have built" (4:30), the king hears a voice from the sky mid-sentence, and the sentence falls: driven from men, eating grass like oxen, wet with the dew of the sky. At the end of the days he lifts his eyes to heaven, his understanding returns, and the restored king praises the King of heaven with the letter's last word: "those who walk in pride he is able to abase" (4:34–37).
**Tags:** `dreams-and-visions` — a dream and "the visions of my head" trouble the king (4:5, 10–17), and only Daniel can interpret them (4:18–19); `humble-exaltation` — the whole arc: pride at the height of power (4:30), abasement among the animals (4:31–33), and restoration with "excellent greatness... added to me" once he honors God (4:34–37); `divine-judgment` — "The sentence is by the decree of the watchers" (4:17) and "it is the decree of the Most High, which has come on my lord the king" (4:24), fulfilled the same hour it is pronounced (4:31–33); `providence` — the refrain "the Most High rules in the kingdom of men, and gives it to whomever he will" (4:17, 25, 32), and none "can stop his hand" (4:35); `praise` — "I blessed the Most High, and I praised and honored him who lives forever" (4:34), closing with "Now I, Nebuchadnezzar, praise and extol and honor the King of heaven" (4:37); `god-reigns` — the letter opens on the confession itself: "His kingdom is an everlasting kingdom" (4:1–3), the kingship the whole ordeal ends by praising (4:34, 37); `sovereignty-of-god` — the chapter's taught doctrine, distinct from `providence`'s event-steering register: God's active rule over the world's governments, stated as the lesson the living must learn — "the Most High rules in the kingdom of men, and gives it to whomever he will" (4:17, 25, 32); `angels` — "a holy watcher" comes down from the sky bearing the sentence, which stands "by the decree of the watchers" (4:13, 17) — the dream's own heavenly messengers

- **4:4–18 — Nebuchadnezzar's Dream of a Great Tree** (title: BSB) — At ease in his palace, the king dreams of a world-sheltering tree ordered cut down to a banded stump by a holy watcher, so that the living may know the Most High rules in the kingdom of men; his wise men fail, and he tells the dream to Daniel.

## Daniel 5
**Summary:** King Belshazzar throws a great feast for a thousand lords and, warmed with wine, calls for the gold and silver vessels his father Nebuchadnezzar took from the Jerusalem temple, so that his court, wives, and concubines can drink from them while they praise "the gods of gold, and of silver, of bronze, of iron, of wood, and of stone" (5:1–4). In the same hour "the fingers of a man's hand" write on the palace wall, and the king's knees knock together; his wise men can't even read the writing (5:5–9). At the queen's urging Daniel is brought in, waves away the offered rewards, and preaches the king his own family history: the Most High gave Nebuchadnezzar his greatness, humbled him when "his heart was lifted up" (5:20), and "You, his son, Belshazzar, have not humbled your heart, though you knew all this" (5:22) — you have toasted lifeless gods with the vessels of "the God in whose hand your breath is" (5:23). The writing reads "MENE, MENE, TEKEL, UPHARSIN" (5:25): God has counted your kingdom and ended it; "you are weighed in the balances, and are found wanting" (5:27); your kingdom is divided to the Medes and Persians. That night Belshazzar is slain, and Darius the Mede receives the kingdom at about sixty-two years old.
**Tags:** `divine-judgment` — the sentence written by God's own hand and read out — "God has counted your kingdom, and brought it to an end... you are weighed in the balances, and are found wanting" (5:26–27) — is executed that same night (5:30); `humble-exaltation` — Nebuchadnezzar deposed "when his heart was lifted up" (5:20) becomes the warning Belshazzar ignores — Daniel tells him, "You... have not humbled your heart, though you knew all this" (5:22) — and he falls; `providence` — Daniel's sermon grounds the sentence in the God who gives and removes kingdoms: the Most High God "gave Nebuchadnezzar your father the kingdom" (5:18), humbled him "until he knew that the Most High God rules in the kingdom of men, and that he sets up over it whomever he will" (5:21), and now holds Belshazzar's breath and ways in his hand (5:23); `idolatry` — the sacrilege that triggers the sentence: the temple's vessels raised to "the gods of gold, and of silver, of bronze, of iron, of wood, and of stone" (5:4), indicted by Daniel against "the God in whose hand your breath is" (5:23); `sovereignty-of-god` — Daniel's sermon teaches the rule-over-kingdoms doctrine outright: the Most High humbled Nebuchadnezzar "until he knew that the Most High God rules in the kingdom of men, and that he sets up over it whomever he will" (5:21) — the lesson Belshazzar knew and ignored (5:22)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings

## Daniel 6
**Summary:** Under Darius the Mede, Daniel so outshines the other officials — "because an excellent spirit was in him" — that the king plans to set him over the whole realm (6:1–3). His rivals can find no charge, "because he was faithful" (6:4), so they aim at the one thing they can count on: "the law of his God" (6:5). Flattering Darius into a thirty-day decree that petitions may go to no god or man but the king, they catch Daniel doing what he has always done: windows open toward Jerusalem, "he kneeled on his knees three times a day, and prayed, and gave thanks before his God, as he did before" (6:10). The trapped king labors till sundown to save him, then must seal Daniel into the den of lions, telling him, "Your God whom you serve continually, he will deliver you" (6:16); after a sleepless, fasting night Darius runs to the den at dawn and hears Daniel answer: "My God has sent his angel, and has shut the lions' mouths" (6:22). Daniel is lifted out without a scratch "because he had trusted in his God" (6:23), his accusers — with their children and their wives — are thrown to the lions in his place (6:24), and Darius decrees to all the earth that men tremble before the God of Daniel — "for he is the living God, and steadfast forever" (6:26).
**Tags:** `prayer` — Daniel's fixed discipline is the story's hinge: kneeling "three times a day" in prayer and thanksgiving "as he did before", decree or no decree (6:10–11); `gods-protection` — "My God has sent his angel, and has shut the lions' mouths" (6:22), so that "no kind of harm was found on him" (6:23); `trust-in-god` — the narrator's own verdict: he was unharmed "because he had trusted in his God" (6:23), the God Darius twice calls the one Daniel serves "continually" (6:16, 20); `work-and-diligence` — Daniel is "distinguished above the presidents" through an excellent spirit, and his enemies comb his public service and find "no occasion or fault, because he was faithful" (6:3–4); `persecuted-for-gods-word` — a law engineered specifically against Daniel's fidelity: his rivals can find no charge except "the law of his God" (6:5), and he is sealed into the den for praying as he always had (6:10–17); `leadership` — faithful administration under a pagan crown: one of three presidents over the realm, "distinguished above the presidents", his service so clean that scrutiny finds "no occasion or fault" (6:1–4) — the governing register alongside `work-and-diligence`'s working one; `angels` — the deliverance named in Daniel's own words: "My God has sent his angel, and has shut the lions' mouths" (6:22); `god-reigns` — Darius decrees to all the earth that men tremble before the God of Daniel, "for he is the living God, and steadfast forever" (6:26), his kingdom and dominion declared unending in the decree's own words

## Daniel 7
**Summary:** Dated to the first year of Belshazzar — the book steps back in time here from the events of chapters 5–6 — Daniel now becomes the dreamer: in a night vision the four winds churn the great sea and four great animals (the familiar "beasts") come up, each different — a lion with eagle's wings, a lopsided bear with ribs in its teeth, a four-headed, four-winged leopard, and a fourth, "awesome, powerful, and exceedingly strong", with iron teeth and ten horns, among which a little horn rises with human eyes and "a mouth speaking arrogantly" (7:2–8). Then the scene turns to a courtroom: thrones placed, "one who was Ancient of Days sat", clothed in white, on a throne of fiery flames, with ten thousand times ten thousand standing before him — "The judgment was set. The books were opened." (7:9–10). The arrogant animal is slain, and "there came with the clouds of the sky one like a son of man", who is given everlasting dominion, "that all the peoples, nations, and languages should serve him" (7:13–14); Christians have historically heard in this scene the title Jesus most often used for himself, "the Son of Man". An attendant interprets: the four animals are four kings, "but the saints of the Most High will receive the kingdom" (7:17–18); the fourth kingdom's final horn "made war with the saints, and prevailed against them" and will speak against the Most High, and the saints "will be given into his hand until a time and times and half a time" (7:25) — until the court sits, his dominion is destroyed, and the everlasting kingdom passes to the saints (7:21–27). The chapter names none of the kingdoms — readers have traditionally matched them with the four of chapter 2, an identification the text leaves unstated — and Daniel keeps the matter, troubled, in his heart.
**Tags:** `dreams-and-visions` — "Daniel had a dream and visions of his head while on his bed. (7:9–10), the arrogant horn's dominion is taken away and destroyed (7:11, 26); `providence` — dominion is throughout given and removed from above — "dominion was given to it" (7:6), the animals' dominion "was taken away" (7:12), and the everlasting kingdom is granted from the throne to the son of man and to the saints (7:14, 27); `god-reigns` — the throne scene issues in unending kingship: dominion given "that all the peoples, nations, and languages should serve him" (7:14), and "the saints of the Most High will receive the kingdom" (7:18) when the everlasting kingdom passes to them (7:27); `messianic-prophecy` — "there came with the clouds of the sky one like a son of man", given everlasting dominion (7:13–14): the enthronement scene this entry already signposts as the one Christians have historically heard behind Jesus' own chosen title, carried on the text's own terms with no identification asserted (see Decisions record #10)

## Daniel 8
**Summary:** Two years later, Daniel sees himself in the citadel of Susa by the river Ulai: a two-horned ram — one horn higher, and rising later — pushes west, north, and south, doing as it pleases, until a male goat with one notable horn flies in from the west without touching the ground and tramples it (8:1–7). The goat "magnified himself exceedingly", but at the height of its strength the great horn breaks, four horns rise toward the four winds, and out of one comes a little horn that grows great toward "the glorious land", exalts itself against the very prince of heaven's army, takes away the continual burnt offering, and throws truth to the ground — for "two thousand and three hundred evenings and mornings", until the sanctuary is cleansed (8:8–14). This time the interpretation is in the text: a voice commands, "Gabriel, make this man understand the vision" (8:16), and Gabriel names the kingdoms — the ram is "the kings of Media and Persia", the goat "the king of Greece", its four horns four kingdoms from a broken first king (8:20–22) — and foretells "a king of fierce face" whose cunning destroys many, who even stands up against the prince of princes, "but he will be broken without human hands" (8:23–25). The vision belongs to "the time of the end", is to be sealed up, and leaves Daniel sick for days — he does the king's business, but no one understands the vision.
**Tags:** `dreams-and-visions` — "a vision appeared to me, even to me, Daniel" (8:1), interpreted at God's command by Gabriel — "make this man understand the vision" (8:16); `humble-exaltation` — powers that magnify themselves are broken from above: the goat "magnified himself exceedingly" and its great horn snapped at full strength (8:8), and the fierce king who magnifies "himself in his heart... will be broken without human hands" (8:25); `end-times` — the vision's own stated horizon, in the text's own vocabulary for the appointed final period: it belongs to "the time of the end" (8:17) and is sealed up accordingly (8:26); `angels` — Gabriel, the Bible's first named angel, commanded to interpret: "Gabriel, make this man understand the vision" (8:16)

## Daniel 9
**Summary:** In the first year of Darius, Daniel works out from "the books" — the LORD's word to Jeremiah — that Jerusalem's desolations are to last seventy years, and he turns to the Lord "with fasting and sackcloth and ashes" (9:1–3). His prayer is the Old Testament's model of confession, and it fills most of the chapter: he addresses the "great and dreadful God, who keeps covenant and loving kindness with those who love him and keep his commandments" (9:4), owns the nation's guilt without excuse — "we have sinned, and have dealt perversely, and have done wickedly, and have rebelled" (9:5) — grants that God is righteous in all the disaster that the law of Moses warned of, and grounds every plea not in Israel's merit but in God's character: "To the Lord our God belong mercies and forgiveness" (9:9), we plead "for your great mercies' sake" (9:18), "Lord, hear. Lord, forgive. Lord, listen and do." (9:19). While he is still speaking, Gabriel flies to him at the time of the evening offering with an answer and an astonishing aside: "you are greatly beloved" (9:23). The answer is the famous decree: "Seventy weeks are decreed on your people and on your holy city" to finish disobedience, make an end of sins, make reconciliation for iniquity, and "bring in everlasting righteousness" (9:24); an Anointed One, the prince, comes after seven and sixty-two weeks and then "will be cut off, and will have nothing" (9:25–26); city and sanctuary are destroyed, and in the final week sacrifice ceases and a desolator comes until the decreed end. Christians have historically read the Anointed One as pointing to Jesus; the many schemes for calculating the weeks divide careful readers, and this summary endorses none of them.
**Tags:** `prayer` — the chapter is one long recorded prayer, sought "by prayer and petitions, with fasting" (9:3) and answered while still being spoken (9:20–21); `repentance` — corporate confession without self-defense: "we have sinned... and have rebelled, even turning aside from your precepts" (9:5), with the acknowledged need to "turn from our iniquities" (9:13); `forgiveness-of-sins` — the prayer's whole basis: "To the Lord our God belong mercies and forgiveness" (9:9), rising to "Lord, hear. Lord, forgive." (9:19); `covenant` — addressed to the God "who keeps covenant and loving kindness" (9:4), whose covenant curses "written in the law of Moses" have been justly poured out (9:11–13); `fasting` — the prayer is sought "with fasting and sackcloth and ashes" (9:3), the fast that serves confession — the register lay queries about a "Daniel fast" reach for; `angels` — Gabriel flies to Daniel at the time of the evening offering with the answer and the aside "you are greatly beloved" (9:21–23); `messianic-prophecy` — the decree's Anointed One, the prince, who after the sixty-two weeks "will be cut off, and will have nothing" (9:25–26) — carried as this entry's own signposted reading, with no calculation scheme endorsed (see Decisions record #11)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings
**Sections:**
- **9:1–19 — Daniel's Prayer for His People** (title: BSB) — Reading Jeremiah's seventy years, Daniel fasts and prays the great confession: the nation has sinned, God is righteous in all that has come, and every plea for Jerusalem rests on God's mercies and God's name, not Israel's righteousness.
- **9:20–27 — Gabriel's Prophecy of the Seventy Weeks** (title: BSB) — While Daniel is still praying, Gabriel arrives with the decree of seventy weeks — restoration, an Anointed One cut off, the city and sanctuary destroyed, and desolations determined until the decreed end.

## Daniel 10
**Summary:** In the third year of Cyrus, after mourning and fasting "three whole weeks" (10:2–3), Daniel stands by the great river Hiddekel (the river we know as the Tigris) and sees "a man clothed in linen", his waist adorned with pure gold, his body like beryl, "his face as the appearance of lightning", his voice like a multitude (10:4–6). His companions flee without seeing it; Daniel's strength drains away, and he falls face-down (10:7–9); a hand sets him on hands and knees (10:10), and he stands trembling (10:11). The messenger's words open a window unlike anything else in the book: "Don't be afraid, Daniel; for from the first day that you set your heart to understand, and to humble yourself before your God, your words were heard" — yet "the prince of the kingdom of Persia withstood me twenty-one days", until "Michael, one of the chief princes, came to help me" (10:12–13) — Daniel's prayer was heard at once, while an unseen conflict delayed the answer. Twice more the overwhelmed prophet — "there was no breath left in me" (10:16–17) — is touched and strengthened — "Peace be to you. Be strong. Yes, be strong." (10:19) — and the messenger, who must return to fight the prince of Persia and then face the prince of Greece, prepares to tell him "that which is inscribed in the writing of truth" (10:20–21).
**Tags:** `dreams-and-visions` — Daniel alone sees "this great vision" (10:7–8), an overwhelming appearance that fells him until he is strengthened to hear it; `fear-not` — the word comes twice with a touch: "Don't be afraid, Daniel" (10:12) and "Greatly beloved man, don't be afraid. Peace be to you. Be strong." (10:19); `prayer` — "from the first day that you set your heart to understand, and to humble yourself before your God, your words were heard. I have come for your words' sake" (10:12) — prayer heard immediately even when the answer is three weeks in coming; `spiritual-warfare` — the Old Testament's most explicit window on the unseen conflict behind events: "the prince of the kingdom of Persia withstood me twenty-one days", until "Michael, one of the chief princes, came to help me" (10:13), with the messenger returning to that fight (10:20–21) — described strictly in the text's own words, with no speculative demonology; `angels` — Michael named in the text as "one of the chief princes", the helper who comes when the messenger is withstood (10:13, 21); `fasting` — the vision follows mourning and fasting "three whole weeks", no pleasant food, meat, or wine (10:2–3)

## Daniel 11
**Summary:** The messenger now unrolls "the truth" (11:2) — the Bible's most detailed forecast: after three more Persian kings and a rich fourth, a mighty king arises whose kingdom is broken "toward the four winds of the sky", not to his descendants (11:2–4); then follows a long, grinding chronicle of "the king of the south" and "the king of the north" — marriage alliances that fail, invasions and counter-invasions, a tax collector, and at last "a contemptible person" who seizes the kingdom by flatteries (11:5–21). This king profanes the sanctuary, takes away the continual burnt offering, and sets up "the abomination that makes desolate" (11:31), corrupting covenant-breakers with flattery — "but the people who know their God will be strong and take action" (11:32). "Those who are wise among the people will instruct many", and some of them fall by sword and flame, "to refine them, and to purify, and to make them white, even to the time of the end" (11:33–35) — persecution given a refining purpose. Then "the king will do according to his will. He will exalt himself and magnify himself above every god" (11:36), honoring only a god of fortresses, prospering "until the indignation is accomplished" — and at the time of the end, the king of the north comes against him "like a whirlwind" (11:40), and after that last whirlwind of war he pitches his palace tents between the sea and the glorious holy mountain — "yet he will come to his end, and no one will help him" (11:36–45). Readers have long matched verses 2–35, step by step, with the wars of the Persian era and the Greek kingdoms that followed Alexander, with the sanctuary-profaning king matched to Antiochus Epiphanes; whether verses 36–45 continue that king's story or look beyond him is a question the text leaves open and faithful readers answer differently — this summary keeps to what the vision says.
**Tags:** `providence` — the entire tangle of wars runs on God's clock: "the end will still be at the appointed time" (11:27), "at the appointed time" (11:29), "it is yet for the time appointed" (11:35), and the self-willed king only prospers until "that which is determined will be done" (11:36); `testing` — the fall of some of the wise has a stated purifying purpose: "to refine them, and to purify, and to make them white, even to the time of the end" (11:35); `humble-exaltation` — the king who exalts and magnifies "himself above every god" (11:36–37) ends helpless: "yet he will come to his end, and no one will help him" (11:45); `end-times` — the vision's own vocabulary for the appointed final period: the wise are refined "even to the time of the end" (11:35), and the text places the last campaign at that time in its own dating (11:40)
**Sources:** brooks-1919; mhcc; bsb-headings
**Sections:**
- **11:1–35 — Kings of the South and North** (title: BSB) — The long wars of the kings of the south and north, told in advance: alliances, invasions, and betrayals, climaxing in the contemptible king who profanes the sanctuary and sets up the abomination that makes desolate — while the people who know their God stand firm, and the wise who fall are refined until the appointed time.
- **11:36–45 — The King Who Exalts Himself** (title: BSB) — A king who does as he wills, magnifies himself above every god, and honors only a god of fortresses conquers to the time of the end — and then comes to his end with no one to help him.

## Daniel 12
**Summary:** "At that time Michael will stand up, the great prince who stands for the children of your people", and the worst distress in history breaks — "a time of trouble, such as never was since there was a nation" — but "your people will be delivered, everyone who is found written in the book" (12:1). Then the Old Testament's plainest resurrection promise, the text's own words and its own claim: "Many of those who sleep in the dust of the earth will awake, some to everlasting life, and some to shame and everlasting contempt" (12:2), and "Those who turn many to righteousness will shine as the stars forever and ever" (12:3). Daniel is told to "shut up the words and seal the book, even to the time of the end" (12:4), and overhears one last exchange: the man clothed in linen swears by him who lives forever that it will be "for a time, times, and a half", until the holy people's shattering is complete (12:5–7). When Daniel confesses he heard but did not understand, the reply is not an explanation but a dismissal in peace: the words are sealed, the wise will understand, the numbered days run their course — "Blessed is he who waits" (12:12) — and the book's last word is a personal promise to the old exile himself: "But go your way until the end; for you will rest, and will stand in your inheritance at the end of the days" (12:13).
